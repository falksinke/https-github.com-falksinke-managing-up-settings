**Primary subject:** Leading from the middle, managing up, influencing without authority, strategic thinking, constructive push-back, team leadership, change leadership, resilience, talent development, and leadership presence  
**Extraction purpose:** High-recall extraction for multi-source synthesis  
**Source edition:** AMACOM, 2010

## 1. Book in one model

The book’s central idea is that **“leading your boss” is really a form of leading from the middle**. A middle manager is simultaneously responsible upward, sideways, and downward: upward to the boss and senior leadership, sideways to peers and cross-functional partners, and downward to direct reports. The person who leads up does more than “manage up” administratively. He or she uses initiative, influence, judgment, courage, and selflessness to help the boss, team, and organization succeed.

The book’s master sequence can be reconstructed as:

**See what the leader and organization need → think strategically rather than only tactically → speak truth and push back when necessary → give others room to own the work → influence people across boundaries → work the system and prioritize → persist through setbacks → develop other leaders → create trust and credibility through leadership presence.**

Baldoni organizes this into nine steps:

1. **Leading Up**
2. **Thinking and Acting Strategically**
3. **Pushing Back the Right Way**
4. **Letting Others Create the How**
5. **Breaking Down the Doors**
6. **Working the System**
7. **Bending but Not Breaking**
8. **Preparing Others to Lead**
9. **Leading with Presence**

These steps answer three overarching questions:

### What does the leader need?
- someone who sees the larger organizational picture;
- someone who acts with initiative;
- someone who can think strategically;
- someone who can disagree constructively;
- someone who can help the boss succeed without merely flattering or obeying.

### What does the team need?
- clarity about direction and behavior;
- coordination, cooperation, and collaboration;
- enough authority to solve problems;
- trust;
- influence across boundaries;
- someone who can help the team work the system rather than become trapped by it.

### What can I do to help the leader and the team succeed?
- remain resilient;
- stay calm under pressure;
- develop others;
- share authority;
- create future leaders;
- show confidence, optimism, and presence;
- act from character and values.

The book’s fundamental causal model is:

**Credibility + trust + expertise + initiative → influence → ability to shape decisions upward and sideways → better decisions and stronger execution → greater trust and authority → more ability to lead from the middle.**

The opposite loop is:

**Narrow tactical focus + poor communication + weak trust + fear of push-back → unresolved problems and bad decisions → more managerial intervention and frustration → less autonomy → still weaker initiative.**

The book also repeatedly contrasts **authority** with **influence**. Formal authority lets leaders push; middle managers often need to pull through persuasion. Therefore, leading up depends less on rank and more on:
- accurate information;
- credibility;
- personal relationships;
- a persuasive business case;
- persistence;
- coalition building;
- emotional intelligence;
- visible commitment.

The book is strongly normative. Baldoni argues that people in the middle have a responsibility to act when their initiative can make a positive organizational difference, even when doing so is uncomfortable. At the same time, leading up is not rebellion. The person leading up is expected to support the final decision once the debate is over, except where ethics or legality make compliance unacceptable.

## 2. Core concepts, models, and frameworks

### 2.1 Leading up vs. managing up

**Managing up** is framed as the administrative side of the relationship:
- keeping the boss informed;
- managing work;
- coordinating;
- planning;
- making sure commitments are met.

**Leading up** adds:
- initiative;
- strategic thinking;
- persuasion;
- constructive dissent;
- willingness to fill leadership voids;
- acting for the good of the team or organization rather than personal advantage.

The underlying principle is:
**Do what helps the leader do the job better, but do so with enough leadership judgment that the organization benefits, even if you must put your own preferences aside.**

Leading up requires:
- initiative;
- persuasion;
- influence;
- persistence;
- passion;
- courage;
- accountability.

---

### 2.2 Three questions for leading from the middle

Baldoni gives three orienting questions:

1. **What does the leader need?**
2. **What does the team need?**
3. **What can I do to help the leader and team succeed?**

These questions shift attention away from:
- “What do I want?”
- “Why isn’t my boss doing this?”
- “Why don’t other people understand?”

and toward:
- “What is required for the organization to move?”
- “What role can I play?”
- “Where should I step up and where should I step aside?”

This is the book’s main selflessness mechanism: **personal influence grows when your actions visibly serve the larger mission rather than your ego.**

---

### 2.3 Leading from the middle

Leading from the middle means:
- thinking about the whole system;
- translating strategy into action;
- acting across levels;
- influencing without full authority;
- managing tactical details while retaining strategic perspective;
- helping others lead.

Middle managers occupy a difficult position because they are:
- pushed by senior leadership;
- pulled by employee needs;
- accountable for results;
- often dependent on peers over whom they have no authority.

The book treats this pressure as a training ground for senior leadership.

---

### 2.4 Reflective assertiveness

Baldoni distinguishes ordinary assertiveness from **reflective assertiveness**.

#### Ordinary/overt assertiveness
- decisive;
- direct;
- confident;
- visible;
- useful in crisis.

#### Reflective assertiveness
A quieter form of power based on:
- listening;
- experience;
- resilience;
- calm;
- acting only after reflection.

Its logic:

**Listen first → absorb context → remain calm → decide → act firmly.**

Reflective assertiveness signals:
- “Look at us,” not “look at me.”
- confidence without ego display;
- strength without needless domination.

The manager should be able to **dial assertiveness up or down** depending on circumstances.

---

### 2.5 Energy as leadership fuel

Energy is treated as internal drive that enables leadership action.

Baldoni asks managers to understand:

#### Where their energy comes from
Examples:
- working with people;
- new ideas;
- problem solving;
- crisis management;
- big goals;
- guiding teams.

#### What they can do with it
Energy:
- powers persistence;
- creates enthusiasm;
- draws attention;
- can energize a team.

#### Its limits
Continuous work without recovery:
- narrows thinking;
- lowers productivity;
- increases task time;
- risks burnout.

A central rule is:
**Know how to replenish your energy, not merely how to spend it.**

---

### 2.6 Strategic thinking vs. tactical doing

Middle managers are often promoted because they are strong tacticians. They know how to:
- execute;
- solve immediate problems;
- deliver deadlines;
- supervise technical work.

Strategic thinking requires stepping back.

#### Tactical orientation
- what must be done now;
- execution;
- deadlines;
- details.

#### Strategic orientation
- why it matters;
- what larger system is changing;
- what the organization may need next;
- where opportunities and threats lie;
- how today’s actions affect future outcomes.

The key transition is:
**Do less immediate doing long enough to create perspective.**

Strategic thinking becomes leadership only when linked to:
- metrics;
- execution;
- follow-up.

---

### 2.7 Creativity → imagination → innovation

The book uses a sequence:

**Imagination → creativity → innovation → execution → results.**

#### Imagination
Seeing possibilities not yet realized.

#### Creativity
Generating ideas and alternate approaches.

#### Innovation
Applying creative thought to products, services, processes, or systems.

#### Execution
Turning the new idea into a working result.

Baldoni repeatedly warns that creativity without application is not enough.

---

### 2.8 Discovery

Discovery is both:
- external learning;
- internal self-learning.

Managers can promote discovery by:
- inviting outside speakers;
- working with communities;
- holding “discovery days”;
- creating cross-functional learning;
- challenging employees to share lessons;
- encouraging experiments.

Discovery creates:
- new knowledge;
- self-awareness;
- creative energy.

---

### 2.9 Information vs. knowledge

A major distinction:

- **Information = data and messages.**
- **Knowledge = information interpreted and applied to useful action.**

The modern problem is not lack of information but overload.

The manager’s role is to convert:
**data → meaning → application.**

Suggested mechanisms:
- internal alert systems;
- external intelligence gathering;
- staff discussion;
- knowledge ambassadors;
- knowledge fairs;
- simplifying information systems;
- turning observations into product or process improvements.

---

### 2.10 Counterintuitive leadership

Counterintuition means deliberately doing what is unexpected because it better fits the person or situation.

Examples:
- praise when anger is expected;
- calm when aggression is expected;
- give autonomy where people expect control.

Critical distinction:

**Counterintuition ≠ inconsistency.**

Inconsistency is:
- mood swings;
- arbitrary reversals;
- poor judgment.

Counterintuitive leadership is:
- deliberate;
- grounded in knowledge of people;
- aligned with values;
- designed to serve the whole.

---

### 2.11 Ambiguity

Ambiguity is treated as an unavoidable leadership condition.

Managers often must act with:
- incomplete data;
- changing markets;
- uncertain stakeholder reactions;
- unclear consequences.

Baldoni’s ambiguity framework includes:

1. **Read the situation.**
2. **Read the people.**
3. **Know the opposition.**
4. **Act decisively.**
5. **Revisit the situation.**
6. **Tune into the silence.**
7. **Act as best you can.**
8. **Embrace ambiguity.**

Critical boundary:
**Ambiguity is acceptable in uncertain business conditions, not in ethics.**

---

### 2.12 The 3 Cs of team behavior

Managers should set behavioral expectations as explicitly as performance expectations.

The 3 Cs are:

1. **Coordinate**
2. **Cooperate**
3. **Collaborate**

#### Coordinate
Share information, responsibilities, schedules, and handoffs.

#### Cooperate
Work toward a joint purpose.

#### Collaborate
Pool talents and resources to produce the result.

This replaces vague judgments like “bad attitude” with observable behavioral language:
- “You are not coordinating.”
- “You are withholding information.”
- “You are not collaborating.”

---

### 2.13 The 5 Cs generated by asking

Baldoni argues that asking rather than constantly telling produces five consequences:

1. **Courtesy**
2. **Comity**
3. **Cooperation**
4. **Commitment**
5. **Community**

The causal chain is:

**Asking → respect → participation → cooperation → commitment → community.**

---

### 2.14 “No solutions thinking”

One of the book’s strongest management ideas is to **hold back on giving answers**.

Instead of instantly solving a problem:
- ask;
- probe;
- map the process;
- let people doing the work generate the solution;
- give them decision authority where appropriate.

This creates:
- ownership;
- problem-solving capability;
- accountability;
- leadership development.

The manager shifts from:
**answer-provider → thinking catalyst.**

---

### 2.15 Push and pull influence

Influence is divided into:

#### Push
- power;
- formal authority;
- direction;
- force;
- mandate.

#### Pull
- persuasion;
- credibility;
- voluntary commitment.

Middle managers often lack sufficient authority to push and must rely on pull.

---

### 2.16 Seven influence styles

Baldoni presents seven means of influence:

1. **Information**
   - facts, data, evidence, business case.

2. **Charisma**
   - leadership presence, confidence, personal appeal.

3. **Participation**
   - involving others in the cause.

4. **Compromise**
   - both sides give something and gain something.

5. **Reason**
   - logic and intellectual argument.

6. **Emotional appeal**
   - connect to values, identity, and “what’s in it for me.”

7. **Coercion**
   - authority, enforcement, “do it or else.”

The book does not argue that one is universally best. Effective influence often combines several.

---

### 2.17 The business-case influence model

Baldoni’s practical influence process:

1. **Scan the horizon.**
2. **Identify pluses and minuses.**
3. **Argue the value proposition.**
4. **Make the business case.**
5. **Overcome obstacles.**
6. **Go for the heart.**
7. **Create win-wins.**

This combines logic, politics, emotion, and coalition building.

---

### 2.18 Leadership presence

Leadership presence is one of the book’s most distinctive terms.

**Definition:** “Earned authority.”

Baldoni also describes it as:
**the radiance of commitment.**

Presence emerges when:
- competence is visible;
- values are consistent;
- people know you care;
- you stay calm under pressure;
- you recognize others;
- you act with optimism and conviction.

Presence is not performance theater. The outward leadership “act” is legitimate when it expresses real internal conviction.

## 3. Step 1 — Leading Up

### 3.1 Identify the opportunity and act

Leading up begins with noticing a leadership void or organizational need and acting.

Example pattern:
**Need is visible → boss has not acted → middle manager develops plan → seeks approval → moves work forward.**

Key rule:
**Do not wait passively when constructive initiative can help the organization.**

---

### 3.2 Mission clarity

From Eleanor Roosevelt, Baldoni draws three lessons:

- know your mission;
- put energy into it;
- know your subject.

Mission clarity keeps upward leadership from becoming ego-driven.

---

### 3.3 Be around, be seen, be curious

Middle managers must avoid becoming desk-bound.

#### Be around
Spend time where work is happening.

#### Be seen
Walk the halls, visit employees, eat with people, join informal settings.

#### Be curious
Ask:
- What are people working on?
- What are customers saying?
- What is getting in the way?
- What ideas are emerging?

Mechanism:
**visibility + curiosity → better information → stronger judgment → more credible upward influence.**

---

### 3.4 Persuade upward

Baldoni’s persuasion-up sequence:

#### Know what is happening
- use accurate data;
- analyze business impact;
- include competitive context.

#### Present the big idea
- frame aspiration;
- show what could be different.

#### Sell the big idea
- believe in it;
- translate it into compelling value.

#### Get involved in planning
- ask questions;
- visit customers;
- supply real-world input.

#### Channel passion
Use your own motivation constructively without meddling in everyone else’s work.

#### Leverage customers
Customer demand is powerful evidence.

#### Keep pushing
The first “no” is information, not always a final verdict.

---

### 3.5 Assert yourself diplomatically

Reflective assertiveness practices:

- listen first;
- absorb;
- speak calmly;
- avoid emotional escalation;
- act decisively once ready;
- keep people informed if you need more decision time.

The person who acts late but explains the decision process may still be seen as deliberate. The person who simply goes silent is seen as dithering.

---

### 3.6 Lead-up requirements

Baldoni’s summary:

- establish trust by following through;
- connect authentically;
- make yourself available;
- share credit;
- demonstrate initiative;
- act and think for the boss when useful;
- use common sense;
- remain accountable.

---

## 4. Step 2 — Thinking and Acting Strategically

### 4.1 Step back from the task

The central skill is creating distance from immediate operations.

Questions:
- What are we really trying to achieve?
- What assumptions are we making?
- What might change?
- What matters to customers?
- What should we stop?
- What would make the process faster, better, cheaper?

---

### 4.2 “Rip up the box”

Baldoni criticizes vague commands to “think outside the box.”

Instead:

#### Create realistic expectations
Define what creative output should improve.

Examples:
- faster;
- cheaper;
- safer;
- simpler;
- better customer experience.

#### Reframe the issue
Look from another stakeholder’s perspective.

#### Stretch your head
Give ideas time before rejecting them.

Principle:
**Creative thinking needs constraints clear enough to direct effort but loose enough to allow alternatives.**

---

### 4.3 Unlock creativity through discovery

Methods:

- promote learning;
- invite outsiders;
- cross disciplines;
- teach others;
- hold discovery days;
- ask what failed and what was learned;
- allow idea-sharing;
- tolerate failed experiments.

One of the most important innovation rules:
**Do not punish people for attempting ambitious things and failing in good faith.**

---

### 4.4 Apply creativity

The book rejects creativity as indiscriminate novelty.

Examples of domains where strict reliability matters:
- surgery;
- nuclear engineering;
- financial reporting.

Therefore:
**creative freedom must be bounded by standards, risk, and purpose.**

---

### 4.5 Encourage imagination

- invite possibility;
- signal from leadership that new ideas matter;
- institutionalize idea generation;
- allow time to think;
- involve peers;
- prevent middle-management inertia from killing ideas;
- improve handoffs across departments.

---

### 4.6 Innovate at the edge

Practices:
- challenge convention;
- track competitors;
- listen to customers;
- encourage new thinking;
- find a home for promising ideas;
- accept incremental improvements;
- celebrate idea generators.

Important distinction:
A “home run” innovation is rare. **Singles matter.**

---

### 4.7 Hope + innovation

Innovation requires:
- vision;
- mission;
- strategies;
- execution;
- enough hope to sustain effort during uncertainty.

Sequence:

**Think the vision → market the mission → act on strategies.**

---

### 4.8 Strategic idea gathering

Baldoni recommends learning from outside the industry.

Example principle:
**Do not benchmark only peers. Study analogous excellence elsewhere.**

Purpose:
- expose assumptions;
- create fresh analogies;
- heighten observation;
- import useful process ideas.

Boundary:
Cross-industry analogy should improve your own domain; it does not replace domain expertise.

---

### 4.9 Convert information into knowledge

#### Internal alert system
Assign people to scan internal information.

#### External intelligence gatherers
Assign team members to monitor:
- competitors;
- customer behavior;
- service feedback;
- market media.

#### Chief information officer role
Gather, compare, and discuss the intelligence.

#### Turn data into application
Ask:
- What does this mean for our product?
- What should we change?
- What should we test?

#### Keep seeking
Rotate intelligence roles to generate new perspectives.

---

### 4.10 Think counterintuitively

To use counterintuition well:

- know the people;
- plan ahead;
- choose the moment;
- avoid using surprise as manipulation;
- hold to values.

Counterintuitive action should still be coherent with your leadership character.

---

### 4.11 Leverage peer-to-peer networks

Peer groups are important sources of:
- credibility;
- tacit knowledge;
- organizational reality.

Practices:
- foster knowledge networks;
- preserve cross-functional relationships after projects;
- appoint knowledge ambassadors;
- hold knowledge fairs;
- simplify digital knowledge systems.

Warning:
Peer networks can also spread:
- gossip;
- rumor;
- slander.

Therefore:
- trust known credible sources;
- triangulate;
- compare internal talk to external evidence.

---

### 4.12 Strategic-thinking requirements

Baldoni’s summary:
- think critically;
- challenge status quo;
- reframe opportunities;
- hit singles;
- challenge convention;
- get out of your cubicle;
- turn information into knowledge;
- deal with ambiguity;
- act on incomplete knowledge;
- find comfort in uncertainty.

---

## 5. Step 3 — Pushing Back the Right Way

### 5.1 Criticize the boss with a velvet glove

Baldoni argues honest criticism is necessary, but upward criticism must be tactful.

Rules:
- be factually right;
- be diplomatic;
- focus on behavior;
- explain performance impact;
- use examples;
- avoid attacks on personality;
- frame the goal as helping the boss and team perform.

Instead of:
“You’re mean.”

Use:
“When the tone becomes harsh, people stop contributing, which makes it harder to get the information you need.”

---

### 5.2 Receive criticism well yourself

A person who wants to give upward criticism must also model receiving criticism.

Practices:
- breathe;
- thank the critic;
- avoid immediate retaliation;
- reflect;
- act later.

Mechanism:
**good response to criticism → more future candor → more complete information → better leadership.**

---

### 5.3 Learn from criticism

Criticism should be:
- fact-based;
- improvement-oriented;
- timely.

There are moments to delay criticism:
- acute crisis;
- immediate failure response;
- highly emotional moment.

But difficult times still require truth after the situation stabilizes.

---

### 5.4 How to disagree with your boss

Procedure:

1. Let the boss explain first.
2. Do not interrupt unnecessarily.
3. Ask questions.
4. Make your own case clearly.
5. Stay organized and concise.
6. Invite dialogue.
7. Listen to objections.
8. Hold your conviction.
9. Let the boss have the final word.
10. Support the final decision publicly.

This is **organizational savvy**, not weakness.

---

### 5.5 Agree to disagree

Rule:
**Disagree privately, support publicly.**

Why:
- preserves the boss’s authority;
- preserves the relationship;
- makes future dissent safer.

Exception:
**Do not comply with illegal or unethical instructions.**

---

### 5.6 Demand relevance

Communication fails when timing and relevance are poor.

Ask:

#### What is going on?
What does the audience’s current reality look like?

#### What do people need to know now?
Filter information.

#### What can I do to make the message real?
- coach;
- demonstrate;
- participate;
- connect the message to actual work.

Relevance converts abstract communication into usable action.

---

### 5.7 Accept criticism publicly when appropriate

Organizations should:
- own problems;
- face customers;
- discuss failures;
- correct them.

The book distinguishes:
**PR acknowledgment** from **real correction**.

You need both:
- say what happened;
- fix what happened.

---

### 5.8 Avoid zero-sum games

Zero-sum behavior:
- one side must win;
- one side must lose;
- power is hoarded.

Better management:
- share power;
- involve others;
- seek win-win;
- maintain autonomy and dignity.

Practices:

#### Make a habit of yes
More openness to ideas creates goodwill and makes later “no” easier to accept.

#### Involve others
Participation improves decision quality and ownership.

#### Defer to the judgment of others
Let people closest to the work decide where possible.

#### Be magnanimous
After winning, bring opponents back into the process.

#### Decide to decide
Do not negotiate forever.

---

### 5.9 Act on what you do not know

Ambiguity-management procedure:

- read situation;
- read people;
- know opposition;
- act;
- revisit;
- watch silence;
- adjust.

Key heuristic:
**Not deciding is still a decision.**

---

### 5.10 Tune into silence

Absence of information is itself information.

Signals:
- employee says “fine” but avoids eye contact;
- normally lively workplace becomes quiet;
- no dissent appears where dissent should exist.

Ask:
**What am I not hearing?**

---

### 5.11 Push-back requirements

- develop self-assurance;
- read the situation;
- know when to disagree;
- know when to go with the flow;
- give timely feedback;
- tolerate uncertainty.

---

## 6. Step 4 — Letting Others Create the How

### 6.1 Give authority away

Using Stockdale’s leadership example, Baldoni argues:
**great leaders gain authority by giving it away.**

The manager should:
- set direction;
- make expectations clear;
- then allow people to determine execution.

---

### 6.2 Set both performance and behavioral expectations

Performance:
- what gets done.

Behavior:
- how people work together.

This is where the 3 Cs matter:
- coordination;
- cooperation;
- collaboration.

---

### 6.3 Use the 3 Cs for conflict resolution

If coworkers are in conflict:

1. Let them state the issue.
2. Require coordination.
3. Make them cooperate on a solution.
4. Require collaboration in execution.

This reframes interpersonal friction as a work requirement.

---

### 6.4 Find the right fit

Managers should:
- assess what talent they have;
- adapt their management style;
- offer development;
- recruit complementary strengths;
- place people in roles suited to them.

Bad fit examples:
- socially avoidant person in intensive sales;
- highly independent specialist in a role requiring constant group coordination.

Core principle:
**Right people + right roles → right results.**

---

### 6.5 Ask employees about their expectations

Do not only tell employees what you expect.

Ask:
- What do you want from this job?
- What do you want to learn?
- What kind of work energizes you?
- What responsibility do you want?

Benefits:
- learn motivation;
- detect poor fit early;
- create participation;
- increase commitment.

---

### 6.6 Ask for ideas, support, and feedback

The manager should ask:
- for ideas;
- for support;
- how he/she can help;
- for feedback on management style.

This shifts leadership from monologue to exchange.

---

### 6.7 Hold back solutions

Practical techniques:

#### Encourage thinking
Require people to bring ideas, not only problems.

#### Ask questions
Examples:
- What is happening?
- Why?
- What should we do?
- What would improve this?
- What is the next step?

#### Process map
Map:
**input → process → output.**

#### Hand over the go/no-go switch
Let teams make decisions within clear boundaries.

---

### 6.8 Know when senior authority is required

Not every decision should be delegated.

Senior authority is appropriate for:
- competing resource claims;
- enterprise-level risk;
- strategic trade-offs;
- decisions affecting multiple groups.

Principle:
**Push decisions down when local knowledge dominates; pull decisions up when cross-system trade-offs dominate.**

---

### 6.9 Emotional intelligence

Baldoni frames EQ as the ability to engage hearts and minds.

Practices:
- connect as a person;
- check understanding;
- share knowledge/resources;
- give of yourself.

But EQ is not softness. Leaders must still make:
- layoffs;
- transfers;
- difficult performance calls;
- crisis decisions.

---

### 6.10 Compromise

Compromise is:
**agreement in which both sides give and gain.**

It is not:
- surrender of ethics;
- surrender of core values.

Methods:
- insist on collaboration;
- leverage dissent;
- seek comity;
- model willingness to give.

Compromise becomes leadership when a manager sacrifices a pet idea for a better shared outcome.

---

### 6.11 Rebuild trust

When trust has been lost:

#### Be open
- admit the mistake;
- apologize;
- avoid excuses.

#### Turn it around
- correct the specific harm;
- restore missing recognition/resources.

#### Prove it
- act consistently over time;
- share credit;
- share hard work;
- defend the team.

Trust is rebuilt through behavior, not speeches.

---

### 6.12 Team composition

Good teams are not necessarily collections of stars.

Managers should:
- select complementary skills;
- use role players;
- deliberately combine opposites;
- promote harmony;
- encourage dissent about ideas.

Dissent is productive when:
- directed at ideas;
- grounded in shared goals.

It is destructive when:
- directed at personality;
- used to win status.

---

### 6.13 Step 4 requirements

- set behavioral expectations;
- put right people in right roles;
- practice compromise;
- communicate teamwork;
- insist on ownership;
- insist on accountability.

---

## 7. Step 5 — Breaking Down the Doors

### 7.1 Influence without authority

The core challenge:
**How do you create movement when people can refuse you?**

The answer:
- expertise;
- credibility;
- relationships;
- coalition;
- business case;
- emotional connection;
- persistence.

---

### 7.2 Seven influence styles in practice

Use **information** when:
- facts are disputed;
- analytical audiences matter.

Use **charisma** when:
- personal confidence can rally people.

Use **participation** when:
- ownership is needed.

Use **compromise** when:
- interests diverge but agreement is possible.

Use **reason** when:
- the issue is primarily logical or economic.

Use **emotional appeal** when:
- identity, aspiration, fear, or personal stakes matter.

Use **coercion** when:
- law, ethics, safety, or survival demands compliance.

---

### 7.3 Sell upward

Procedure:

#### Know the real situation
- validate facts;
- understand financial impact;
- understand performance impact;
- include competitor/customer context.

#### Develop the story
- business case;
- narrative;
- visual;
- 30-second elevator explanation.

#### Build coalition
- recruit peers;
- exchange support;
- create allies before presenting upward.

#### Leverage customers
External and internal customers make compelling sponsors.

#### Keep pushing
Revise after rejection.

---

### 7.4 Relate to people as people

Influence begins with connection.

Practices:
- emphasize human impact;
- see the alternate point of view;
- sell rather than dictate;
- teach networking;
- show enthusiasm.

---

### 7.5 Present ideas effectively

#### Tell the benefits story
Show how life/work improves.

#### Add “sex appeal”
Make the idea compelling:
- credibility;
- novelty;
- testimonials;
- aspiration.

#### Create urgency
Explain why action matters now.

But urgency should be genuine, not exaggerated.

---

### 7.6 Respect people and ideas

Rules:
- disagree with ideas, not personalities;
- listen to objections;
- respect rejection;
- maintain trust.

Credibility comes from:
**doing what you said you would do.**

---

### 7.7 Influence across boundaries

To influence other functions:

#### Study the situation
Understand:
- previous attempts;
- supporters;
- resisters;
- incentives;
- local culture.

#### Be available
Stay involved after the pitch.

#### Tell stories
Use real adoption examples.

#### Walk humbly
Avoid know-it-all posture.

---

### 7.8 The seven-step influence case

1. **Scan horizon**
2. **Map pluses/minuses**
3. **Define value proposition**
4. **Make business case**
5. **Overcome obstacles**
6. **Go for heart**
7. **Create win-win**

This is iterative, not linear.

---

### 7.9 Limits of influence

Influence can fail because:
- senior manager says no;
- culture resists;
- initiative lacks sponsorship;
- people have other priorities.

But resistance itself can strengthen adoption because ideas debated and defended are more likely to become owned rather than passively complied with.

---

### 7.10 Advocacy

Advocacy is outward leadership in defense of an issue or organizational interest.

Steps:

#### Inform
Explain issue and impact.

#### Teach
Help others explain the issue in their own words.

#### Prepare
Train for objections.

Boundary:
Do not coerce employees to advocate publicly. Genuine advocacy must be voluntary.

---

### 7.11 Step 5 requirements

- learn influence;
- look outward while leading upward;
- keep people’s needs visible;
- overcome obstacles;
- convert influence into leadership.

## 8. Step 6 — Working the System

### 8.1 Challenge assumptions without attacking people

Middle managers often see where existing assumptions fail.

Best practice:
**challenge the idea, not the authority of the person.**

---

### 8.2 Establish priorities

Priorities should survive changing conditions.

Two communication practices:

#### Tell people what they need to know
Knowledge-sharing builds trust.

#### Radiate calm
Confidence makes shifting priorities easier to accept.

---

### 8.3 The change factor

Crises disrupt annual plans.

Therefore:
- plan for disruption;
- reprioritize;
- communicate what changed;
- distinguish real “red balls” from permanent false urgency.

Chronic emergency mode causes burnout.

---

### 8.4 Maintain your own skills

Managers should stop doing everything, but not become helpless administrators.

Risks of too little direct involvement:
- loss of technical awareness;
- dependence on consultants;
- poor decision quality.

Risks of too much:
- micromanagement;
- weakened employee initiative.

---

### 8.5 Make yourself available

Manager contact can:
- reassure;
- align;
- create learning.

Too much contact:
- creates dependence.

Rule:
**Be available without hovering.**

---

### 8.6 Make the big decisions

Decision-making should be distributed, but enterprise-level decisions require senior responsibility.

Senior decision preparation requires:
- knowledge of people;
- key details;
- strategy;
- outcomes.

---

### 8.7 Dive into a project

Occasional direct involvement can:
- sharpen judgment;
- reconnect with operations;
- restore energy.

But it must not displace core managerial responsibilities.

---

### 8.8 Think like a guide

“Thinking like a guide” means:
- see ahead;
- see the whole;
- monitor team and individual condition;
- balance achievement against overload;
- watch for risk.

---

### 8.9 Leading change

To make change adoptable:

#### Make it simple
Avoid jargon.

#### Make people comfortable
Invite questions.

#### Be specific
Translate strategy into:
- tasks;
- roles;
- responsibilities.

#### Give buy-in
Invite resistance and questions.

#### Use self-deprecating humor
Reduce status distance.

#### Add drama
Connect people’s contribution to a larger meaningful story.

---

### 8.10 Know your limits

Scope the job:
- define what is yours;
- define what is not;
- reprioritize when new work arrives.

Hold yourself accountable:
- tell the boss what you can and cannot do;
- negotiate;
- gain agreement.

Burnout prevention depends heavily on delegation.

---

### 8.11 Delegate responsibility and authority

If you grant responsibility without authority:
- employees remain dependent.

If you give both:
- ownership;
- growth;
- less managerial overload.

Manager’s job:
- state expectations;
- step away;
- remain available.

---

### 8.12 Learn from setbacks

When change fails:
- do your job;
- communicate your case;
- protect credibility;
- learn;
- move forward.

What senior leaders may remember is not only whether the initiative succeeded, but:
- whether you knew your stuff;
- whether you were credible;
- whether you understood the business;
- how you conducted yourself.

---

### 8.13 Step 6 requirements

- prioritize short and long term;
- anticipate, adapt, embrace change;
- lead visibly;
- let others speak first;
- develop patience;
- resist finishing others’ work.

## 9. Step 7 — Bending but Not Breaking

### 9.1 Resilience

Definition:
**the ability to withstand setback and recover enough to remain in the game.**

Resilience is both:
- internal strength;
- visible leadership signal.

When others see a leader recover, they gain confidence.

---

### 9.2 Know the odds

Use SWOT:
- strengths;
- weaknesses;
- opportunities;
- threats.

Do not confuse resilience with reckless persistence.

Question:
**Are the odds difficult or essentially impossible?**

---

### 9.3 Acknowledge failure

Failure should be:
- named;
- analyzed;
- converted into learning.

Avoid:
- shame;
- denial;
- premature abandonment.

---

### 9.4 Turn setbacks into comebacks

- listen to useful criticism;
- ignore baseless noise;
- draw on strengths;
- ask others for ideas;
- combine suggestions;
- test again.

---

### 9.5 Share stories of adversity

Stories about:
- failed attempts;
- recovery;
- eventual success;

can normalize setbacks and build team confidence.

---

### 9.6 Keep your cool

During crisis:
- remain calm;
- distance briefly from panic;
- focus on next action;
- steady your voice;
- listen frequently;
- stay visible.

A leader may feel fear internally but should not transmit panic to the group.

---

### 9.7 Manage the “no”

Baldoni treats “no” as something to diagnose.

#### First no = invitation
Ask:
- why not?
- what concern is behind it?
- what must change?

#### Frame around organizational need
Avoid personal entitlement.

#### Go around the no cleverly
Find ways to keep progress moving without needless confrontation.

#### Sometimes accept the no
Persistence must have limits.

---

### 9.8 Know when to stop pushing

Resilience requires:
- patience;
- timing;
- forbearance.

Do not burn every bridge over one issue.

---

### 9.9 Fairness vs. equality

Baldoni distinguishes:

- **Fairness/equality of opportunity and treatment**
- **Differentiated rewards based on performance**

Managers should:
- give fair opportunity;
- evaluate work by job standards;
- reward high performance;
- update objectives when responsibilities change;
- avoid favoritism.

A strong performer should not be held back merely to protect weaker performers.

---

### 9.10 After-action review

Questions:
- What happened?
- What went right?
- What went wrong?
- What could I have done better?
- What did I learn?
- What will I change next time?

---

### 9.11 Step 7 requirements

- know the odds;
- acknowledge failure;
- conduct after-action review;
- turn setbacks into comebacks;
- visibly hold to principles.

## 12. The Smart Guide to Positive Push-Back

The closing guide compresses the book into three themes:

### 12.1 Stick with facts

Rules:
- keep digging;
- find evidence that contradicts your own preferred conclusion;
- ask a devil’s advocate to challenge the premise;
- distinguish correlation from causation;
- collect enough evidence to decide, but avoid endless analysis.

#### Failure mode: analysis paralysis
Asking for more and more data can become a method of avoiding decision.

---

### 12.2 Deal with the “jerk boss”

If reason fails because the boss is insecure, controlling, and irrational, Baldoni gives three options:

#### 1. Roll over / go with the flow
Use when:
- you need the job;
- direct confrontation is dangerous.

Stay civil and prepare for downside risk.

#### 2. Fight back strategically
- document behaviors;
- document impact on productivity, absenteeism, turnover, team relationships;
- work with trusted HR;
- escalate carefully.

Warning:
Boss may have powerful allies.

#### 3. Leave
- seek transfer;
- plan exit;
- move to another organization.

---

### 12.3 Bounce back

Resilience questions:

1. **What happened?**
2. **What could I have done better?**
3. **What did I learn?**

If the third answer is “nothing,” learning has not occurred.

## 13. Principles and mechanisms

### 13.1 Initiative creates upward credibility
When you solve problems the boss should care about, your influence grows.

### 13.2 Selflessness increases influence
People trust upward leaders more when they visibly act for the team and organization.

### 13.3 Trust is behavioral
Trust comes from:
- follow-through;
- honesty;
- availability;
- sharing credit;
- accountability.

### 13.4 Leadership should be distributed
Giving authority downwards:
- builds ownership;
- creates future leaders;
- frees managers to think strategically.

### 13.5 Strategic thinking requires distance
If every minute is spent doing, there is no time to interpret.

### 13.6 Strategic thinking still requires execution
Big-picture thinking without metrics is fantasy.

### 13.7 Creativity requires psychological permission
People will not propose ideas if failure is punished.

### 13.8 Innovation benefits from cross-domain analogy
Novel solutions often come from studying excellence outside your own field.

### 13.9 Data are not knowledge
Knowledge appears when data are interpreted and applied.

### 13.10 Facts should precede influence
A factually weak case undermines both the idea and the advocate.

### 13.11 The first “no” is often diagnostic
Find out what the no means:
- concern;
- politics;
- missing information;
- timing;
- real rejection.

### 13.12 Dissent can strengthen decisions
Constructive disagreement improves analysis and increases ownership.

### 13.13 Public support preserves private dissent
Backing the boss after the decision protects authority and relationship.

### 13.14 Ethics are a hard boundary
Do not compromise law or core values for organizational harmony.

### 13.15 Asking builds ownership
People support what they help shape.

### 13.16 Holding back answers develops capability
When managers solve everything, employees remain dependent.

### 13.17 Influence without authority is the middle manager’s core competence
The flatter the organization, the more this matters.

### 13.18 Emotion matters
Reason alone may not move people when identity, loss, or fear is involved.

### 13.19 Win-win outperforms domination
People cooperate more fully when autonomy and dignity remain.

### 13.20 Calm communicates control
In crisis, emotional steadiness is part of leadership authority.

### 13.21 Trust lost through credit-stealing can be rebuilt through action
Apology is necessary but insufficient; behavior must change.

### 13.22 Resilience is not stubbornness
Persistence without judgment destroys relationships.

### 13.23 Fairness does not mean identical rewards
Equal opportunity can coexist with differentiated performance recognition.

### 13.24 Leaders create other leaders
Your readiness for senior responsibility is partly judged by whether the team can function without you.

### 13.25 Presence is character made visible
Leadership presence is the external manifestation of internal conviction, competence, and concern.

## 15. Diagnostic rules, heuristics, and decision criteria

- **If your boss is not acting on an obvious need,** prepare a solution rather than merely complain.
- **If you are overwhelmed by tactics,** create time for strategic reflection.
- **If the boss asks for creativity,** clarify the actual target.
- **If people stop proposing ideas,** inspect whether failed ideas are punished.
- **If information is overwhelming,** assign filtering roles and convert data into action.
- **If a proposed idea feels impossible,** look for smaller “singles.”
- **If the first no arrives,** treat it as information before treating it as closure.
- **If your boss is emotional,** delay push-back until the emotional temperature falls.
- **If your boss is wrong,** focus on facts and impact rather than personality.
- **If the boss makes the final decision after debate,** support it publicly unless ethics or law are violated.
- **If people are not collaborating,** define the required behavior explicitly.
- **If you are solving every employee problem,** stop supplying answers and start asking.
- **If a decision belongs close to the work,** push authority downward.
- **If the decision affects competing enterprise priorities,** elevate it.
- **If people resist change,** simplify, specify, involve, and explain WIFM.
- **If reason does not persuade,** assess emotional interests.
- **If influence across functions fails,** inspect whether you understand their local reality.
- **If the organization is in constant crisis,** the problem may be poor prioritization rather than workload alone.
- **If you are losing technical awareness,** re-enter selected projects without taking over.
- **If you are working harder because others cannot act independently,** inspect delegation and authority.
- **If a setback occurs,** separate learning from shame.
- **If persistence starts damaging every relationship,** stop and wait for another opportunity.
- **If employees receive changed assignments,** update performance objectives.
- **If you have a strong performer,** create advancement opportunities.
- **If someone is underperforming despite support,** inspect role fit.
- **If team performance collapses without you,** you have not developed enough leadership capacity.
- **If enthusiasm looks strange during crisis,** replace cheerleading with problem-solving confidence.
- **If optimism ignores facts,** it becomes naiveté.
- **If a boss is irrational and abusive despite good data,** choose among adaptation, strategic escalation, or exit.

## 17. Failure modes, warnings, and anti-patterns

### 17.1 Leading up for ego
If your initiative is primarily about visibility or status, peers and bosses may resist.

### 17.2 Confusing busyness with leadership
Tactical overload can prevent strategic thought.

### 17.3 “Think outside the box” without constraints
Vagueness produces wasted creative effort.

### 17.4 Punishing failed innovation
Destroys future experimentation.

### 17.5 Treating raw information as knowledge
More data can worsen confusion.

## 18. Important distinctions and taxonomies

### Managing up vs. leading up
- **Managing up:** administration and coordination.
- **Leading up:** initiative, influence, strategic action.

### Tactical vs. strategic
- **Tactical:** execute now.
- **Strategic:** understand system and future.

### Assertiveness vs. reflective assertiveness
- **Assertiveness:** visible decisiveness.
- **Reflective assertiveness:** quiet confidence grounded in listening and judgment.

### Information vs. knowledge
- **Information:** data.
- **Knowledge:** usable meaning.

### Creativity vs. innovation
- **Creativity:** ideas.
- **Innovation:** applied ideas.

### Counterintuition vs. inconsistency
- **Counterintuition:** deliberate unexpected action.
- **Inconsistency:** arbitrary variability.

### Push vs. pull
- **Push:** authority.
- **Pull:** persuasion.

### Compliance vs. commitment
- **Compliance:** people do it because required.
- **Commitment:** people own it.

### Performance expectations vs. behavioral expectations
- **Performance:** what.
- **Behavior:** how.

### Coordination vs. cooperation vs. collaboration
- **Coordination:** align information and tasks.
- **Cooperation:** work jointly.
- **Collaboration:** pool resources/talents to create.

### Talent vs. skill
- **Talent:** predisposition.
- **Skill:** learned capability.

### Equality vs. fairness
- **Equality:** equal treatment/opportunity.
- **Fairness:** differentiated consequences based on relevant performance.

### Optimism vs. naiveté
- **Optimism:** hope plus action.
- **Naiveté:** hope without reality.

### Resilience vs. stubbornness
- **Resilience:** adapt and recover.
- **Stubbornness:** persist without judgment.

### Authority vs. presence
- **Authority:** rank.
- **Presence:** earned authority.

### Confidence vs. arrogance
- **Confidence:** belief backed by capability.
- **Arrogance:** superiority detached from listening.

## 21. Integrated conceptual map

The book can be represented as a leadership-from-the-middle system with seven interacting layers.

### Layer 1 — Self
Inputs:
- energy;
- resilience;
- confidence;
- values;
- optimism;
- emotional regulation.

If weak:
- you cannot persist;
- cannot push back;
- cannot project presence.

### Layer 2 — Situational awareness
Observe:
- boss;
- peers;
- customers;
- competitors;
- team;
- silence;
- changing context.

If weak:
- influence arguments are poorly timed or irrelevant.

### Layer 3 — Strategic interpretation
Convert:
**information → knowledge → strategic choices.**

Capabilities:
- reframe;
- challenge assumptions;
- benchmark;
- manage ambiguity.

### Layer 4 — Influence
Use:
- facts;
- reason;
- story;
- emotion;
- participation;
- compromise;
- coalition;
- persistence.

### Layer 5 — Team enablement
Set:
- expectations;
- 3 Cs;
- authority;
- decision rights.

Then:
- ask;
- hold back solutions;
- develop ownership.

### Layer 6 — System navigation
Manage:
- priorities;
- change;
- politics;
- cross-functional boundaries;
- senior decisions;
- organizational resistance.

### Layer 7 — Leadership multiplication
Develop:
- talent;
- skills;
- future managers;
- shared leadership;
- self-led teams.

The reinforcing loop is:

**Good self-management → credible initiative → better influence → more trust → more delegated authority → more strategic capacity → stronger team → stronger organizational results → greater earned authority.**

A second loop concerns talent:

**Manager gives authority → employee solves problems → capability grows → trust rises → more authority can be delegated → manager gains time for strategic leadership.**

A failure loop:

**Manager solves everything → employee dependence → manager overload → less strategic time → more tactical intervention → still greater dependence.**

Another failure loop:

**Boss/organization says no → middle manager gives up immediately → no learning or coalition → idea dies → organization sees little initiative → future influence remains weak.**

The book’s desired alternative:

**No → diagnose objection → revise case → build support → persist appropriately → either win adoption or learn and move on.**
