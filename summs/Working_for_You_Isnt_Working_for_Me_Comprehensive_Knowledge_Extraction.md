# Working for You Isn’t Working for Me — Comprehensive Knowledge Extraction

**Author:** Katherine Crowley and Kathi Elster
**Primary subject:** Managing difficult bosses and authority relationships at work
**Extraction purpose:** High-recall extraction for multi-source synthesis

**Source note:** This extraction is based on the full uploaded 323-page edition. It preserves the authors’ terminology and practical architecture while compressing stories and repetition. It represents what the book claims; it does not add outside theories or external fact-checking.

## 1. Book in one model

The book treats a difficult boss/employee relationship as a coupled system rather than a problem solved by “fixing the boss.” Bosses may behave in persistently confusing, critical, controlling, avoidant, boundary-crossing, or hostile ways. Employees normally react by trying to regain control through rumination, avoidance, overwork, confrontation, retaliation, or withdrawal. Those coping reactions often create additional professional, emotional, and physical costs. The authors therefore move the intervention target toward the employee’s controllable side of the relationship: accurately diagnose the pattern, stop trying to change the boss, reduce emotional capture, understand personal authority triggers, and choose deliberate protective behavior.

The core method is the **Four D’s**:
- **Detect —** Recognize that the relationship has become toxic or destabilizing; identify your coping behavior, the phase of deterioration, and the boss behavior involved.
- **Detach —** Accept the boss’s recurring pattern instead of organizing your life around changing it; reclaim physical, emotional, and confidence resources.
- **Depersonalize —** Separate the boss’s stable behavior from your self-worth and identify your own “Boss Baggage”: expectations, needs, and fears that make certain bosses especially activating.
- **Deal —** Create a plan of protection. Adjust expectations that this boss cannot meet, address real needs through alternative sources, alleviate triggered fears by changing your own self-defeating responses, and use boss/profile-specific tactics in the moment and behind the scenes.

The book’s causal model can be reconstructed as:

**Recurring boss behavior → internal alarm → fear/expectation/need activation → automatic coping tactic → poorer communication or self-care → more negative workplace outcomes → stronger fear and rumination → physical/emotional depletion → burnout.**

The Four D’s interrupt that loop at different points: Detect makes the loop visible; Detach reduces physiological/emotional capture; Depersonalize corrects the meaning assigned to the boss’s behavior; Deal substitutes planned actions for fear-driven reactions.

## 2. Core concepts, models, and frameworks

### 2.1 The Four D’s

The Four D’s are sequential but reusable. The authors expect readers to revisit them whenever a boss relationship destabilizes.

- **Detect:** “Name the situation.” Determine what the boss is doing, how you are reacting, and where the relationship sits in its deterioration cycle.
- **Detach:** “Stop wrestling reality.” Accept the boss’s recurring behavior and restore your own energy, emotional stability, and confidence.
- **Depersonalize:** “It is not all about you, but your triggers matter.” Recognize that the boss behaved this way before you, while also identifying what in your authority history makes the behavior especially painful.
- **Deal:** “Protect self-image and career.” Use the Three A’s—adjust expectations, address unmet needs, alleviate fears—plus a boss/profile-specific response strategy.

### 2.2 Ten counterproductive coping tactics

These are normal attempts to restore power under stress, but the book argues that they usually worsen the employee’s position.

#### Obsession
- **Pattern:** Mentally replaying mixed messages and trying to solve the boss as a puzzle.
- **Cost:** Sleeplessness, distraction, lateness, forgetfulness, reduced performance, and energy drain.
#### Avoidance
- **Pattern:** Reducing contact, small talk, face-to-face communication, ideas, and visibility to stay out of the line of fire.
- **Cost:** Can look uncommitted, uncooperative, or disengaged and can itself generate poor performance feedback.
#### Self-doubt
- **Pattern:** Assuming the boss’s behavior proves something is wrong with you; old negative beliefs become louder.
- **Cost:** Erodes self-worth, confidence, and performance.
#### Sulking
- **Pattern:** Publicly displaying hurt, resentment, withdrawal, sighing, pouting, or terse replies.
- **Cost:** Others may see the behavior as childish or petty, obscuring the legitimate problem.
#### Wishing for the boss’s demise
- **Pattern:** Revenge fantasies in which the boss is removed, humiliated, punished, or harmed.
- **Cost:** Consumes mental space and produces an emotional hangover rather than practical control.
#### Gloating over the boss’s failure
- **Pattern:** Seeking vindication when the boss makes a mistake or looks bad.
- **Cost:** Makes the employee look petty and can damage the same team/company on which the employee depends.
#### Bad-mouthing
- **Pattern:** Trying to injure the boss’s reputation through complaints, disparagement, or disclosure of private information.
- **Cost:** Damages the employee’s reputation for discretion and professionalism.
#### Confrontation
- **Pattern:** A frontal accusation or emotional attack intended to force acknowledgement or fairness.
- **Cost:** Invites misunderstanding and counterattack; the power asymmetry favors the boss.
#### Retaliation / acting out
- **Pattern:** Trying to even the score by exposing, embarrassing, withholding, bypassing, or sabotaging the boss.
- **Cost:** Can burn bridges, trigger discipline, damage reputation, and lead to termination.
#### Shutting out
- **Pattern:** Silent treatment, refusal of eye contact, third-party communication, or minimal contact.
- **Cost:** Looks emotionally immature/unreasonable and can lead to formal discipline or termination.

### 2.3 Eight phases of a deteriorating boss/employee relationship

1. **Honeymoon Period —** New job/promotion/transfer/manager; both parties are on best behavior and early warning signs are rationalized or ignored.
2. **Internal Alarm —** A surprising incident produces a bodily “uh-oh” response; the employee senses a problem but often explains it away.
3. **Restart, Try Harder —** Employee works harder, becomes more agreeable, stays out of the way, or otherwise tries to restore the original goodwill.
4. **Disappointment —** Pattern becomes undeniable; the boss is not who the employee hoped. Hurt, anger, confusion, avoidance, and negative talk increase.
5. **Rehearsing and Rehashing —** The employee repeatedly reviews meetings, emails, and conversations to reconstruct what went wrong and seeks validation from others.
6. **Anger and Blame —** Victimization and resentment dominate; sulking, gloating, bad-mouthing, retaliation, and revenge fantasies become more likely.
7. **Emotional Pain Turns Physical —** Chronic strain appears as sleep problems, headaches, neck/back pain, digestive problems, TMJ, fatigue, blood pressure changes, or weight/appetite changes (examples given by the book).
8. **Burnout —** Chronic mental, physical, and emotional exhaustion; low self-esteem, depression/anger, unhealthy coping, isolation, suspicious thinking, and a trapped feeling make even job searching harder.

The phases are diagnostic rather than a rigid clock. A particularly volatile boss can move an employee through them quickly; other relationships may stall for long periods in disappointment, rumination, or anger. The practical purpose is to locate the employee in the cycle so that escalation is no longer experienced as mysterious.

### 2.4 Five boss-behavior categories and twenty boss types

#### Head Game Players
##### Chronic Critic
- **Definition:** Compulsively finds flaws in work, behavior, presentation, or results; success is quickly followed by another correction.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Perfectionistic fault-finding keeps the employee in anticipatory self-monitoring and makes adequate performance feel perpetually insufficient.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Nothing seems fully right
  - Success is immediately qualified
  - You check and recheck to preempt criticism
  - You predict what flaw the boss will find
  - You begin avoiding the boss’s judgment
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - You can never fully please this person
  - The boss must locate a flaw, shortcoming, or imperfection
- **Depersonalizing rule:** The boss’s need to criticize is not a reliable measure of your worth or competence.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Aim for small personal wins
  - Contribute outside the department
  - Record daily successes
  - Spend time with people who support you

##### Rule Changer
- **Definition:** Treats decisions, schedules, priorities, and policies as provisional and changes them without adequate notice.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Constant reversals create uncertainty, memory-doubt, rumination, and paralysis because yesterday’s rule may not survive today.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Commands and priorities keep shifting
  - You question memory/hearing
  - You repeatedly review prior conversations and emails
  - Mixed messages immobilize you
  - You stop trusting commitments
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - The boss is inconsistent and resists being pinned to fixed commitments
  - Rules, plans, decisions, and policies will keep changing
- **Depersonalizing rule:** Treat plans as provisional; consistency is not this boss’s operating mode.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Use a trusted ally for perspective/humor
  - Run a quick reality check
  - Maintain an enjoyable activity outside work
  - Create order in your home/life where you can

##### Yeller
- **Definition:** Uses loud, angry, demeaning eruptions at predictable and unpredictable moments.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Unresolved rage externalizes onto staff; employees scan the boss’s mood, brace physiologically, avoid contact, and can move quickly toward burnout.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - You monitor the boss’s emotional temperature
  - People leave meetings crying or sullen
  - You time approaches to reduce explosion risk
  - You feel dread before meetings
  - You avoid the boss to preserve self-esteem
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - The boss has anger you did not cause and cannot control
  - Explosions will continue at predictable and unpredictable moments
- **Depersonalizing rule:** The rage is a recurring boss pattern; do not treat an outburst as a precise diagnostic statement about you.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Breathe deliberately before/during/after encounters
  - Use reminders that protect self-worth
  - Exercise for physical discharge
  - Find a mentor or experienced guide

##### Underminer
- **Definition:** Delegates responsibility, then overrides it, duplicates it, reassigns it, or fails to communicate the authority that was supposedly given.
- **Book’s mechanism:** The boss’s trust/control problems create contradictory authority signals; employees feel set up to fail and colleagues compete over unclear territory.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Responsibilities are given then taken away
  - You discover dishonesty or contradictory messages
  - Boss tells different people different things
  - Trust erodes
  - You feel set up to fail
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - The boss will continue to give responsibility and then take it away
  - The boss has difficulty trusting others to carry out plans
- **Depersonalizing rule:** The boss’s trust and control problems apply broadly; retraction of authority is not proof of your incompetence.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Use a reality-check ally
  - Separate facts from mixed signals
  - Seek coaching/counseling if the confusion is chronic
  - Use positive/inspiring inputs to counter self-doubt

#### Big Shots and Mother Superiors
##### “I’m Always Right”
- **Definition:** Needs ideas, opinions, decisions, and policies to be treated as correct; disagreement becomes a contest that must be won.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Being wrong is experienced as weakness, so the boss argues, reframes, or shifts blame to preserve infallibility.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Discussion becomes point-proving
  - You agree to avoid exhausting battles
  - You resent the certainty
  - You fantasize about forced admission of error
  - Colleagues roll their eyes in meetings
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - The boss must believe he or she is correct
  - The boss is very unlikely to admit error
- **Depersonalizing rule:** The boss equates being wrong with being weak and cannot tolerate appearing weak.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Build achievement/voice in other venues
  - Create small areas of ownership
  - Use a mentor for reality and growth
  - Contribute to causes/projects that restore agency

##### “You Threaten Me”
- **Definition:** Encourages employee performance until the employee receives independent recognition, then minimizes, blocks, or punishes the employee’s visibility.
- **Book’s mechanism:** The boss experiences another person’s success as a threat to status and reasserts hierarchy by withholding credit or opportunity.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Praise turns to devaluation after recognition
  - Boss minimizes external accolades
  - Inclusion shifts to exclusion
  - Raise/promotion requests are treated as presumptuous
  - You receive difficult or low-probability assignments
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - You cannot safely outshine this boss without consequences
  - Independent positive attention may trigger efforts to hold you back
- **Depersonalizing rule:** This boss has difficulty tolerating other people’s success when it competes with his or her own status.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Find contribution/recognition outside the boss’s domain
  - Use reality checks after competitive devaluation
  - Maintain passions where success is safe
  - Spend time with people who recognize your capabilities

##### Grandiose
- **Definition:** Centers conversation and recognition on personal expertise, accomplishments, connections, and stories; listening is weak.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Self-image requires repeated reinforcement; staff become an audience and ego-support system, which is emotionally and energetically costly.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Only boss’s experience seems to count
  - Conversations revert to boss’s past stories
  - Others are rarely as smart/accomplished
  - Meetings drift from present problems into self-focused narratives
  - You want the boss to listen for once
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - Part of the job may involve acknowledgement and respectful ego support
  - The boss needs to be recognized as exceptional
- **Depersonalizing rule:** The grandiosity functions to reinforce self-image; under the bluster the book posits fear of being found lacking.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Breathe and center yourself
  - Keep energy stable rather than relying on sugar/caffeine spikes
  - Exercise
  - Protect sleep

##### Control Freak
- **Definition:** Requires visibility into nearly everything, approves decisions personally, and dislikes surprises.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Perfectionism and anxiety about losing control generate micromanagement and approval bottlenecks; employees stop exercising independent judgment.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Projects wait for boss approval
  - You second-guess decisions
  - You imagine how boss would act instead of thinking for yourself
  - You cannot answer others without checking
  - You feel boredom and frustration
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - The boss has trouble trusting others to execute directives
  - Decisions will require boss approval until trust is built
- **Depersonalizing rule:** The controlling behavior is driven by perfectionism and fear of losing control, not simply by your performance.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Take brief healthy distractions during high frustration
  - Use humor/perspective with a trusted colleague
  - Exercise to discharge control frustration
  - Take healthy restorative escapes

#### Line Crossers
##### Love-Struck
- **Definition:** Boss seeks emotional or sexual closeness through flirtation, special attention, private contact, or preferential companionship.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Power asymmetry makes both rejection and reciprocation professionally risky; the employee may feel trapped regardless of personal interest.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Long meetings become personal/flirty
  - Coworkers notice the crush
  - You seek outside advice
  - Business calls turn personal
  - You feel pressure to say/do exactly the right thing
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - You cannot control the boss’s attraction or emotional feelings
  - A professional boundary will eventually be necessary
- **Depersonalizing rule:** The boss’s feelings are not obligations; they do not have to be acted upon.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Use a reality check
  - Take time-outs when emotionally flooded
  - Maintain strong nonwork relationships
  - Reconnect with personal values and ethics

##### Calculating Confidant
- **Definition:** Creates apparent intimacy, draws out private information, then uses that information as professional leverage or judgment.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Reciprocal disclosure creates false safety; personal data becomes an asymmetrical source of power.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - You disclose substantial nonwork information
  - Meetings feel like counseling
  - Boss invites increasing off-hours closeness
  - You feel you said too much
  - Personal details later appear in work judgments
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - The boss may use personal information for professional advantage
  - Do not assume apparent personal concern equals confidentiality or protection
- **Depersonalizing rule:** The closeness serves information advantage; keep sensitive personal material outside the relationship.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Breathe before vulnerable conversations
  - Keep physical energy steady
  - Develop safe confidants outside work
  - Use professional support for personal problems
  - Exercise/mind-body practices to reduce vulnerability

##### Tell-All
- **Definition:** Overshares personal dramas and recruits staff as listeners, supporters, and sometimes practical substitutes for the boss’s own work.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Emotional disclosure elicits sympathy and caretaking; staff compensate for the boss and gradually absorb a second, unpaid emotional job.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - You spend extensive time listening
  - Boss calls you a great friend/listener
  - You feel drained by recurring dramas
  - You take on parts of boss’s work
  - You make excuses for boss based on personal troubles
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - The boss is preoccupied with personal problems
  - Sharing dramas is a way of eliciting care and protection
- **Depersonalizing rule:** You can care without becoming the boss’s emotional hand-holder or operational substitute.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Use breathing to avoid emotional contagion
  - Use a reality check against your actual job role
  - Exercise
  - Protect sleep and recovery

##### Liar, Liar
- **Definition:** Makes false promises, alters facts, denies prior statements, or engages in more serious unethical deception.
- **Book’s mechanism:** The boss treats truth instrumentally; employees lose trust, fact-check constantly, and repair relationships damaged by promises the organization cannot keep.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Boss statements and outcomes rarely match
  - Boss denies things you heard directly
  - You use other sources to establish truth
  - You repair relationships after false promises
  - You distrust almost everything said
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - The boss is comfortable altering the truth
  - The boss is not a trustworthy source without verification
- **Depersonalizing rule:** Treat important claims as needing verification rather than assuming ordinary reliability.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Breathe and stay physiologically grounded
  - Take brief mental breaks
  - Use photos/values reminders
  - Exercise
  - Use counselor/coach/mentor to reality-test and plan

#### Ambivalent Leaders
##### Sacred Cow
- **Definition:** Protected or promoted through tenure, loyalty, relationships, or family connections; risk-averse and invested in existing systems.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Security comes from preserving status quo and relationships upward, not from innovation or decisive leadership; employee initiatives repeatedly stall.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Boss defends established ways
  - Encourages ideas but blocks action
  - You wonder what boss does all day
  - You cannot understand why boss is still there
  - Frustration and disdain dominate
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - Boss may be ineffective but is likely protected
  - Direct challenge to the protected position is unlikely to work in your favor
- **Depersonalizing rule:** Stop organizing your strategy around the hope that the protected boss will disappear.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Breathe through frustration
  - Create small achievements where you control completion
  - Exercise regularly
  - Spend time with supporters

##### Checked Out
- **Definition:** Physically or psychologically absent because of personal crisis, addiction, disengagement, or chronic preoccupation.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Leadership vacuum leaves unclear expectations, stalled decisions, territorial conflict, and employees creating emergencies to attract attention.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Boss is unavailable/unresponsive
  - Lack of direction frustrates staff
  - Infighting rises
  - You wonder if old boss will return
  - You question department viability
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - Boss is preoccupied with nonwork matters
  - For now, boss may be incapable of managing effectively
- **Depersonalizing rule:** Operate as if you are temporarily in a leaderless environment and create structure where you can.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Breathe and stay engaged yourself
  - Reality-check what is actually controllable
  - Use mentor/coach/career guidance
  - Use restorative breaks/escapes

##### Spineless
- **Definition:** Avoids taking positions that could provoke disapproval; listens, nods, but does not enforce, defend, or intervene.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Fear of interpersonal conflict transfers the cost of decisions and disputes to staff, who feel unprotected and take matters into their own hands.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Sticky issues go unaddressed
  - Staff complain about wishy-washy behavior
  - You wish boss would learn management
  - You self-resolve coworker misconduct
  - You want the boss to do something
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - Boss is not capable of reliably defending others
  - Boss avoids rules or actions likely to incur someone’s anger
- **Depersonalizing rule:** Do not base your safety plan on the expectation that this boss will suddenly become confrontational on your behalf.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Change environment briefly to cool off
  - Use perspective/humor with a trusted ally
  - Get professional career guidance
  - Use mind-body stress regulation

##### Artful Dodger
- **Definition:** Protects a friendly public image by avoiding unpleasant feedback directly and using intermediaries to deliver bad news or enforce decisions.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Image management produces a split between public warmth and private criticism; staff become messengers or “bad cops” and reality becomes hard to read.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Other people deliver boss’s corrections/firings
  - Boss praises publicly and criticizes privately
  - Boss is highly image-conscious
  - You glimpse a colder calculating side
  - You increasingly see manipulation/control
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - Boss will avoid information that could tarnish image
  - Friendly exterior may coexist with calculated, indirect management
- **Depersonalizing rule:** Do not assume pleasant face-to-face feedback equals the boss’s complete view.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Use a grounded colleague for perspective
  - Keep visual reminders of what matters outside work
  - Maintain hobbies/pastimes
  - Use spiritual/values practices if meaningful

#### Delicate Circumstances
##### Junior
- **Definition:** Younger or less experienced manager with strong current/technical skills who may discount seasoned employees’ experience.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Mutual intimidation and generational differences in values, technology, and communication produce tension between “new” and “experienced.”
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - You struggle taking orders from someone younger
  - You want experience to be valued
  - Age feels like a handicap
  - You have trouble taking boss seriously
  - You feel pressure to keep up
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - The younger boss has valuable skills you may not possess
  - The boss may not automatically value your experience
- **Depersonalizing rule:** Different experience profiles can coexist; depersonalization requires respecting what the younger boss genuinely brings.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Build contribution/recognition outside department
  - Use photos/symbols for grounding
  - Reconnect with peers who value your experience
  - Contribute to causes where your knowledge is useful

##### Former Colleague
- **Definition:** A former peer/friend becomes your manager; information, authority, accountability, and social boundaries change.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Loss of peer equality is easily misread as personal coldness; the new role creates legitimate distance and performance-accountability duties.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - You miss the peer relationship
  - You are surprised by power’s effect
  - Orders trigger indignation
  - Jealousy appears
  - You long for prior camaraderie
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - The old peer relationship will not fully return
  - The new role requires your former colleague to hold you accountable
- **Depersonalizing rule:** Treat the change as a role transition, not evidence that the former colleague has necessarily betrayed you.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Use reality checks
  - Take time-outs before reactive confrontations
  - Build/reinforce other relationships
  - Develop interests and progress outside the changed relationship

##### Unconscious Discriminator
- **Definition:** Book’s term for a boss who expresses bias or treats an employee unfairly without recognizing the prejudice involved.
- **Book’s mechanism:** Unexamined beliefs about race, gender, religion, ethnicity, age, appearance, sexuality, or other differences shape opportunity, tone, inclusion, and expectations.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - You question whether a biased remark really happened
  - Boss seems uneasy with a characteristic/difference
  - Work friendliness coexists with social exclusion
  - Promotion patterns appear in-group biased
  - You oscillate between trying to change boss and feeling defeated
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - In the book’s framing, the boss may be unaware of the unfair treatment
  - Boss may be uncomfortable with differences
- **Depersonalizing rule:** The book advises distinguishing ignorance from deliberate malice while still recognizing the behavior as unfair.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Use values/proverb reminders
  - Use photos/meaningful objects for grounding
  - Maintain a strong support network
  - Use spiritual/values resources if helpful

##### Persecutor
- **Definition:** Selects an employee as a recurring target/scapegoat for hostile scrutiny, exclusion, or blame that is disproportionate to performance.
- **Book’s mechanism:** The boss appears to need a target; attempts to explain or win approval may intensify the dynamic, and the target’s self-doubt and rumination increase.
- **Diagnostic signs:**
  - Nothing seems right in boss’s eyes
  - Boss is outwardly hostile
  - Remedial efforts worsen the situation
  - You obsess over what you did
  - You are singled out for mistakes others make without incident
- **What must be accepted to detach:**
  - For reasons you may never understand, you have become the target
  - There may be little you can do to change the boss’s view
- **Depersonalizing rule:** Do not make endless self-correction the strategy; the boss may rotate targets independent of performance.
- **Primary self-recharging countermeasures:**
  - Breathe frequently
  - Use fact-based reality checks
  - Take time-outs when pressure spikes
  - Exercise
  - Seek counselor/coach/HR expertise
  - Find a mentor

### 2.5 Boss Baggage: the employee-side authority model

**Boss Baggage** is the book’s term for the historically learned mixture of expectations, needs, fears, and habitual ways of relating to authority. The authors locate its origins in earlier experiences with parents, teachers, caregivers, religious authorities, and other figures who could reward, punish, protect, or evaluate the child. The model is not presented as a clinical diagnosis. Its purpose is to explain why the same boss behavior can strongly activate one employee and barely affect another.

Three questions operationalize Boss Baggage:
- Which expectations of authority are not being fulfilled?
- Which workplace needs are not being met?
- Which fears are being triggered by this boss?

The book distinguishes these three constructs:
- **Expectations** are assumptions or hopes about what a boss will provide. They are adjustable when reality shows that a particular boss cannot deliver.
- **Needs** are stronger workplace requirements. The solution is not simply to suppress them; if the boss cannot meet them, find other people, roles, projects, communities, or structures that can.
- **Fears** are automatic threat reactions that may be triggered by ambiguous cues before evidence is available. They require awareness plus corrective behavior.

### 2.6 Ten Boss Baggage Profiles

#### Nurturer — Caregivers
- **Orientation:** Emotionally responsible for others; caring, helpful, service-oriented; notices needs and volunteers.
- **Typical expectations:**
  - Boss will trust and confide in me
  - I will feel needed by the boss
  - Caring and thoughtfulness will be appreciated
  - Acts of kindness will be valued at work
- **Typical needs:**
  - To be needed
  - To be useful and helpful
  - Appreciation for acts of kindness
  - Caring connections with boss and staff
- **Typical fears:**
  - Being found lacking
  - Appearing cold or uncaring
  - Not carrying my weight
  - Not holding it together
- **Predictable vulnerability:** Overfunctioning, blurred boundaries, rescuing, and saying yes when overloaded.

#### Harmonizer — Caregivers
- **Orientation:** Warm team player and bridge-builder; adapts to others and tries to lower interpersonal tension.
- **Typical expectations:**
  - To get along with everyone
  - To be an important member of the boss’s team
  - To be entrusted with responsibility
  - Bridge-building will be appreciated
  - To exert a positive influence on colleagues
- **Typical needs:**
  - To get along with everyone
  - To be liked
  - Appreciation for efforts
  - Benefit of the doubt after mistakes
  - To help people feel good and cooperate
- **Typical fears:**
  - Discord or infighting
  - Confrontation
  - Seeing someone in emotional pain
  - Being perceived as difficult
  - Disappointing the boss or team
- **Predictable vulnerability:** Conflict avoidance, over-accommodation, and indirect communication.

#### Star — Extroverts
- **Orientation:** Visible, ambitious, ownership-seeking performer; wants growth, leadership, and recognition.
- **Typical expectations:**
  - Access to the boss
  - Professional growth opportunities
  - Constructive criticism and training
  - Clear direction
  - A leader with a communicated vision
- **Typical needs:**
  - To shine
  - Responsibility
  - Authority to make decisions
  - To feel important
  - Skills, knowledge, and talent to be valued
- **Typical fears:**
  - Invisible work
  - Being held back
  - Micromanagement
  - Marginalization
  - Lack of respect
- **Predictable vulnerability:** Pushing too hard for visibility, ownership, or acknowledgement when feeling constrained.

#### Challenger — Extroverts
- **Orientation:** Independent, competitive, fairness-oriented questioner of authority; speaks uncomfortable truths and challenges the status quo.
- **Typical expectations:**
  - Boss wants to hear my view
  - Honest opinions will be appreciated
  - Outside-the-box thinking will be valued
  - Boss will prove worthy of respect
  - Irreverence has a place at work
- **Typical needs:**
  - To be heard
  - Permission to try new things
  - Freedom to think outside the box
  - Acknowledgement when right
- **Typical fears:**
  - Not being heard
  - Marginalization
  - Being taken lightly
  - Being labeled a troublemaker
- **Predictable vulnerability:** Turning disagreements into power struggles or overcommunicating dissent.

#### Observer — Introverts
- **Orientation:** Quiet, skeptical, process-oriented evaluator who needs time to understand before acting.
- **Typical expectations:**
  - Time to process people, places, and information
  - Boss will prove worthy of respect
  - Efforts will be seen and appreciated
  - To be heard when I speak
  - To be trusted to do a good job
- **Typical needs:**
  - Time to assess before acting

### 2.7 Boss Baggage Assessment procedure

The book turns the ten profiles into a self-assessment rather than insisting that a person belongs to a single type.
- Read all ten profile descriptions and answer each profile’s yes/no questions.
- Tally the “yes” responses for each profile.
- Rank the top three authority orientations; ties are allowed and multiple profiles may be meaningful.
- For those top profiles, review the associated expectations, needs, and fears and check the items that fit.
- Choose the top five expectations, top five needs, and top five fears. Together with the top three orientations, these form the reader’s Boss Baggage Profile.
- Observe actual authority interactions: good relationships tend to meet some key needs/expectations while activating fewer fears; difficult relationships tend to do the reverse.

### 2.8 The Three A’s of dealing

The final D—**Deal**—converts self-knowledge into a protection plan. The Three A’s are the internal architecture of that plan.
- **Adjust expectations:** Stop expecting a boss to provide qualities he or she repeatedly demonstrates an inability to provide. Adjustment is framed as adaptation and protection, not approval or lowered standards.
- **Address unmet needs:** Identify which needs remain genuinely important and build alternative sources of fulfillment—coworkers, mentors, professional associations, projects, committees, task forces, communities, volunteering, or a future job.
- **Alleviate fears:** Identify the fear most active in the relationship, notice the behavior that fear produces, and deliberately practice a corrective action that breaks the self-confirming loop.

### 2.9 Fear feedback loop

The book includes a simple reinforcing-loop diagram in Chapter 7:

**Hidden fear → automatic self-protective behavior → negative reaction from the recipient → the feared outcome appears to come true.**

The important move is to intervene between fear and behavior. A fear may be understandable and the boss may genuinely be difficult, but if the employee’s protective behavior predictably evokes more rejection, criticism, exclusion, or distrust, the fear becomes self-confirming.

## 3. Principles and mechanisms

### The quality of authority relationships has spillover effects
The authors argue that a painful boss relationship can contaminate work, home, social life, sleep, self-esteem, and physical well-being because the employee continues mentally interacting with the boss after work has ended.

### Attempts to control the boss often increase dependence on the boss
Rumination, appeasement, confrontation, revenge, and avoidance all keep attention organized around the boss. The more the employee tries to force a different response, the more the boss’s next action determines the employee’s internal state.

### Acceptance reduces the power struggle
Acceptance means accurately predicting the boss’s recurring behavior. When the employee stops requiring reality to be different before acting, planning becomes possible. Acceptance explicitly does not mean approval, agreement, or condoning.

### Detachment is participation without emotional capture
The target state is not coldness. The employee can still communicate, contribute, and care while maintaining enough psychological distance that the boss’s mood or judgment does not dictate self-worth.

### Short-term self-medication is different from restoration
Food, alcohol, cigarettes, television, gaming, shopping, and other escape behaviors may reduce distress immediately but can add longer-term costs. Recharging activities are meant to restore capacity rather than merely suppress awareness.

### Confidence expands perceived options
When chronic criticism or persecution erodes self-confidence, employees feel trapped and cannot imagine a better role. Rebuilding competence evidence and support is therefore a career intervention, not just a mood intervention.

### Boss behavior and employee meaning are separable
The boss can genuinely behave badly while the employee also adds an interpretation such as “this proves I am incompetent” or “I am being erased.” Depersonalization separates conduct from identity without absolving the boss.

### Stable boss patterns should change forecasts
Once a boss is correctly detected, treat future recurrence as probable: the Rule Changer will change, the Chronic Critic will criticize, the Control Freak will seek oversight, and the Yeller may erupt. Better forecasting reduces surprise and helps choose strategy.

### Expectations are negotiable; needs require sourcing; fears require counter-behavior
This distinction prevents a single intervention from being applied to all distress. Some disappointment is solved by expectation adjustment, some by building alternative support, and some by exposure to feared actions such as asking, saying no, or speaking up.

### Different employee profiles require different doses of the same tactic
Silence helps a Storyteller with a Yeller but can worsen a Worker Bee’s invisibility with other bosses. The book therefore cross-tabulates boss behavior with employee authority style rather than prescribing one generic communication rule.

### In-the-moment response and behind-the-scenes strategy are different
Acute tactics protect the interaction now; longer-term tactics manage career trajectory, alliances, documentation, skill growth, HR escalation, or exit.

### Some situations are not relationship-improvement problems
Across the deal chapters, especially for Persecutors and severe dishonesty/boundary violations, the book sometimes recommends documentation, professional advice, HR, or exit rather than endless adaptation.

## 4. Strategies, tactics, and techniques

### 4.1 Acceptance practice

**Goal:** Stop spending energy on the proposition that the boss should become a different person before you can function.

**Procedure:**
- Write a sentence for every recurring boss trait that irritates or destabilizes you.
- Prefix each sentence with **“I accept the fact that…”** Examples in the book include accepting disorganization, moodiness, fault-finding, truth-stretching, or credit-taking.
- Read the statements aloud. Notice which facts are relatively easy to acknowledge and which trigger strong resistance.
- Add the emotional facts you resist as well: “I accept that I do not like this person,” “I accept that I wish the boss were different,” or “I accept that I miss my former boss.”
- Review the list regularly. The book treats acceptance as a practiced skill rather than a one-time intellectual insight.
- Use the clearer forecast created by acceptance to decide what needs protection, alternative sourcing, documentation, or exit.

**Mechanism claimed by the book:** Each accepted fact reduces surprise and the interpersonal tug-of-war, freeing attention for actions under the employee’s control.

### 4.2 Taking back personal power: the full recharging curriculum

#### Restore energy — after work
- Exercise: walking, running, swimming, dancing, sports, weights, aerobics, hiking, boxing/kickboxing, cycling, skating/skiing, martial arts, yoga, Pilates.
- Mind/body remedies: massage, acupuncture, reflexology, Alexander Technique, biofeedback, and moving or sitting meditation.
- Medical attention if the stress is affecting health: physical checkups, physical therapy, nutrition support.
- Healthy escapes: nature, gardens, forests, trails, beaches/water, landmarks, day trips, weekend getaways, sports/spa/relaxation/sightseeing breaks.
- Sleep: protect a decent bedtime and remove television, computer, phone, music, and other distractions when they interfere with restorative sleep.

#### Restore energy — at work
- Breathing exercise: sit upright with feet grounded; inhale gently while thinking “I am”; exhale slowly with “relaxed”; repeat until arousal drops.
- “H2O cure”: wash hands, splash face, drink cold water, or sip hot water as a sensory reset.
- Use pleasant scent cues if helpful (flowers, oils, other acceptable workplace scents).
- Change environment briefly: walk outside, visit another floor, step away from the immediate trigger.
- Reduce energy spikes by lowering sugar/caffeine and choosing steadier food/drink.

#### Repair emotional state — after work
- Return to a passion or pastime: reading, art, fishing, languages, gardening, cooking, crafts, or another enjoyable skill.
- Reach out and reconnect; actively counter the tendency to isolate from friends, family, and positive professional relationships.
- Turn to a professional: short-term counseling, career coach, support group, or other trained support.
- Use spiritual/religious resources if personally meaningful: prayer, meditation, services, reading, community.
- Improve the home environment through music, art, plants/flowers, scent, or decluttering.
- Seek inspiration through books, music, museums, concerts, plays, movies, sports, or exhibits.

#### Repair emotional state — at work
- Comic relief ally: a trusted colleague/friend who can listen, problem-solve, provide perspective, and help lower tension.
- Photo therapy: keep visual reminders of family, friends, pets, meaningful places, nature, or other priorities beyond the boss.
- Game break: a brief low-stakes game as a time-limited distraction/reset.
- Quick Reality Check: What is happening? What are the facts? What is the boss’s part? What is my part? What are my options?
- Call a time-out: temporarily leave the interaction/space until emotional arousal drops.
- Lucky charm / grounding object: carry a meaningful symbol, stone, beads, heirloom, or religious object that cues strength and perspective.

#### Rebuild confidence — after work
- Find a mentor: an older/more experienced person in the company, alumni network, professional association, or personal network who can advise and reality-check.
- Write a daily success list, including small actions and major accomplishments.
- Spend time with people who support you and remember your capabilities.
- Make a difference by volunteering time/skills to a cause, community, civic group, or professional association.

#### Rebuild confidence — at work
- Post workplace proverbs or statements that cue a constructive internal dialogue.
- Gratitude inventory: when work feels like a losing game, write three things that are still going well or matter to you.
- Aim for small achievements you can complete and own.
- Find ways to be useful outside your immediate department through task forces, committees, campaigns, conferences, and special events.

### 4.3 Detachment anti-patterns

- Do not increase sugar/caffeine as a stress-management strategy; the book argues the resulting highs and lows make emotional reactivity worse.
- Do not isolate; reach outward rather than hiding and nursing the injury alone.
- Do not convert venting into chronic bad-mouthing, complaining, or gossip. If you vent, choose someone who helps you move toward a solution.
- Do not overuse alcohol, drugs, television, games, shopping, gambling, or other numbing/escape behaviors as “self-medication.”
- Do not redirect workplace anger onto family, friends, or other easy targets.
- If distress becomes severe—depression, self-loathing, destructive thoughts, or major health effects—the book explicitly recommends professional and possibly medical support.

### 4.4 Boss-specific detachment plans

#### Chronic Critic
**Accept:**
- You can never fully please this person
- The boss must locate a flaw, shortcoming, or imperfection
**Then take back power through:**
- Aim for small personal wins
- Contribute outside the department
- Record daily successes
- Spend time with people who support you
**Operational stance:** The boss’s need to criticize is not a reliable measure of your worth or competence.

#### Rule Changer
**Accept:**
- The boss is inconsistent and resists being pinned to fixed commitments
- Rules, plans, decisions, and policies will keep changing
**Then take back power through:**
- Use a trusted ally for perspective/humor
- Run a quick reality check
- Maintain an enjoyable activity outside work
- Create order in your home/life where you can
**Operational stance:** Treat plans as provisional; consistency is not this boss’s operating mode.

#### Yeller
**Accept:**
- The boss has anger you did not cause and cannot control
- Explosions will continue at predictable and unpredictable moments
**Then take back power through:**
- Breathe deliberately before/during/after encounters
- Use reminders that protect self-worth
- Exercise for physical discharge
- Find a mentor or experienced guide
**Operational stance:** The rage is a recurring boss pattern; do not treat an outburst as a precise diagnostic statement about you.

#### Underminer
**Accept:**
- The boss will continue to give responsibility and then take it away
- The boss has difficulty trusting others to carry out plans
**Then take back power through:**
- Use a reality-check ally
- Separate facts from mixed signals
- Seek coaching/counseling if the confusion is chronic
- Use positive/inspiring inputs to counter self-doubt
**Operational stance:** The boss’s trust and control problems apply broadly; retraction of authority is not proof of your incompetence.

#### “I’m Always Right”
**Accept:**
- The boss must believe he or she is correct
- The boss is very unlikely to admit error
**Then take back power through:**
- Build achievement/voice in other venues
- Create small areas of ownership
- Use a mentor for reality and growth
- Contribute to causes/projects that restore agency
**Operational stance:** The boss equates being wrong with being weak and cannot tolerate appearing weak.

#### “You Threaten Me”
**Accept:**
- You cannot safely outshine this boss without consequences
- Independent positive attention may trigger efforts to hold you back
**Then take back power through:**
- Find contribution/recognition outside the boss’s domain
- Use reality checks after competitive devaluation
- Maintain passions where success is safe
- Spend time with people who recognize your capabilities
**Operational stance:** This boss has difficulty tolerating other people’s success when it competes with his or her own status.

#### Grandiose
**Accept:**
- Part of the job may involve acknowledgement and respectful ego support
- The boss needs to be recognized as exceptional
**Then take back power through:**
- Breathe and center yourself
- Keep energy stable rather than relying on sugar/caffeine spikes
- Exercise
- Protect sleep
**Operational stance:** The grandiosity functions to reinforce self-image; under the bluster the book posits fear of being found lacking.

#### Control Freak
**Accept:**
- The boss has trouble trusting others to execute directives
- Decisions will require boss approval until trust is built
**Then take back power through:**
- Take brief healthy distractions during high frustration
- Use humor/perspective with a trusted colleague
- Exercise to discharge control frustration
- Take healthy restorative escapes
**Operational stance:** The controlling behavior is driven by perfectionism and fear of losing control, not simply by your performance.

#### Love-Struck
**Accept:**
- You cannot control the boss’s attraction or emotional feelings
- A professional boundary will eventually be necessary
**Then take back power through:**
- Use a reality check
- Take time-outs when emotionally flooded
- Maintain strong nonwork relationships
- Reconnect with personal values and ethics
**Operational stance:** The boss’s feelings are not obligations; they do not have to be acted upon.

#### Calculating Confidant
**Accept:**
- The boss may use personal information for professional advantage
- Do not assume apparent personal concern equals confidentiality or protection
**Then take back power through:**
- Breathe before vulnerable conversations
- Keep physical energy steady
- Develop safe confidants outside work
- Use professional support for personal problems
- Exercise/mind-body practices to reduce vulnerability
**Operational stance:** The closeness serves information advantage; keep sensitive personal material outside the relationship.

#### Tell-All
**Accept:**
- The boss is preoccupied with personal problems
- Sharing dramas is a way of eliciting care and protection
**Then take back power through:**
- Use breathing to avoid emotional contagion
- Use a reality check against your actual job role
- Exercise
- Protect sleep and recovery
**Operational stance:** You can care without becoming the boss’s emotional hand-holder or operational substitute.

#### Liar, Liar
**Accept:**
- The boss is comfortable altering the truth
- The boss is not a trustworthy source without verification
**Then take back power through:**
- Breathe and stay physiologically grounded
- Take brief mental breaks
- Use photos/values reminders
- Exercise
- Use counselor/coach/mentor to reality-test and plan
**Operational stance:** Treat important claims as needing verification rather than assuming ordinary reliability.

#### Sacred Cow
**Accept:**
- Boss may be ineffective but is likely protected
- Direct challenge to the protected position is unlikely to work in your favor
**Then take back power through:**
- Breathe through frustration
- Create small achievements where you control completion
- Exercise regularly
- Spend time with supporters
**Operational stance:** Stop organizing your strategy around the hope that the protected boss will disappear.

#### Checked Out
**Accept:**
- Boss is preoccupied with nonwork matters
- For now, boss may be incapable of managing effectively
**Then take back power through:**
- Breathe and stay engaged yourself
- Reality-check what is actually controllable
- Use mentor/coach/career guidance
- Use restorative breaks/escapes
**Operational stance:** Operate as if you are temporarily in a leaderless environment and create structure where you can.

#### Spineless
**Accept:**
- Boss is not capable of reliably defending others
- Boss avoids rules or actions likely to incur someone’s anger
**Then take back power through:**
- Change environment briefly to cool off
- Use perspective/humor with a trusted ally
- Get professional career guidance
- Use mind-body stress regulation
**Operational stance:** Do not base your safety plan on the expectation that this boss will suddenly become confrontational on your behalf.

#### Artful Dodger
**Accept:**
- Boss will avoid information that could tarnish image
- Friendly exterior may coexist with calculated, indirect management
**Then take back power through:**
- Use a grounded colleague for perspective
- Keep visual reminders of what matters outside work
- Maintain hobbies/pastimes
- Use spiritual/values practices if meaningful
**Operational stance:** Do not assume pleasant face-to-face feedback equals the boss’s complete view.

#### Junior
**Accept:**
- The younger boss has valuable skills you may not possess
- The boss may not automatically value your experience
**Then take back power through:**
- Build contribution/recognition outside department
- Use photos/symbols for grounding
- Reconnect with peers who value your experience
- Contribute to causes where your knowledge is useful
**Operational stance:** Different experience profiles can coexist; depersonalization requires respecting what the younger boss genuinely brings.

#### Former Colleague
**Accept:**
- The old peer relationship will not fully return
- The new role requires your former colleague to hold you accountable
**Then take back power through:**
- Use reality checks
- Take time-outs before reactive confrontations
- Build/reinforce other relationships
- Develop interests and progress outside the changed relationship
**Operational stance:** Treat the change as a role transition, not evidence that the former colleague has necessarily betrayed you.

#### Unconscious Discriminator
**Accept:**
- In the book’s framing, the boss may be unaware of the unfair treatment
- Boss may be uncomfortable with differences
**Then take back power through:**
- Use values/proverb reminders
- Use photos/meaningful objects for grounding
- Maintain a strong support network
- Use spiritual/values resources if helpful
**Operational stance:** The book advises distinguishing ignorance from deliberate malice while still recognizing the behavior as unfair.

#### Persecutor
**Accept:**
- For reasons you may never understand, you have become the target
- There may be little you can do to change the boss’s view
**Then take back power through:**
- Breathe frequently
- Use fact-based reality checks
- Take time-outs when pressure spikes
- Exercise
- Seek counselor/coach/HR expertise
- Find a mentor
**Operational stance:** Do not make endless self-correction the strategy; the boss may rotate targets independent of performance.

### 4.5 Profile-specific depersonalization matrix

#### “I’m Always Right”
**Universal law:** Being wrong is equated with weakness; appearing weak is intolerable.
- **Nurturer —** Nurturers understand that every boss needs a certain amount of ego stroking. The Always Right boss is an extreme case of this, but you can depersonalize his or her over-the-top behavior by reminding yourself that making your boss right is how you get the most out of this person.
- **Harmonizer —** When your headstrong boss refuses to consider your point of view, you quickly realize that it’s best to back off and approach the issue at another time. To depersonalize, understand that your confident leader has an indisputable need to win every discussion.
- **Star —** employees can see the bigger picture and learn to use agreement as a tool to get what they want. Depersonalize by reminding yourself that the best way to shine is to always agree with and acknowledge your boss’s perspective first before delivering your own ideas.
- **Challenger —** To depersonalize from this kind of boss, you have to put your need to be heard on hold. You can put your ideas and opinions in writing, but wait for the appropriate opportunity to share them.
- **Observer —** To depersonalize from this kind of authority figure, try to see his or her need to be right as a mental dis ability that you did not cause and cannot cure. Do your best to look beyond it.
- **Worker Bee —** The challenge comes when the boss starts to correct your assignments and diminish your accom plishments. At those times, you can take his or her critical comments less personally by recognizing that this individual corrects and admonishes all employees in order to feel superior.
- **Storyteller —** To depersonalize this situation, find other people who want to hear your anecdotes and ideas. Realize that your leader can toler ate only short, concise comments.
- **Disarmer —** Disarmers see the writing on the wall quickly when it comes to an “I’m Always Right” boss. Once you’ve discovered that your manager must be correct on every issue, you can depersonalize by using humor to deflect any negative reactions to your ideas.
- **Coaster —** If you need to depersonalize from an Always Right boss, use your friendly personality to affirm and acknowledge your boss on a regular basis.
- **Low Beamer —** To depersonalize from these moments, refrain from arguing over who is correct. Take notes, make the adjustments, and understand that everyone, not just you, is adhering to your Always Right boss’s rules.

#### “You Threaten Me”
**Universal law:** The boss has trouble with other people’s success.
- **Nurturer —** They are not that concerned about receiving accolades for their accomplishments. For this reason, “You Threaten Me” bosses aren’t threatened by them.
- **Harmonizer —** If, however, you sense that a colleague’s success could threaten the boss, you may help that person depersonalize by suggesting ways to defuse your boss’s envy.
- **Star —** Instead of challenging his or her attempts to shoot you down, you can depersonalize. Until you are ready to move on, focus on doing your usual excellent work and let the criticism roll off your back.
- **Challenger —** It’s important to remember that a “You Threaten Me” boss can listen to your ideas if you don’t question his or her authority. Depersonalize by understanding that your employer must have top billing.
- **Observer —** Should you be disturbed by “You Threaten Me”’s envy of other people’s success, you can depersonalize by seeing your boss’s insecurity with compassion rather than disdain.
- **Worker Bee —** To depersonalize, remember that you deserve the praise, even if your boss tries to convince you otherwise.
- **Storyteller —** The Storyteller’s greatest need is to be heard and to have his or her experiences acknowledged. While a “You Threaten Me” boss may be competitive regarding success, he or she is often willing to listen to employees in exchange for their loyalty and support.
- **Disarmer —** Should you receive too much posi tive attention—in the form of either general popularity or apprecia tion from high-level executives—you may have to depersonalize your manager’s sudden cutting comments. To do this, remember that you didn’t do anything wrong.
- **Coaster —** Because Coasters aren’t competing to be recognized for their excellent work, you pose no threat to a “You Threaten Me” boss. This kind of leader should be no problem for you.
- **Low Beamer —** Like the Coaster, you aren’t trying to gain visibility in the workplace. Your low-risk approach to work virtually guarantees smooth sailing with a “You Threaten Me” boss.

#### Grandiose
**Universal law:** The boss invests heavily in reinforcing self-image; the book interprets the bluster as covering fear of being found lacking.
- **Nurturer —** Nurturers tend to see through the Grandiose boss’s blustery ways and focus on supporting their emotionally needy leader without judgment. You may not need help depersonalizing from your boss, but make sure to restore your energy on a daily basis (see Chapter 3).
- **Harmonizer —** To depersonalize your relationship with this overbear ing manager, keep the amount of time you spend in his or her company to a minimum. Working around a Grandiose boss usually yields the best results.
- **Star —** Star employees find the Grandiose boss’s shameless self-promotion entertaining. You’ll quickly learn that this loud leader attributes any success you have on the job to his or her brilliance: “I made you who you are today!” To depersonalize, learn to let your boss have the credit while you move forward with your career.
- **Challenger —** To depersonalize from your big-ego boss, remember that his or her inability to acknowledge your point of view is not about you. Save your ideas for more receptive audiences and vent your frustration outside of work.
- **Observer —** To depersonalize from this brand of leader, try limiting your contact—if possible. When you do have to listen, try to respond to your boss’s self-aggrandizing statements with a glimmer of respect.
- **Worker Bee —** To depersonalize from your Grandiose manager, don’t take his or her dramatic delivery of criticism or anger to heart. Instead, try giving your boss the praise, recognition, and acknowl edgment that he or she desires.
- **Storyteller —** Anyone who works for a Grandiose boss quickly learns that this person has very little interest in listening. A storytelling employee will find this characteristic extremely frustrating.
- **Disarmer —** You’ll learn how to make this attention-seeking supervisor laugh.
- **Coaster —** To depersonalize, learn to hear your blustery boss’s claims about your incompetence as part of his or her superiority song.
- **Low Beamer —** To depersonalize, add “listen to my boss talk” to your job description and take frequent breaks to reenergize.

#### Control Freaks
**Universal law:** Perfectionism and fear of losing control drive the behavior.
- **Nurturer —** You may find your controlling boss’s rigid rules confining and, frankly, some what insulting. To depersonalize, understand that the Control Freak’s need to scrutinize everything you do is not a reflection of your value or competence.
- **Harmonizer —** Harmonizers like to be entrusted with re sponsibility and don’t appreciate waiting around for approval. With time, however, you can depersonalize by understanding that your boss needs to carefully oversee all functions before he or she can loosen the reigns.
- **Star —** To depersonalize from your boss’s constricting behavior, realize that this person’s inability to let you charge ahead is not about you. Slow down, follow the rules, and wait for opportunities to shine.
- **Challenger —** Your manager will thwart your attempts to shake things up until he or she believes you are a trustworthy member of the team. If you can slow down and play by your boss’s rules, you may be able to win his or her trust.
- **Observer —** To depersonalize, your best bet is to prove that you don’t require constant surveillance by following your boss’s explicit instructions for a period of time.
- **Worker Bee —** Worker Bees may find this boss’s need to hone in on their work and dictate the exact way of doing things unnerving. To depersonalize from a Control Freak boss, understand that this person needs time and experi ence to ease up on the oversight.
- **Storyteller —** To depersonalize, try to develop a thicker skin when your controlling leader starts to correct you. Remember that this individual needs to microman age everyone, not just you.
- **Disarmer —** To depersonalize, remember that there will be times when you miss the mark. Your boss can’t resist finding the one detail that you overlooked.
- **Coaster —** To deperson alize from your vigilant manager, realize that your boss isn’t questioning your competence. Try to play along.
- **Low Beamer —** A Control Freak boss gives low-beaming employees clear instruction, detailed assignments, and constant oversight. Your boss won’t ask you to do anything without showing you how to do it.

#### Love-Struck
- **Nurturer —** To depersonalize, realize that you didn’t cause this to happen, and your boss will survive no matter what you do.
- **Harmonizer —** To depersonal ize, understand that your boss will survive any verdict you arrive at.
- **Star —** To depersonalize, see that your boss’s feelings are his or hers to manage.
- **Challenger —** It can be exciting for Challengers when the person who holds power over them also finds them irresistible. Depersonalize by remembering that one of you may get hurt in the end.
- **Observer —** To depersonalize, use your cautious behavior to deliver a consistently cool message.
- **Worker Bee —** To depersonalize from a boss who has amorous feelings for you, understand that you don’t have to do anything to address the situation directly.
- **Storyteller —** If your boss is Love- Struck, he or she may find everything you have to say fascinating—an experience you will relish. To depersonalize from a Love-Struck boss, keep in mind that you don’t have to explain or defend your reasons for keeping the relationship professional.
- **Disarmer —** Depersonalize by keeping the focus on doing your job.
- **Coaster —** To depersonalize, help your boss steer the relationship into safer waters.
- **Low Beamer —** To depersonalize, remind yourself that you are not re sponsible for your boss’s feelings.

#### Calculating Confidants
**Universal law:** Closeness can be used to gain the upper hand through information.
- **Nurturer —** To depersonalize from this kind of boss, understand that not everyone who appears nurturing actually is. Learn to keep confidential information to yourself.
- **Harmonizer —** To depersonalize from this kind of leader, trust your instincts when you sense that your boss’s need for the intimate details about your life outside of work may have ulterior motives.
- **Star —** To deper sonalize, understand that you are not alone.
- **Challenger —** Trust your gut and keep your invasive boss at arm’s length.
- **Observer —** To depersonalize from a Calculating Confi dant, trust your internal signals and keep your private information under wraps.
- **Worker Bee —** For these reasons, you won’t have to work at taking a Calculating Confidant less personally. It’s your instinctive inclination.
- **Storyteller —** To depersonalize, try not to take this authority figure’s dismissive attitude toward you to heart.
- **Disarmer —** To the Calculating Confidant’s dismay, disarming employees are actually quite private about their lives outside of work and quite adept at keeping other people’s secrets. To depersonalize, understand that your boss has a bad habit of uncovering other people’s secrets only to misuse them.
- **Coaster —** To depersonalize, keep in mind that your willingness to offer information about others could backfire.
- **Low Beamer —** A Calculating Confidant will quickly learn that you are not interested in divulging much of anything except your time sheets and workplace requirements.

#### Tell-Alls
- **Nurturer —** Problems arise when you find yourself doing two jobs—yours and your employer’s. To depersonal ize, recognize that your boss is using you (and others).
- **Harmonizer —** With time, however, you’ll notice that your best advice goes unheeded, and there’s no end to the Tell-All’s woes. To depersonalize, remind your self that the person you work for doesn’t really want to get better.
- **Star —** To depersonalize, realize that you aren’t responsible for solving your boss’s problems. Tell-Alls can always find someone else to lean on.
- **Challenger —** To depersonalize from this helpless manager, understand that some people have no interest in changing, getting better, or resolving their personal problems.
- **Observer —** To depersonalize, check in regularly to make sure your in-box isn’t overloaded with your manager’s responsibilities. Remember that Tell-Alls tend to take advantage of those who are willing to carry a heavy load.
- **Worker Bee —** You may find this employer’s colorful tales and personal dramas both fascinating and amusing. To depersonalize, realize that your boss’s dramas and difficulties will never end.
- **Storyteller —** To depersonalize, understand that your boss is incapable of hearing anyone’s story but his or her own.
- **Disarmer —** Using your humor and charm, you’ll naturally know how to dodge the emotional neediness of a Tell-All manager. To deper sonalize, trust your instincts and set limits.
- **Coaster —** To depersonalize, don’t take to heart the pressure the Tell-All puts on you.
- **Low Beamer —** Most low-beaming employees see a job as a job and resent situations where they are asked to shoulder additional responsibilities. To depersonalize, realize that you didn’t do anything to warrant this pres sure.

#### Liar, Liars
**Universal law:** Truth is bent to serve the boss’s purposes.
- **Nurturer —** You’re able to see the boss’s habit of stretching, bending, or altering the truth through somewhat compassionate eyes. To depersonalize, con tinue to regard your manager’s lying as a problem that you didn’t cause.
- **Harmonizer —** Harmonizers have a very hard time with Liar, Liar bosses because they see and feel the fallout when this kind of leader deceives or misleads others. To depersonalize, realize that you are not responsible for the falsehoods that your boss fabricates.
- **Star —** To depersonalize from this kind of leader, know that you work for someone who is incapable of being honest.
- **Challenger —** To depersonalize from a Liar, Liar boss, you have to realize that you didn’t cause this person’s condition and you aren’t going to change it.
- **Observer —** To depersonalize, understand that your boss lies to everyone—not just you.
- **Worker Bee —** To depersonal ize, understand that your boss operates from a unique moral code.
- **Storyteller —** To depersonalize from a Liar, Liar boss, you’ll have to realize that you work for someone whose words and actions don’t always match.
- **Disarmer —** While you may be able to uncover certain falsehoods, you’ll still need to depersonalize by understanding that your boss will continue to lie.
- **Coaster —** To deper sonalize, realize that, if caught in a lie, this kind of boss will probably try to lay the blame on you.
- **Low Beamer —** To depersonalize from this kind of leader, you’ll have to understand that you’re working for some one who doesn’t value the truth the way you do.

#### Sacred Cows
**Universal law:** Risk aversion and protection of the status quo dominate.
- **Nurturer —** On those occasions when the Sacred Cow thwarts your attempts at trying anything new or innovative, you can depersonalize by remem bering to give your boss credit for any positive results. The more secure and praised your Sacred Cow boss feels, the more likely he or she is to see things your way.
- **Harmonizer —** They have a hard time with anyone who would systematically block their best efforts. To depersonalize, realize that you are working for someone whose greatest fear is looking foolish.
- **Star —** To depersonalize, use your strong people skills to work around the problem. Understand that the first line of business is to make your ideas seem as if they are also the property of your boss.
- **Challenger —** To depersonalize, you’ll need to constantly remind yourself that your infuriating manager is probably threatened by your innovative ap proach to problem solving. This person isn’t going anywhere, so your best bet is to back off and look for ways to build trust.
- **Observer —** To depersonal ize, use your perceptive abilities to determine when your boss is open to taking action and when the door is closed. It’s a lot of work, but you can keep a careful watch on this leader’s mind-set and push your ideas when the perfect opportunity arises.
- **Worker Bee —** Ever-resourceful Worker Bees are masters of the work- around and do not find the Sacred Cow’s static ways too cumbersome. If, at times, you are frustrated by the lack of progress that your department or division seems to make, you can depersonalize by realizing that this leader isn’t going anywhere.
- **Storyteller —** You can deper sonalize from a brusque boss by understanding that this brand of bovine is not open to anyone’s stories.
- **Disarmer —** You’ll know how to humor this kind of leader, but your best jokes won’t move the boss to relinquish control. To depersonalize, remember that this individual is extremely risk-averse and fears doing anything beyond the status quo.
- **Coaster —** You’ll appreciate the fact that your employer isn’t overly con cerned with producing results. Your boss will like your relaxed attitude on the job and your desire to balance work and home life.
- **Low Beamer —** Sacred Cow bosses will ask you to do your job by the book. Because they don’t like to make mistakes, change policies, or look bad, Sacred Cows will instruct you to do your work carefully, exactly as it has been done in the past.

#### Checked Out Bosses
- **Nurturer —** To depersonalize, understand that you’re in a temporary situation. Try delegating some of the responsibilities you’re shouldering to colleagues who can help carry the weight.
- **Harmonizer —** To depersonalize from a Checked Out boss, realize that you cannot cure your employer’s personal malaise. Focus instead on building cooperation among your colleagues and dis tributing the work.
- **Star —** To depersonalize, focus on the boss’s actions and the fact that he or she is not capable of steering the company ship. You can then step into a leadership position, keep the boss informed of your actions, and wait for your manager’s safe return.
- **Challenger —** If the boss is Checked Out, Challengers find the lack of leadership frustrating and a little disorient ing. It’s important to depersonalize by realizing that your boss’s behavior has nothing to do with you and that acting out is not likely to help the situation.
- **Observer —** Observing employees may not be disturbed by a Checked Out boss. Keep cool, continue to do your work, and know that the boss will eventually return.
- **Worker Bee —** Depersonalizing this person’s mental and emotional absence will come naturally to you.
- **Storyteller —** To depersonalize, understand that the boss’s disinterest is not an indictment of you.
- **Disarmer —** Because you are accustomed to your boss’s being an apprecia tive audience, it may be challenging when this person checks out and pulls away. To depersonalize, realize that your employer is on a temporary hiatus.
- **Coaster —** For these reasons, a Checked Out boss may not cause any kind of distress. You can maintain your usual relaxed, friendly stance and wait for the old boss to come back.
- **Low Beamer —** Understand that this leader is not out to foil you. See if anyone else in the office can take the lead while you wait for your troubled employer to check back in.

#### Spineless Bosses
- **Nurturer —** The only time you’ll have to depersonalize is when the boss fails to stand up for you when it’s time for a promotion, a raise, or some other form of recognition. You’ll need to consistently remind your boss of the raise, promo tion, award, or policy change that you’re seeking.
- **Harmonizer —** While you may not mind the fact that your boss cannot stand up on your behalf, you’ll find it very disheartening when this manager’s lack of a backbone causes distress to other members of the staff. To depersonalize, you’ll need to step back and see that this person is constitutionally incapable of making strong decisions or coming to someone’s aid.
- **Star —** To depersonalize, use your leadership skills to fill in the gap.
- **Challenger —** Very quickly, you’ll learn that this person is incapable of such a transformation, and you’ll be sorely disappointed. To depersonal ize, do your best not to hold your boss in contempt.
- **Observer —** You can depersonalize by scanning your work environment and determining who the real decision makers are.
- **Worker Bee —** To depersonalize, you’ll have to let go of any hope that your boss can advo cate for you.
- **Storyteller —** You can offer your boss explicit descriptions and details of improvements that you think would smooth out opera tions, but you aren’t likely to see any results. To depersonalize, don’t waste your words on someone who can only nod in agreement.
- **Disarmer —** Depersonalize by learning how best to work around your employer. You may find that you need to make decisions and take a stand on behalf of your boss.
- **Coaster —** To depersonalize, understand that this individual is incapable of protecting anyone at work. See if someone else can come to your assistance.
- **Low Beamer —** To depersonalize, you may have to forgive your boss for failing to come to your aid when someone attacks your performance. Learn to seek guidance and protection elsewhere.

#### Artful Dodgers
**Universal law:** The boss avoids direct honesty when honesty could damage the preferred image.
- **Nurturer —** To depersonalize, understand that you work for someone who is incapable of giving negative feedback to anyone.
- **Harmonizer —** Initially, a harmonizing employee will try to carry out the Artful Dodger’s biddings. To depersonalize, realize that being the bad cop is a primary function of your job.
- **Star —** To deper sonalize, realize that your boss is using you to do his or her dirty work. Try becoming a bit less available as the negative mouthpiece.
- **Challenger —** To depersonalize, do your best to zip your lip.
- **Observer —** To de personalize, keep a safe distance from your manager by artfully avoiding too much contact. Focus on doing your job with minimal fanfare.
- **Worker Bee —** To depersonalize, understand that your seem ingly kind, appreciative boss is actually afraid of soiling his or her perfect image.
- **Storyteller —** You’ll be able to convey your boss’s criticisms and concerns to others in a diplomatic way. Your boss will appreciate your willingness to deliver any message—no matter what the content.
- **Disarmer —** To depersonalize, con sider this an exercise in using your humor to deliver negative feedback in a way that the staff can hear without being offended.
- **Coaster —** To depersonalize in these situations, notice that this two-faced approach to relating applies to everyone in the Artful Dodger’s life.
- **Low Beamer —** To depersonalize, console yourself with the fact that everyone receives the same treatment.

#### Junior Bosses
- **Nurturer —** To depersonalize, you’ll need to step back and see that you’re work ing for someone who requires a different kind of support than you’re accustomed to offering.
- **Harmonizer —** To depersonalize, realize that your boss is inexperienced and could use interpersonal sup port. Find subtle ways to build bridges with the more senior members of the staff.
- **Star —** If the boss fails to respect your drive or recognize your talents, you can depersonalize by stepping back to discover what your new boss needs from you.
- **Challenger —** Your job is to refrain from overwhelming this talented but inexperienced boss, who needs time to acclimate before adopting your plan. To depersonalize, do your best to back off until this leader comes to you.
- **Observer —** If you feel pressured to adopt your youthful manager’s high-tech ways, you can depersonalize by under standing that the stress you feel today will yield a stronger skill set for tomorrow.
- **Worker Bee —** Worker Bees take a while to adjust to changes that are imposed on them from the outside and may find a Junior boss’s schemes threatening. To depersonalize, try to view your newbie leader’s plans and suggestions as opportunities to stay current.
- **Storyteller —** If you happen to have a youthful employer with little interest in history, you can deperson alize by finding someone else in the company with a sympathetic ear.
- **Disarmer —** You’ll jump on the change bandwagon and learn whatever skills are necessary to do the job. If your boss says or does things that leave you feeling incompetent, depersonalize by chalking his or her behavior up to technological arro gance.
- **Coaster —** To depersonalize, know that whatever the change is, it’s happening to everyone around you. Try to go with the program.
- **Low Beamer —** To depersonalize, try to see this youthful leader as a necessary teacher. The stress of learning new skills is temporary.

#### Former Colleagues
**Universal law:** Promotion permanently changes the balance of power and the friendship-at-work contract.
- **Nurturer —** To depersonalize, give your ex-buddy boss time to adjust to the new posi tion. In time, you can find other ways to be close.
- **Harmonizer —** Harmonizers pride themselves on their relationships and see themselves as valuable assets to their peers. To depersonalize, focus on discovering new ways that you can support and assist this friend who happens to be the boss.
- **Star —** Rather than focus on the unfairness of the situation, depersonalize by examin ing what your former colleague did right to move ahead. Use your com petitive spirit to learn from his or her success.
- **Challenger —** To depersonalize, give your former colleague time and space to learn the ropes of the new job. Stay in touch, but try to keep your demands in check.
- **Observer —** While you may wish your friend well in his or her new position, you’ll also keep a careful eye to see how power changes (and corrupts) this former colleague. To depersonalize, try tempering your judgment by becoming knowledgeable about your former colleague’s current respon sibilities.
- **Worker Bee —** Your ability to keep a cool emotional distance allows you to incorporate the change without feeling slighted.
- **Storyteller —** To depersonalize, understand that the diminished time and attention you’re receiving are not meant as a personal affront. Try to back off and schedule chatting time outside of the workplace.
- **Disarmer —** To depersonalize, realize that your former colleague is simply doing his or her new job.
- **Coaster —** To depersonalize, understand that holding you accountable is part of your former colleague’s new job description.
- **Low Beamer —** If you have trouble with the shift in power, deperson alize by understanding that your colleague’s management status requires a more formal way of interacting with you.

#### Unconscious Discriminators
**Universal law:** The book assumes the boss may be unaware of bias and advises not automatically treating every insensitive act as deliberate malice.
- **Nurturer —** To de personalize from an Unconscious Discriminator, try to hear and see the biased behavior as an indication that your unaware leader lacks informa tion. Focus your efforts on educating this person.
- **Harmonizer —** To depersonalize, realize that whatever the boss is saying or doing, it comes from a place of misinformation, not malice. Try to gently dispel your leader’s distorted perceptions.
- **Star —** To depersonalize, let your natural teaching ability come to your aid. Focus on imparting your wisdom to the uneducated.
- **Challenger —** To depersonalize, try to see your uninformed leader as innocent instead of guilty.
- **Observer —** When Observers realize that their employer has an uncon scious prejudice against them, the first reaction is to lose all respect for that person. The next step may be to label your boss as a loser, a jerk, an idiot, or a moron.While it may be gratifying to fight fire with fire, try to depersonalize.
- **Worker Bee —** Understand that whatever the boss says or does is a reflection of him or her, not you.
- **Storyteller —** Storytellers have less trouble depersonalizing from this kind of situation than do other employees.
- **Disarmer —** To depersonalize, real ize that your leader’s bumbling ways are not meant to harm you.
- **Coaster —** To depersonalize, do your best to see the boss as oblivious, not spiteful.
- **Low Beamer —** You’ll be tempted to go directly to HR and report the behavioral misdemeanor to the office “police.” To depersonalize, see if you can give the boss an opportunity to amend his or her egregious conduct before reporting it to the authorities.

#### Persecutors
**Universal law:** The boss repeatedly singles out targets; being targeted is not proof that the employee caused the pattern.
- **Nurturer —** If you are the target of a persecuting manager, depersonalize by seeing your boss as emotionally handicapped. Continue to express your concern, but keep your distance.
- **Harmonizer —** To depersonalize, realize that your boss is unhappy with you because he or she is unhappy.
- **Star —** To depersonalize, know that there is a way out, and your employer cannot hold you back for long.
- **Challenger —** To depersonalize, you have to practice extreme self-restraint. Understand that there is nothing you can do to change the boss’s mind.
- **Observer —** To depersonalize, see your boss’s destructive behavior as a sign to start looking for a more sane work environment for the long run.
- **Worker Bee —** To depersonal ize, trust that your colleagues and coworkers know your value. Reach out to the people who know you for support and a reality check.
- **Storyteller —** They like to keep conversation to a minimum, unless it’s focused on them. If you do become the object of your boss’s disdain, depersonalize by leaning on your colleagues for support.
- **Disarmer —** To depersonalize, know that you aren’t the first target and you won’t be the last. Try not to take anything this person says or does to heart as you polish up your résumé.
- **Coaster —** To depersonalize, trust that your colleagues see what’s happening.
- **Low Beamer —** Low Beamers cannot understand why someone would single them out and treat them badly for no reason. To depersonalize, you’ll have to do your best to leave the boss’s negativity at the office—until you can secure a position elsewhere.

### 4.6 The Fear Fake-Out Guide: twenty fear interventions

The book asks the reader to **“pick your pain”**: choose the fear causing the most current distress, identify how it is producing self-defeating protection, and practice a corrective behavior. The twenty fear rules are:

#### 1. Fear of Appearing cold or uncaring
- **Related framing:** Also: being seen as difficult/demanding.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Over-serving, overworking, covering for the boss, becoming too involved in private affairs.
- **Corrective action:** Keep a healthy distance; stay focused on work; listen neutrally to personal issues without taking responsibility for solving them; practice saying you cannot help with matters outside your role.

#### 2. Fear of Appearing foolish or incompetent
- **Related framing:** Also: not holding it together / looking stupid.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Covering up what you do not know or have not finished; lying to avoid looking bad.
- **Corrective action:** Admit gaps early and pair truth with a recovery plan or delivery commitment.

#### 3. Fear of Asking questions
- **Related framing:** Assumption: “I should already know.”
- **What strengthens the fear:** Guessing, making assumptions, executing without clarification.
- **Corrective action:** Ask clarifying questions even when you think you understand; frame them as a desire to get the work right.

#### 4. Fear of Being criticized
- **Related framing:** Also: being judged.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Hiding mistakes, omitting information, hoping problems disappear, delaying bad news.
- **Corrective action:** Surface bad news and possible problems early; alert the boss while correction is still possible.

#### 5. Fear of Being devalued
- **Related framing:** Fear that asking exposes you to being told you are not good enough.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Waiting passively for recognition, raises, promotions, help, or feedback; resenting louder self-promoters.
- **Corrective action:** Prepare and rehearse explicit requests; practice reporting accomplishments rather than waiting to be noticed.

#### 6. Fear of Being dismissed
- **Related framing:** Also: being marginalized.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Forcefully inserting ideas early, appearing overly certain, defending opinions as if recognition must be compelled.
- **Corrective action:** Pace visibility; listen first; offer ideas as suggestions/options; trust that value can be demonstrated over time.

#### 7. Fear of Being found lacking
- **Related framing:** Also: not doing enough / not carrying my weight.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Saying yes to too much, overpromising, overloading, then underdelivering.
- **Corrective action:** Finish existing responsibilities, pause before volunteering, state workload constraints, ask for help or more time; underpromise and overdeliver.

#### 8. Fear of Being held back
- **Related framing:** Also: invisible tasks / underutilization.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Aggressive self-promotion, going over boss’s head, threatening to leave, pushing visibility in a way that prompts further censorship.
- **Corrective action:** Use patience and impulse control; make a discreet visibility plan; showcase skills in safer internal venues and external professional groups.

#### 9. Fear of Being labeled a troublemaker
- **Related framing:** Often linked to nonconformity and a drive to challenge the status quo.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Refusing rules reflexively, redesigning before learning the system, presenting dissent in ways that provoke defense.
- **Corrective action:** Practice following some rules/plans; vet novel ideas with a trusted person; choose timing and delivery so disagreement is easier to receive.

#### 10. Fear of Being misunderstood
- **Related framing:** Strong need for others to understand the full story.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Overexplaining, excessive detail, repeating, defending through longer stories.
- **Corrective action:** Use concise facts and lists; watch the listener; ask whether more detail is useful before adding it.

#### 11. Fear of Being pressured to perform
- **Related framing:** Also: being caught off guard / failing in high-pressure situations.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Saying yes then avoiding or procrastinating, concealing that you need help.
- **Corrective action:** Break work into small steps, ask for examples/clarity, and enlist experienced help early.

#### 12. Fear of Being rejected
- **Related framing:** Fear causes “reject first.”
- **What strengthens the fear:** Disqualifying yourself from promotions, projects, leadership, or opportunities before anyone can reject you.
- **Corrective action:** Build rejection resilience by staying in the process and pursuing opportunities rather than opting out preemptively.

#### 13. Fear of Being ridiculed
- **Related framing:** Also: being ostracized.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Flying under the radar, staying silent, avoiding participation, keeping ideas to yourself.
- **Corrective action:** Get on the radar in small ways; speak up in meetings, participate, and ask the boss how best to present an idea.

#### 14. Fear of Confrontation
- **Related framing:** Also: dealing with anger / being yelled at.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Ignoring tension, hoping conflict disappears, procrastinating asks until pressure is high.
- **Corrective action:** Address problems while they are still small; alert the boss early; prepare and rehearse difficult requests/conversations.

#### 15. Fear of Disappointing
- **Related framing:** Need to please makes “no” difficult.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Overwork, overextension, failure to prioritize/delegate, eventual dropped balls.
- **Corrective action:** Use the “no-action remedy”: stop volunteering reflexively, let others step in, calibrate promises to actual capacity.

#### 16. Fear of Having to defend myself
- **Related framing:** Discomfort with being confronted about mistakes.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Hiding or ignoring risks until the boss discovers them and demands an explanation.
- **Corrective action:** Overcommunicate anticipated problems; say early that you want to alert the boss before the issue becomes larger.

#### 17. Fear of Making mistakes
- **Related framing:** Also: unclear assignments / unfamiliar work.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Perfectionistic waiting, need for explicit instructions, very slow completion.
- **Corrective action:** Reframe mistakes as evidence of participation; take small risks; submit first drafts/rough outlines and invite correction.

#### 18. Fear of Making the wrong decision
- **Related framing:** Search for a single correct choice.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Procrastinating, polling many people, overresearching until deadlines/opportunities pass.
- **Corrective action:** Develop an internal decision maker through small decisions; use one trusted decisive colleague for large choices.

#### 19. Fear of Not being heard
- **Related framing:** Strong desire for ideas/experiences to register.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Repeating, restating, raising volume or adding detail until the listener tunes out.
- **Corrective action:** Less is more: package the message for the listener, state it once, back off, then confirm later if needed.

#### 20. Fear of Not being respected
- **Related framing:** Also: talked down to / not taken seriously.
- **What strengthens the fear:** Sulking, brooding, withholding, bad-mouthing, hostility, refusing instructions, shutting down.
- **Corrective action:** Give the benefit of the doubt long enough to clarify; calmly ask what the boss meant before concluding disrespect.

### 4.7 Deal strategies by employee authority profile

Chapters 8–12 organize the final intervention by employee profile. Every boss type receives an **approach**, an **in-the-moment response**, and a **behind-the-scenes strategy**. This is the book’s most granular practical layer.

#### Nurturer
**Overall dealing principle:** The main problem is over-caregiving: the Nurturer can become responsible for the boss’s mood, health, workload, and private life. Deal by setting limits, keeping empathy neutral, and getting appreciation from other sources.
##### With Chronic Critic
- **Approach:** Resilience
- **In the moment:** Now that you understand your boss must always find fault, don’t try to defend yourself against the Chronic Critic’s correc tions. Smile, take notes, and walk away without taking the negative feedback to heart.
- **Behind the scenes:** Instead of working overtime to avoid making mis takes, be prepared to receive ongoing criticism. Look for your boss’s posi tive attributes and chalk up the rest to insecurity.
##### With Rule Changer
- **Approach:** Flexibility
- **In the moment:** When your boss cancels a plan, reverses a decision, or contradicts a previous statement, remind yourself that, with this person, change is the rule, not the exception.
- **Behind the scenes:** Make it your business to check in regularly with a Rule Changer (more often than you think you should). Daily updates may help you stay abreast of the always-changing plans.
##### With Yeller
- **Approach:** Shield
- **In the moment:** When the Yeller lets loose, tell yourself, “I am not this person’s problem.” Picture your boss as a two-year-old having a temper tantrum. Do not absorb his or her pain.
- **Behind the scenes:** Try to get transferred or find another job ASAP.
##### With Underminer
- **Approach:** Meet head-on
- **In the moment:** When your boss once again gives you responsibility then hands the authority over to someone else, take a deep breath and tell yourself, “This is not about my competence, it’s about my boss’s inability to delegate properly.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Confront the boss in private and see if he or she is willing to own up to the event. The next time your undermining boss of fers you an assignment, look for discrepancies—see whom else your boss may be entrusting with similar or conflicting responsibilities.
##### With “I’m Always Right”
- **Approach:** Tolerance
- **In the moment:** No matter how tempting it may be to introduce another way of seeing an issue with this boss, stick to quiet agreement or “I see what you mean.”
- **Behind the scenes:** If there’s a cause you care about or a decision you’d like the boss to make, approach him or her with a statement like, “I think this might fit with your priorities.”
##### With “You Threaten Me”
- **Approach:** Compassion
- **In the moment:** Do what comes naturally to you—play down your suc cesses and ascribe credit to the boss.
- **Behind the scenes:** Coach your colleagues regarding the boss’s insecurity in the area of public recognition and help them minimize friction over their accomplishments.
##### With Grandiose
- **Approach:** Patience
- **In the moment:** You’re very good at giving this kind of boss the audience that he or she needs. Sit back and watch the show.
- **Behind the scenes:** Schedule and take regular vacations. Find ways to restore your energy through exercise, sleep, and a healthy diet.
##### With Control Freak
- **Approach:** Appeasement
- **In the moment:** Do your best to follow your boss’s explicit instructions. If he or she wants a specific kind of report, chart, or agenda, serve it up.
- **Behind the scenes:** Understand that earning your boss’s trust will hap pen faster if you’re able to fulfill your actual job requirements. Focus on delivering what the boss wants.
##### With Love-Struck
- **Approach:** Restraint
- **In the moment:** When you feel your boss’s amorous feelings intensifying, stay neutral. Respond to any compliment with a cool thank-you and move on to the business at hand.
- **Behind the scenes:** This may be tough for you, but your best strategy is to pull back from your usual nurturing role. That means you will refrain from bringing food, candy, or other comforting items to the boss. Do not linger to hear about your manager’s personal experiences. Stick to the tasks of your actual job.
##### With Calculating Confidant
- **Approach:** Caution
- **In the moment:** Anytime your boss attempts to glean personal informa tion about you or a colleague, do not bite the bait. Respond to friendly inquiries with short responses like, “We’ll see.” “Same as usual.” “I’m out of the loop.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Keep your distance from this kind of leader. When you do have to meet, keep all aspects of your personal life private.
##### With Tell-All
- **Approach:** Setting limits
- **In the moment:** As the Tell-All dumps more and more of his or her personal problems in your lap, try not to assume the job of solving them. Instead, practice the Zen of not solving anything. Say, “I see.” “That’s rough.” “I’m overloaded, but let me find someone else to help you out.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Practice setting limits with the boss. Whenever your manager starts confessing and confiding in you, try cutting the “meeting” short. Prepare to be called cold and uncaring. That means you’re on the right track.
##### With Liar, Liar
- **Approach:** Discernment
- **In the moment:** Whenever you sense that your boss may be bending the truth to suit his or her needs, take the statement or promise with a grain of salt.
- **Behind the scenes:** Decide if you can live with the particular form of deception that your boss practices. If not, find greener pastures.
##### With Sacred Cow
- **Approach:** Allegiance
- **In the moment:** If you sense that your risk-averse boss is unwilling to move forward on a business initiative or decision, back off.
- **Behind the scenes:** Package your ideas so that they either support one of your Sacred Cow boss’s cherished goals or they enhance his or her reputation.
##### With Checked Out
- **Approach:** Fairness
- **In the moment:** If a problem falls on your desk that the boss should be attending to, document the facts of the situation and record the amount of time it takes for you to address this issue.
- **Behind the scenes:** Keep a careful record of the fallout that you’re car rying because of your boss’s absentee behavior. Meet with your manager, show him or her the facts, and ask how this situation will be remedied.
##### With Spineless
- **Approach:** Take the lead
- **In the moment:** When it’s time for your manager to request your raise, offer any documentation (significant results, notable accomplishments, or added responsibilities) that will make it easier.
- **Behind the scenes:** Build relationships with key players in the company so that you can get things done without having to solicit your reluctant leader’s support.
##### With Artful Dodger
- **Approach:** Diplomacy
- **In the moment:** The next time your boss sends you off to deliver a tough message to a colleague or coworker, reach outside of the work place to a friend or associate who can help you script the message in a diplomatic way.
- **Behind the scenes:** Should the cumulative effect of bearing bad news cause you ongoing distress, search for a new boss.
##### With Junior
- **Approach:** Adaptability
- **In the moment:** Whenever you feel slightly shunned by your Junior boss, step back and follow his or her cues.
- **Behind the scenes:** Carefully study your youthful leader to see what his or her priorities are and how you can be of greatest service.
##### With Former Colleague
- **Approach:** Patience
- **In the moment:** In those moments when your former colleague sud denly pulls rank, it may hurt your feelings, but do your best just to follow orders.
- **Behind the scenes:** The loss you feel is real. Over time, the two of you can redefine the friendship beyond the office. For the short term, focus your efforts on expanding your social network and reviving other friendships until the dust settles from this promotion.
##### With Unconscious Discriminator
- **Approach:** Benefit of the doubt
- **In the moment:** If your boss unintentionally says or does something that reveals a clear prejudice, it will hurt; do your best not to react.
- **Behind the scenes:** After you’ve cooled down, make sure to approach your manager privately to clear up the situation. Start the conversation with, “I know it wasn’t your intention, but when you said _____[fill in the blank], it hurt my feelings.”
##### With Persecutor
- **Approach:** Damage control
- **In the moment:** Should this brand of boss target you with unfair accusa tions, snap judgments, or an air of disdain, do your best to view him or her as an emotionally handicapped person.
- **Behind the scenes:** Once you’ve become the target, get out of this job as soon as possible.

#### Harmonizer
**Overall dealing principle:** The main problem is over-accommodation and conflict avoidance. Deal by preserving relationship skill while saying difficult things earlier, building alliances, and not equating disagreement with failure.
##### With Chronic Critic
- **Approach:** Endurance
- **In the moment:** The next time your boss begins to correct or complain about your work, instead of trying to defend yourself, respond with, “Thank you for letting me know.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Take concrete steps to find a better work environ ment: use your relationship skills to forge professional alliances that can lead to a new job with a less critical leader.
##### With Rule Changer
- **Approach:** Finesse
- **In the moment:** Whenever your boss lays out a plan or schedules a meet ing, know that it is tentative. Don’t invest too much hope in any one outcome.
- **Behind the scenes:** Work with your colleagues to keep projects moving forward and to coordinate schedules.
##### With Yeller
- **Approach:** Resilience
- **In the moment:** The next time your boss erupts, watch it from a fresh perspective. Focus in on the physical effects of the anger—notice the red face, popping veins, and shaking body. Keep breathing deeply and gently, and do not defend yourself. Let the storm pass. When the Yeller is finished, simply say, “I heard you.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Do whatever it takes to purge your boss’s toxic energy from your system. Update your résumé and start networking.
##### With Underminer
- **Approach:** Empowerment
- **In the moment:** As you discover that your boss has once again overridden a decision that he or she asked you to make, try saying, “Well, I’m disap pointed, but I can work with that.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Take action to forge relationships beyond your boss’s reach, where you’ll feel more respected and be given more responsibility. In the long run, you may want to get out.
##### With “I’m Always Right”
- **Approach:** Strategic maneuvering
- **In the moment:** The next time you’re tempted to set the boss straight, take a deep breath, calm down, and back off. As you take notes regard ing what your boss is saying, tell yourself that you can revisit this topic at another time.
- **Behind the scenes:** See your boss as a puzzle. Focus on discerning the right moments to go to this manager and offer your point of view.
##### With “You Threaten Me”
- **Approach:** Modesty
- **In the moment:** Should you win an industry award or receive recogni tion from a high-level executive within the company, address your boss’s jealous pride by saying something like, “I couldn’t have done this without your support.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Assist any colleagues who become the object of “You Threaten Me”’s envy by showing them how to acknowledge this boss in a way that alleviates his or her fears.
##### With Grandiose
- **Approach:** Avoidance
- **In the moment:** As you listen to your boss tell the same success story for the tenth time, extract yourself by drawing on any workplace urgencies that you can think of. Try saying, “I’ve got to return an important call.” “I’ll be right back; there’s a client matter I need to attend to.” “I need to get that package out.” Trust us. Your Grandiose boss will be okay.
- **Behind the scenes:** Do whatever it takes to keep meetings, phone calls, and other interactions with this boss at a minimum. To keep him or her informed, send regular updates via e-mail.
##### With Control Freak
- **Approach:** Compliance
- **In the moment:** When your boss zeros in and insists that you follow his or her exact instructions, fight the urge to resist. Instead, even though the direction may feel patronizing, try to listen intently, take copious notes, and thank your boss for the input.
- **Behind the scenes:** Make it your mission to win this person’s trust.
##### With Love-Struck
- **Approach:** Straight talk
- **In the moment:** If the tension of feeling your employer’s amorous inten tions becomes too great, try saying, “I value our work relationship too much to jeopardize it.” Your boss may be cool for a while, but eventually he or she will recover.
- **Behind the scenes:** Be willing to let the boss be disappointed while you focus on carrying out the responsibilities of your job.
##### With Calculating Confidant
- **Approach:** Discretion
- **In the moment:** Notice when your meetings with the boss turn into chat ting sessions. As the Calculating Confidant digs for dirt, say things like, “I wouldn’t know about that” or “There’s not much to tell.” Take any personal information your boss reveals about others with a pound (not a grain) of salt.
- **Behind the scenes:** Carefully start looking for other opportunities with more forthright and honest bosses. Even if it’s a lateral move, you’ll like the working conditions better.
##### With Tell-All
- **Approach:** Let go and let others help
- **In the moment:** When your boss confesses a personal secret, then asks you to cover for him or her at work, don’t rush to rescue him or her. Instead, say, “I feel terrible about your situation, but I can’t take on any more.” If the Tell-All then pleads with you, take the task and look for someone else to help you complete it.
- **Behind the scenes:** Begin to explore other employment options where the person in charge does not require emotional babysitting.
##### With Liar, Liar
- **Approach:** Resignation or flight
- **In the moment:** If you catch your boss making an empty promise, deceiv ing a colleague, or falsifying documents, stay cool in the moment— despite the fact that you may want to confront.
- **Behind the scenes:** You’ll have to decide whether you can live with the deception and disruption that the Liar, Liar causes. If not, plan your exit strategy.
##### With Sacred Cow
- **Approach:** Slow and steady
- **In the moment:** The next time your Sacred Cow boss blocks one of your best ideas, don’t get frustrated or indignant. Say, “I appreciate your feed back. Let me rethink this.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Break your idea or project into small baby steps that you can introduce to your risk-averse leader slowly, over time. Focus on showing this employer how your project can enhance his or her reputation.
##### With Checked Out
- **Approach:** Take control
- **In the moment:** When you realize that your boss is not emotionally or functionally present, take that as your cue to take charge.
- **Behind the scenes:** Organize the people within your department. Cre ate a workplace team so that you can make decisions and get the work done.
##### With Spineless
- **Approach:** Work around the problem
- **In the moment:** The next time you see your boss waffle, back down, or fail to stand up for a member of the staff, take it as a signal that this per son cannot go to bat on anyone’s behalf.
- **Behind the scenes:** Advocate for yourself and others by building connec tions with people in higher, more influential positions.
##### With Artful Dodger
- **Approach:** Push back
- **In the moment:** If your boss assigns you the task of delivering harsh feed back because he or she doesn’t want to be perceived as the bad guy, try saying, “I think this message should come from you.”
- **Behind the scenes:** the wear and tear of acting as this employer’s bad cop is too much for you to handle. Spend your time out of the office looking for a better work opportunity with a more forthright boss.
##### With Junior
- **Approach:** Adaptability
- **In the moment:** At those moments when this boss discounts your experi ence, it may sting a bit, but you can probably see his or her point of view.
- **Behind the scenes:** Help yourself and others get up to speed in terms of learning the technology and adapting to your Junior boss’s way of doing business.
##### With Former Colleague
- **Approach:** Understanding
- **In the moment:** Should your former colleague exclude you from an im portant meeting or load you down with additional assignments, you may feel slighted. Try to put yourself in your friend’s shoes. Understand that this person is making decisions based on a different set of responsibilities and information.
- **Behind the scenes:** Maintain the relationship outside of work. Find out what your former colleague’s new reality is and how you can be supportive.
##### With Unconscious Discriminator
- **Approach:** Thoughtful instruction
- **In the moment:** If you catch your boss saying or doing something that clearly indicates a bias against your gender, race, appearance, or religious practices, you can register the information and tell yourself that you’ll find a way to educate your boss on this issue.
- **Behind the scenes:** Gather your thoughts and collect any data you need so that you can sit down with your boss, explain the inadvertent mistake that he or she made, and dispel the myth.
##### With Persecutor
- **Approach:** Escape
- **In the moment:** As your boss attacks or disparages your behavior, do not attempt to defend yourself. Listen carefully, repeat the boss’s complaints so that he or she knows you’re paying attention, and respond with, “You may be right.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Work as fast as possible to find a better job with a civil boss.

#### Star
**Overall dealing principle:** The main problem is fear of being held back or invisible. Deal by pacing ambition, creating alternative visibility, and choosing when to push, when to share credit, and when to leave.
##### With Chronic Critic
- **Approach:** Learning opportunity
- **In the moment:** Whenever this brand of boss corners you to tell you what you’ve done wrong, simply say, “Thank you very much. I really appreciate knowing how I can be better.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Once you understand that this person can see only the details—not the bigger picture—start looking for your next career move.
##### With Rule Changer
- **Approach:** Move on
- **In the moment:** The next time your boss changes course on a plan of action that you’ve been working toward, take a deep breath. Assess what change has just taken place so that you can figure out your role in the new direction.
- **Behind the scenes:** Expand your professional network and look for a leader who is more decisive and growth-oriented.
##### With Yeller
- **Approach:** Cool objectivity
- **In the moment:** At the moment that your boss starts yelling, listen very carefully to what he or she is saying and respond with, “I understand. I understand.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Repair the problem that triggered your short-fused boss’s tirade. At the same time, put out feelers for a manager who is less volatile and more respectful.
##### With Underminer
- **Approach:** Direct communication
- **In the moment:** As soon as you discover that your manager has overrid den a decision that he or she asked you to make, confront this individual immediately. “I’m confused. You gave me the authority to make a deci sion, then you overruled it. Which way would you like to go? Are you making this decision or am I?”
- **Behind the scenes:** Spend time proving your trustworthiness. Build trust by showing this leader your plans and the thinking behind your decision making.
##### With “I’m Always Right”
- **Approach:** Agreement
- **In the moment:** If you’re caught in a situation where your boss insists that you’re wrong and he or she is right, it’s best to say, “You’re right.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Observe the coworkers who are able to agree with the boss and still push their ideas, viewpoints, and projects forward. Follow their lead.
##### With “You Threaten Me”
- **Approach:** Tactful exit
- **In the moment:** It is not going to feel good when you experience this en vious leader diminishing your success. Your best protection is to register the jealousy, but don’t react.
- **Behind the scenes:** Now that you know your boss is competing with you for the limelight, get busy networking for your next position.
##### With Grandiose
- **Approach:** Entertainment
- **In the moment:** As you sit in a meeting where the Grandiose boss chews your ear off, enjoy the show.
- **Behind the scenes:** As long as your boss allows you to learn and grow on the job, you’re okay. Once you hit a ceiling in terms of opportunities for advancement, move on.
##### With Control Freak
- **Approach:** Gradual success
- **In the moment:** If you find yourself bristling as you wait for your boss’s approval on every action, slow down and do it the boss’s way.
- **Behind the scenes:** Study your leader’s detail-oriented style of completing tasks and mirror it. Focus on winning this person’s trust by being an A+ student.
##### With Love-Struck
- **Approach:** Professionalism
- **In the moment:** If your employer suggests that the two of you could have a meaningful connection, say, “I have a policy not to mix business with pleasure.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Avoid your boss’s advances by keeping your social calendar full.
##### With Calculating Confidant
- **Approach:** Censorship
- **In the moment:** If your boss seems extremely interested in the intimate, personal details of your life, be suspicious. Do your best to limit the amount of personal information that you share with this individual.
- **Behind the scenes:** Don’t get seduced into going to this boss’s house for dinner or getting into any situation where you might talk too much about coworkers or yourself. Talk to friends and family, not the boss.
##### With Tell-All
- **Approach:** Don’t rescue
- **In the moment:** The next time you’re tempted to rescue this boss by doing portions of his or her job, stop. Instead of taking it on, suggest other people who can shoulder the added responsibility.
- **Behind the scenes:** This kind of boss is likely to dump all of his or her responsibility on your trusty shoulders. As long as this association serves your career goals, stick with it. But the minute you start to feel like an underpaid servant, move on.
##### With Liar, Liar
- **Approach:** Eyes wide open
- **In the moment:** When you catch your boss lying, confront in a humorous way. You might say, “Okay, that’s one version of what happened. What’s the real story? What else do I need to know?” Keep pressing until you feel as if you’ve uncovered 80 percent of the truth.
- **Behind the scenes:** As long as you feel that your career is moving for ward, you can work under this deceptive leader’s rule. For the long run, however, decide whether being associated with this person really en hances your professional reputation.
##### With Sacred Cow
- **Approach:** Build alliances
- **In the moment:** The next time this person rejects one of your best busi ness ideas without even considering it, resist the urge to strangle him or her. Instead, say, “I understand.” Then retreat.
- **Behind the scenes:** Quietly build alliances with the people who can actu ally help you realize your ideas. At the same time, keep going to the boss, give this chief credit for any headway you make, and watch for opportuni ties to push your agenda gently forward.
##### With Checked Out
- **Approach:** Conscious leadership
- **In the moment:** If your boss seems unresponsive or absent for important decisions, step in and take the reins. Keep your Checked Out manager informed regarding any actions you take.
- **Behind the scenes:** Rally your coworkers to act as a team while your leader is temporarily preoccupied. Delegate responsibilities and meet regularly to make sure that each person is carrying his or her weight.
##### With Spineless
- **Approach:** Intervention
- **In the moment:** If you need your boss to make a decision, settle a dispute, or reinforce a company policy, but you fear that he or she can’t do it, offer your help. Say, “Would you like me to handle that for you?” Then step up and handle it.
- **Behind the scenes:** Once you realize that acting as your boss’s spine puts you in a precarious position with your peers (they resent you), it’s best to move on.
##### With Artful Dodger
- **Approach:** Awareness of manipulation
- **In the moment:** When your Artful Dodger boss comes to you (once again) to deliver a negative message to an employee, a vendor, or a customer, try saying, “They need to hear this message from you.” Even if your Teflon leader insists that you act as the messenger, he or she will know that you’re on to the game.
- **Behind the scenes:** As a Star, you’re more interested in creating results than acting as a manager’s hit man. Start looking for your next (positive) career move.
##### With Junior
- **Approach:** Stay current
- **In the moment:** Should your significantly younger boss introduce tech nology or business processes that are beyond your comprehension, tell the truth about your experience. You might say something like, “It may be my age, but I don’t see how to work this.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Take classes, hire a consultant, or find a willing col league who’ll help you get up to speed.
##### With Former Colleague
- **Approach:** Wait and see
- **In the moment:** The first time your colleague-turned-boss addresses you in an authoritative manner, it may be very uncomfortable for you. Be professional in the moment by carrying out your new leader’s orders without complaint.
- **Behind the scenes:** Give yourself time to process the disappointment and envy you feel in response to this change in the balance of power. Figure out a way to retain the friendship while you decide whether you want to stay in this job.
##### With Unconscious Discriminator
- **Approach:** Patience
- **In the moment:** If your boss says something that is tasteless and reveals a prejudice against your gender, race, ethnicity, appearance, or religious orientation, you may be tempted to speak up. Take a more professional tack by addressing the incident at another time in a more private setting.
- **Behind the scenes:** Decide whether it’s worth saying anything to your Unconscious Discriminator. Would this authority figure be receptive to the feedback? If so, begin the conversation with an “I” statement: “I felt hurt by your comment” or “I was embarrassed when you asked if I’d put on a few pounds.” It’s important not to blame.
##### With Persecutor
- **Approach:** Quick departure
- **In the moment:** When the Persecutor is attacking your work or your behavior, resist the urge to attack back. Instead, respond to the assaults with, “I hear you,” and wait for the conversation to end.
- **Behind the scenes:** Put all your energy into networking and finding another job.

#### Challenger
**Overall dealing principle:** The main problem is turning valid dissent into a hierarchy contest. Deal by preserving candor while changing timing, dose, and delivery; know when the organization cannot absorb the challenge.
##### With Chronic Critic
- **Approach:** Let go
- **In the moment:** When this boss starts to criticize your work, you’ll instinc tively want to defend yourself. Don’t do it. Zip your lip, listen, and say, “Okay.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Practice letting go. Write down your boss’s complaints and review them when you are in a relaxed state. If his or her comments are legitimate, see how you can incorporate them. Let the rest go.
##### With Rule Changer
- **Approach:** Adaptability
- **In the moment:** As your manager informs you that yesterday’s plans have been changed (again), use your creativity to adjust to the new reality and move forward.
- **Behind the scenes:** Although you may find this kind of boss annoying, you can relate to the resistance to being tied down. Make it a point to stay in close contact with him or her. Check in regularly so that you can stay abreast of the latest developments.
##### With Yeller
- **Approach:** Caution
- **In the moment:** If your boss starts yelling over something you’ve done, refrain from defending yourself—it will only fuel the fire.
- **Behind the scenes:** After you’ve been lambasted, assess your situation. If you’ve become the office scapegoat, plan your exit strategy. If you work for an Equal Opportunity Yeller, stay as long as the work is ap pealing to you.
##### With Underminer
- **Approach:** Keep moving forward
- **In the moment:** Should your boss assign the same project to you and a coworker or otherwise undermine your authority, don’t let the news deter you. Instead, use your competitive spirit to barrel through.
- **Behind the scenes:** Keep showing this uncertain leader that you can de liver on any assignment he or she dishes out. At the same time, be aware that your boss can’t resist interfering after he or she gives anyone a new responsibility.
##### With “I’m Always Right”
- **Approach:** Surrender
- **In the moment:** As your boss asserts that his or her ideas, opinions, and perspective are correct, you’ll want to challenge that information on the spot. Fight the urge to enter verbal combat with your boss and wait for the meeting to end.
- **Behind the scenes:** If you can withstand a situation where you must agree with your boss before you can introduce any alternative information, you may be able to tolerate this position. Otherwise, read the writing on the wall and get out.
##### With “You Threaten Me”
- **Approach:** Share credit
- **In the moment:** Should you be recognized by a high-ranking executive or a professional association for your work, go directly to your boss and say, “This recognition is yours as much as it is mine.”
- **Behind the scenes:** Keep building goodwill with your easily threatened boss by drawing positive attention to his or her initiatives and successes.
##### With Grandiose
- **Approach:** Vacate
- **In the moment:** You’ll be tempted to challenge this leader at every turn. Best not to question the accuracy of a Grandiose boss’s statements or smirk as this big personality brags about past accomplishments.
- **Behind the scenes:** Why would you stay in this kind of situation? Circulate your résumé.
##### With Control Freak
- **Approach:** Compliance
- **In the moment:** If your boss hovers over you, refusing to let you make decisions, pull back, take a breath, and follow instructions.
- **Behind the scenes:** Like it or not, your only job is to play by your boss’s rules. As you build trust, this tightly wound leader may be able to loosen the reins of control.
##### With Love-Struck
- **Approach:** Caution
- **In the moment:** While it may feel great to be your boss’s object of affec tion, tread carefully in this situation. Enjoy the attention, but avoid mak ing an irreversible decision.
- **Behind the scenes:** Weigh the consequences of engaging in a workplace romance. If you can handle any outcome—including getting fired— proceed with caution.
##### With Calculating Confidant
- **Approach:** Prevention
- **In the moment:** Even though you usually have a reliable radar for phony friendliness, a truly skilled Calculating Confidant might fool you. If your boss shows an intense curiosity about your personal life or that of your colleagues, be cautious. Keep your answers as vague and as friendly as you can.
- **Behind the scenes:** Stay on your boss’s good side by doing your job with out generating drama or conflict. You don’t need to give this authority figure any material to hang you with.
##### With Tell-All
- **Approach:** Restraint
- **In the moment:** Resist the urge to offer your boss solutions or remedies to his or her multitude of personal problems. Listen and say, “Uh-huh.” Then go about doing your job.
- **Behind the scenes:** Realize that your boss is not going to change and won’t be open to your solutions. Start putting out feelers for future em ployment. See if you can find a boss who is open to your creative ways.
##### With Liar, Liar
- **Approach:** Personal integrity
- **In the moment:** When you realize that your boss just lied to you, your first impulse will be to challenge this person. As difficult as it may be, resist the urge to go to combat over the transgression.
- **Behind the scenes:** Consider whether you’re willing to work with some one who habitually changes and reframes the truth to suit his or her needs. Decide how offensive your boss’s deceptive ways are to you. If you can’t live with them, search for a more forthright employer.
##### With Sacred Cow
- **Approach:** Self-control
- **In the moment:** The next time your stubborn supervisor rejects one of your ideas or proposals, resist the urge to retaliate. You’ll lose. Return to your desk and regroup.
- **Behind the scenes:** Either decide how you can work around this person without soliciting his or her wrath or get out.
##### With Checked Out
- **Approach:** Embrace leadership
- **In the moment:** When you realize that your boss is missing from an im portant meeting, decision, or event, resist acting out. Instead, try taking on a leadership position where you coordinate efforts with your col leagues to get things done.
- **Behind the scenes:** Understand that your boss will come back. Taking care of business while your employer is Checked Out will enhance your résumé and career.
##### With Spineless
- **Approach:** Resignation
- **In the moment:** When your boss refuses to resolve a conflict that you’re having with a coworker or customer, zip your lip. Instead of lashing out, back off and let the moment pass.
- **Behind the scenes:** If you cannot tolerate working for a leader who is in capable of confrontation or taking a stand, start circulating your résumé.
##### With Artful Dodger
- **Approach:** Endurance
- **In the moment:** The minute this boss asks you to do some version of his or her dirty work, you’ll want to rebel against it. If you want to keep your job, refrain from challenging your manager’s orders.
- **Behind the scenes:** Understand that acting as your boss’s bad cop is part of your job. Decide whether you’re willing to do it.
##### With Junior
- **Approach:** Pace yourself
- **In the moment:** As you meet with your new boss, do your best not to over whelm this youthful leader with your ideas, opinions, and plans. Instead, focus on listening to and implementing Junior’s goals and priorities.
- **Behind the scenes:** You may be eager to introduce your manager to your innovative solutions to many workplace problems. Leave your boss want ing more by offering your ideas one suggestion at a time.
##### With Former Colleague
- **Approach:** Temperance
- **In the moment:** The first time your fomer colleague attempts to manage you, you may be tempted to laugh it off. Instead, do your best to listen carefully and follow this person’s lead.
- **Behind the scenes:** Take some time to adjust to the new balance of power. If you can, approach your friend with a sincere interest in finding out about his or her new responsibilities. Ask how you can help.
##### With Unconscious Discriminator
- **Approach:** Tolerance
- **In the moment:** The minute you detect that your manager unconsciously discriminates against some aspect of your identity, take a deep breath. Relax. Resist the temptation to strangle this person.
- **Behind the scenes:** Go to a trusted colleague or friend and run the sce nario by him or her. See how the listener interprets what happened and what suggestions he or she may have for addressing the boss.
##### With Persecutor
- **Approach:** Immediate exit
- **In the moment:** As your boss attacks or disparages you in some way, don’t even listen to his or her comments. Try to watch this person as if he or she is a horror-film monster. Do your best not to absorb the venom.
- **Behind the scenes:** Get out. This is not a safe workplace for you.

## 5. Diagnostic rules, heuristics, and decision criteria

- **Detect before acting —** If you cannot name the recurring boss pattern, your current coping tactic, and the phase of the relationship, do not assume that your first impulse is strategic.
- **Use recurrence, not a single incident, to type the boss —** The boss categories describe repeated patterns. A one-off mistake, bad day, or awkward interaction is weaker evidence than a stable cross-situation pattern.
- **Check early warning signs during the honeymoon —** Long waits, biting treatment of junior staff, early oversharing, persistent interruption, visible intoxication, or other anomalies may matter even when you strongly want the new relationship to work.
- **Internal alarm is data, not proof —** A sinking feeling is worth investigating, but the book also warns that personal Boss Baggage can amplify ambiguous events. Separate observable facts from triggered meaning.
- **If you are working harder to recover the boss’s favor, test whether you are in Restart/Try Harder —** Additional effort is not automatically productive. Ask whether it is actually changing the boss relationship or merely increasing dependency and exhaustion.
- **If you are repeatedly replaying interactions, shift from explanation-seeking to fact mapping —** Use the Quick Reality Check: facts, boss’s part, your part, options.
- **If distress has become physical, treat this as escalation —** The book treats persistent sleep, pain, fatigue, appetite, or other physical changes as evidence that the relationship is no longer just an irritating communication problem.
- **If burnout makes exit feel impossible, rebuild capacity before assuming there are no options —** Restoring energy and confidence is meant to make realistic career choices possible again.
- **If the boss cannot meet an expectation, adjust the expectation; if a true need remains, source it elsewhere —** Do not use “acceptance” to pretend that important needs vanished.
- **If a fear repeatedly produces the feared result, change the protective behavior first —** Examples: fear of criticism → hiding mistakes → harsher criticism; fear of rejection → self-disqualification → no opportunity; fear of not being heard → repetition → listener tunes out.
- **If you need to communicate bad news, earlier is usually safer —** Several fear interventions converge on prompt alerts rather than concealment, delay, or hope that the problem disappears.
- **If you think you are being disrespected, clarify before retaliating —** The book repeatedly favors a calm clarification question over sulking, bad-mouthing, or immediate hostile interpretation.
- **If you want more visibility, build it deliberately rather than explosively —** Use committees, task forces, professional groups, presentations, documented accomplishments, or external associations instead of bypassing the boss impulsively.
- **If the boss is a Rule Changer or Underminer, increase confirmation frequency —** Check/reconfirm current priorities, assignments, owners, and decisions rather than relying on yesterday’s verbal understanding.
- **If the boss is a Control Freak, reduce surprises and earn trust in increments —** Keep the boss informed, follow explicit process for a period, and bring proposed solutions rather than merely problems.
- **If the boss is Always Right, agreement can be a sequencing tactic —** Acknowledge the boss’s view before introducing alternatives; do not confuse sequencing with intellectual surrender.
- **If the boss is Grandiose, account for ego-maintenance overhead —** Brief acknowledgment may be the price of getting to business; protect energy and keep other venues for being heard.
- **If the boss is a Yeller, do not argue inside the emotional storm —** Regulate breathing, reduce verbal engagement, and wait until arousal is lower before clarification or correction.
- **If the boss crosses personal boundaries, limit disclosure and create external support —** Do not make the boss your primary confidant; the book treats personal information as potentially risky in power-imbalanced relationships.
- **If the situation is a Persecutor pattern, stop assuming perfect performance will fix it —** Document, seek experienced support, and consider exit; multiple chapters regard this as one of the least repairable matches.
- **If dishonesty, discrimination, sexual attention, or other serious conduct is involved, interpersonal technique is not the only layer —** The book includes HR, professional advice, documentation, and exit in some profiles. Its 2009 interpersonal framework is not presented as a complete legal or organizational-procedure guide.

## 6. Exercises, drills, and practices

### Detect worksheet
Check which of the ten coping tactics you use, then identify which of the eight toxic-relationship phases best describes the current situation. Use this as the entry point to the Four D’s.

### Twenty-boss checklist
Review all twenty difficult boss types and check every behavior that meaningfully fits; bosses can show more than one pattern.

### Acceptance list
Write each recurring boss reality as “I accept the fact that…”; read the list aloud; note resistance; add your own emotional facts; reread regularly.

### Personal recharging plan
Choose a small set of activities across three targets: restore energy, repair emotional state, rebuild confidence. Include both at-work and after-work actions.

### Quick Reality Check
In a triggering moment write: What is happening? What are the facts? What is the boss’s part? What is my part? What are my options?

### Daily success log
Record concrete successes every day, including small acts of follow-through and larger accomplishments, to counter a boss-distorted self-image.

### Boss Baggage Assessment
Answer yes/no items for all ten employee profiles, rank top three profiles, then identify top expectations, needs, and fears.

### Current-boss suitcase
List the expectations the current boss fails to fulfill, needs the boss cannot meet, and fears the boss triggers.

### Three A’s plan
For several unmet expectations, write an adjustment; for unmet needs, specify alternate sources; for fears, choose corrective actions.

### Pick Your Pain / Fear Fake-Out
Select the most active fear, identify behavior that aggravates it, practice the corresponding corrective action until the fear has less behavioral control.

### Mentor/reality-testing practice
Use a mentor, coach, counselor, HR expert, or experienced colleague to check distorted interpretations and map career options.

### Visibility practice
For fears of marginalization, rejection, or ridicule, take small visible actions: speak once in a meeting, join a committee, present a concise idea, or contribute to a professional association.

### Boundary practice
For over-caregiving or line-crossing bosses, practice neutral empathy plus refusal to take ownership of private problems; reduce personal disclosure.

### Early-warning communication
When a problem or mistake appears, practice a short alert before it becomes a crisis. The drill is to tolerate the discomfort of disclosure rather than delay.

### Question-asking exposure
Practice asking clarifying questions even when you fear looking uninformed. Treat questions as quality control rather than incompetence.

### Decision exposure
For fear of making the wrong choice, make small decisions more quickly and live with the outcome; use one trusted advisor rather than a large opinion poll.

### No-action practice
For fear of disappointing or being found lacking, deliberately refrain from volunteering for extra work and observe that the system can continue without your rescue.

### Time-out and physiological reset
When arousal rises, step away, breathe, change environment, cool down, and return only when you can choose rather than react.

## 7. Failure modes, warnings, and anti-patterns

- **Trying to change the boss before changing your forecast —** Keeps the employee in a power struggle with traits the boss has repeatedly demonstrated.
- **Confusing acceptance with approval —** Creates resistance to the whole method. The book’s acceptance is descriptive recognition, not moral endorsement.
- **Using overwork to restore the honeymoon —** Restart/Try Harder can look virtuous while silently increasing depletion and dependence.
- **Treating boss criticism as a complete account of reality —** Especially harmful with Chronic Critics, Yellers, Persecutors, and insecure bosses.
- **Letting good work “speak for itself” when the environment requires communication —** Worker Bees and Observers can become invisible, then interpret exclusion as proof they are not valued.
- **Overexplaining under threat —** Storytellers and anyone who fears being misunderstood/not heard can make the boss less receptive by adding more words.
- **Arguing with a Yeller during an eruption —** The book expects escalation rather than clarification while the boss is emotionally flooded.
- **Giving a Calculating Confidant sensitive personal information —** Creates leverage and increases vulnerability.
- **Becoming the Tell-All’s rescuer —** Converts another person’s recurring drama into your workload and emotional responsibility.
- **Assuming a Rule Changer’s current plan is permanent —** Creates repeated surprise and wasted work; plans must be reconfirmed.
- **Assuming delegated authority from an Underminer is stable —** Without confirmation and role clarity, the assignment may be duplicated or overridden.
- **Surprising a Control Freak —** Triggers the exact anxiety that drives tighter control; proactive updates are usually more strategic.
- **Trying to publicly outshine a threatened boss —** May trigger blocking, devaluation, impossible assignments, or exclusion.
- **Expecting a Spineless boss to protect you at the critical moment —** Leaves you without a self-protection plan when conflict arrives.
- **Treating an Artful Dodger’s friendliness as full candor —** Important feedback may travel through intermediaries instead.
- **Trying indefinitely to win over a Persecutor —** Can deepen rumination and self-doubt in a pattern the book considers largely independent of employee performance.
- **Isolating under stress —** Removes perspective, social support, and alternative sources of confidence.
- **Using gossip as emotional regulation —** Temporary relief can create a second reputation problem for the employee.
- **Self-medicating rather than recharging —** Short-term numbness can compound health, mood, and performance problems.
- **Waiting until burnout to consider career options —** By then the employee may lack energy and confidence to search; earlier recharging and network-building preserve optionality.

## 8. Important distinctions and taxonomies

### Acceptance vs. approval
Acceptance = recognizing a recurring fact so you can plan around it. Approval = judging the behavior acceptable or desirable. The book requires the former, not the latter.

### Detachment vs. indifference
Detachment = staying in the relationship without being consumed by it. Indifference/coldness = withdrawal of care or engagement.

### Expectation vs. need
An expectation can be revised when a particular boss cannot fulfill it; a need still requires fulfillment, but perhaps from another source.

### Fear vs. fact
A fear is an activated threat prediction. It may be reasonable, exaggerated, or ambiguous; the book asks the employee to test facts and avoid letting fear automatically choose behavior.

### Boss behavior vs. employee trigger
The boss may genuinely behave poorly; the employee’s personal history determines which meanings and fears become especially active.

### Self-medication vs. self-empowerment
Self-medication reduces distress immediately through numbing/escape; empowerment restores capacity, perspective, and confidence.

### Chronic Critic vs. Persecutor
Chronic Critic finds fault broadly and habitually; Persecutor disproportionately targets a specific employee as scapegoat.

### Underminer vs. Control Freak
Underminer appears to delegate and then retracts/duplicates authority; Control Freak usually keeps approval and oversight from the outset.

### Always Right vs. Grandiose
Always Right organizes interaction around infallibility and winning arguments; Grandiose organizes it around ego, attention, status, and stories of greatness.

### You Threaten Me vs. Control Freak
Threatened boss is especially reactive to the employee’s independent recognition; Control Freak is especially reactive to loss of informational/decision control.

### Calculating Confidant vs. Tell-All
Calculating Confidant draws out your personal information for leverage; Tell-All pushes the boss’s personal information onto you to elicit caretaking.

### Spineless vs. Artful Dodger
Spineless boss cannot reliably take a stand; Artful Dodger can act, but routes unpleasant action through other people to protect image.

### Checked Out vs. Sacred Cow
Checked Out boss is absent/preoccupied and fails to lead; Sacred Cow is often present but risk-averse, protected, and invested in maintaining the status quo.

### Former Colleague problem vs. hostile boss problem
The discomfort can arise from a legitimate role/power transition even when the new boss is not malicious.

### In-the-moment tactic vs. behind-the-scenes strategy
The first contains emotional damage now; the second manages role design, alliances, documentation, career trajectory, escalation, and exit.

### Employee profile vs. personality diagnosis
Boss Baggage Profiles are pragmatic authority-orientation categories created by the authors, not presented as validated clinical personality diagnoses.

## 9. Evidence and notable claims

### 9.1 Epistemic posture of the book

The book is primarily a practitioner framework built from the authors’ psychotherapy/business-consulting experience, workplace observations, examples, and self-assessment heuristics. It presents numerous scenarios and rules of thumb but does not provide formal validation studies for the Four D’s, the twenty boss types, the ten Boss Baggage Profiles, or the 20×10 tactical matrices.

### 9.2 Claims the book presents as empirical or research-based

- Authors state that their research indicates the quality of the employee–boss relationship is the single greatest determinant of an individual’s happiness at work.
- Authors repeatedly claim that difficult boss relationships can spill into sleep, home life, social life, emotional well-being, and physical symptoms.
- Authors treat chronic workplace stress as capable of contributing to headaches, neck/back pain, digestive problems, fatigue, sleep disturbance, blood-pressure changes, and other physical strain.

**Evidence status within the book:** these claims are asserted from the authors’ research/experience, but the text does not, in the main practical chapters, provide a detailed literature review or formal effect estimates. Treat them as book claims rather than externally verified conclusions in this extraction.

### 9.3 Theoretical / explanatory claims

- Boss behaviors are treated as relatively stable patterns with motives such as perfectionism, insecurity, fear of weakness, fear of losing control, emotional hunger, risk aversion, or conflict avoidance.
- Boss Baggage is treated as historically learned from formative authority relationships and carried into adult work relationships.
- Fear-driven protective behaviors can become self-confirming because they evoke negative reactions that resemble the feared outcome.
- Acceptance and depersonalization are claimed to lower emotional reactivity by changing prediction and meaning rather than changing the boss.
- Confidence is treated as a prerequisite for seeing career options and therefore as part of practical career mobility.

### 9.4 Author heuristics / normative recommendations

- Accept recurring boss patterns instead of insisting they disappear.
- Do not bad-mouth, retaliate, sulk, shut out, or chronically avoid as primary strategies.
- Build self-care and support before attempting hard conversations.
- Find alternative sources for recognition, direction, belonging, challenge, or mentorship when the boss cannot provide them.
- Practice corrective actions that contradict fear-based habits: ask questions, state workload limits, report bad news early, speak up, make small decisions, or request clarification.
- Document/escalate/exit when the situation is chronically destructive or cannot be made workable.

### 9.5 Areas where the book’s evidence status is unclear or its framing is dated

- Some physiological language about “toxins,” immune restoration, and specific mind/body remedies is presented practically rather than with detailed supporting evidence in the text.
- The “Unconscious Discriminator” section reflects a 2009 interpersonal-management framing; it often emphasizes ignorance, re-education, and adaptation. The book does not function as a comprehensive guide to contemporary discrimination law, employer duty, or formal reporting procedures.
- The Love-Struck material is also primarily framed as a boundary-management problem. The book does not replace organizational policy, safeguarding, or legal guidance in situations involving harassment, coercion, or retaliation.
- The profile system and boss taxonomy are useful as author-created heuristics but are not presented as psychometrically validated instruments.

## 10. Distinctive contributions of this book

### The Four D’s as a full intervention sequence
The book does not stop at “manage up.” It moves from diagnosis to self-regulation to personal-history interpretation to tactical relationship management.

### Two-sided classification: boss type × employee authority style
The 20 boss behaviors are crossed with 10 employee Boss Baggage Profiles, producing a highly granular claim that the same boss requires different depersonalization and dealing tactics depending on the employee’s habitual relationship to authority.

### The eight-phase deterioration model
The Honeymoon → Alarm → Try Harder → Disappointment → Rehashing → Anger → Physical pain → Burnout progression gives a process model rather than a static “bad boss” label.

### The ten coping tactics as false-control strategies
The book explicitly names revenge fantasies, gloating, gossip, confrontation, retaliation, and shutout alongside more familiar rumination/avoidance/self-doubt, showing how legitimate distress can produce behavior that damages the employee’s own position.

### Acceptance as forecasting rather than forgiveness
The practical value of acceptance is that it changes what the employee predicts and therefore what he or she plans for.

### Boss Baggage as a diagnostic bridge
The framework explains “Why does this boss affect me so much?” through expectations, needs, and fears rather than only through boss pathology.

### Expectation / need / fear separation
This is one of the book’s most reusable distinctions: expectations can be adjusted, needs need alternative fulfillment, fears need behavioral disconfirmation.

### The Fear Fake-Out Guide
Twenty common workplace fears are paired with the behavior that unintentionally reinforces them and a concrete corrective action.

### Recharging as part of management strategy
Exercise, sleep, social support, hobbies, professional help, small wins, and mentoring are treated as prerequisites for effective workplace behavior rather than as generic wellness add-ons.

### Explicit in-the-moment vs. behind-the-scenes tactics
The Deal chapters distinguish how to respond during the boss behavior from what career/support/documentation work to do away from the moment.

### Permission to stop solving the unsolvable
Certain boss types—especially persistent persecution or severe dishonesty—are treated as situations where the plan may need to become documentation, escalation, or exit rather than further relationship optimization.

## 11. Integrated conceptual map

### 11.1 System architecture

The book can be modeled as four interacting layers:

1. **Boss pattern layer** — Twenty recurring forms of difficult authority behavior alter predictability, safety, autonomy, recognition, boundaries, or fairness.
2. **Employee authority layer** — Ten Boss Baggage Profiles contain expectations, needs, fears, and learned default strategies for dealing with power.
3. **Dynamic relationship layer** — Boss behavior and employee triggers interact over time, producing the eight deterioration phases and the ten maladaptive coping tactics.
4. **Intervention layer** — Four D’s + Three A’s + profile-specific tactics target different links in the system.

### 11.2 Main reinforcing loop

**Boss acts in a difficult way**
→ employee interprets the event through Boss Baggage
→ expectations are violated / needs frustrated / fears activated
→ employee uses an automatic coping tactic
→ communication, visibility, trust, boundaries, or self-care deteriorate
→ boss reacts negatively or the employee experiences another adverse outcome
→ the original fear feels confirmed
→ rumination and physiological arousal increase
→ the relationship advances toward anger, physical symptoms, and burnout.

### 11.3 Corrective loop

**Detect pattern and phase**
→ **Detach:** accept recurring boss behavior and recharge
→ **Depersonalize:** distinguish boss pattern from self-worth; map expectations/needs/fears
→ **Deal:** adjust impossible expectations, source needs elsewhere, choose one fear corrective action
→ use profile/boss-specific in-the-moment response
→ build behind-the-scenes support, documentation, visibility, or exit options
→ emotional capture falls and perceived options rise
→ the boss may or may not improve, but the employee becomes less controlled by the boss’s behavior.

### 11.4 Signs that the authors consider the system integrated

- Counterproductive coping tactics are no longer the default.
- The boss can be accepted as the person he or she repeatedly demonstrates being.
- The boss no longer produces the same level of adverse emotional or physical reaction.
- The employee knows how to restore personal power and capacity.
- Unrealistic expectations have been adjusted, real needs are being met through workable channels, and fears are less behaviorally controlling.

The authors caution that insight can create immediate relief but sustained implementation usually takes time; they suggest **at least about three months of diligent practice** to fully implement the tools.

## 12. Atomic knowledge inventory

This section is intentionally redundant: it functions as a high-recall retrieval index for later synthesis.

- **Framework —** Four D’s = Detect → Detach → Depersonalize → Deal.
- **Framework —** Three A’s inside Deal = Adjust expectations → Address unmet needs → Alleviate fears.
- **Principle —** The employee cannot reliably control or redesign the boss; practical leverage begins with the employee’s own forecasts, resources, meanings, and actions.
- **Distinction —** Acceptance is recognition of reality, not approval or condoning.
- **Distinction —** Detachment is emotional distance while remaining engaged, not coldness or indifference.
- **Distinction —** Expectations can be adjusted; needs require alternative fulfillment; fears require corrective behavior and reality testing.
- **Mechanism —** Fear can become self-confirming: hidden fear → automatic protective behavior → negative reaction → fear realized.
- **Heuristic —** When activated, run the Quick Reality Check: what happened, what are the facts, what is the boss’s part, what is my part, what are my options?
- **Exercise —** Acceptance list: prefix recurring boss realities with “I accept the fact that…” and review until the forecast becomes emotionally usable.
- **Exercise —** Boss Baggage Assessment: score ten profiles, rank top three, then select top expectations, needs, and fears.
- **Exercise —** Pick Your Pain: choose the most active fear and practice the Fear Fake-Out corrective action.
- **Practice —** Recharging plan must target energy, emotional state, and confidence—not only the boss interaction.
- **Warning —** Self-medication and numbing can reduce stress briefly while worsening energy, mood, health, and agency later.
- **Warning —** Chronic gossip, retaliation, sulking, shutout, and frontal attack can turn a legitimate boss problem into an employee reputation/performance problem.
- **Coping tactic —** Obsession — Mentally replaying mixed messages and trying to solve the boss as a puzzle. Cost: Sleeplessness, distraction, lateness, forgetfulness, reduced performance, and energy drain.
- **Coping tactic —** Avoidance — Reducing contact, small talk, face-to-face communication, ideas, and visibility to stay out of the line of fire. Cost: Can look uncommitted, uncooperative, or disengaged and can itself generate poor performance feedback.
- **Coping tactic —** Self-doubt — Assuming the boss’s behavior proves something is wrong with you; old negative beliefs become louder. Cost: Erodes self-worth, confidence, and performance.
- **Coping tactic —** Sulking — Publicly displaying hurt, resentment, withdrawal, sighing, pouting, or terse replies. Cost: Others may see the behavior as childish or petty, obscuring the legitimate problem.
- **Coping tactic —** Wishing for the boss’s demise — Revenge fantasies in which the boss is removed, humiliated, punished, or harmed. Cost: Consumes mental space and produces an emotional hangover rather than practical control.
- **Coping tactic —** Gloating over the boss’s failure — Seeking vindication when the boss makes a mistake or looks bad. Cost: Makes the employee look petty and can damage the same team/company on which the employee depends.
- **Coping tactic —** Bad-mouthing — Trying to injure the boss’s reputation through complaints, disparagement, or disclosure of private information. Cost: Damages the employee’s reputation for discretion and professionalism.
- **Coping tactic —** Confrontation — A frontal accusation or emotional attack intended to force acknowledgement or fairness. Cost: Invites misunderstanding and counterattack; the power asymmetry favors the boss.
- **Coping tactic —** Retaliation / acting out — Trying to even the score by exposing, embarrassing, withholding, bypassing, or sabotaging the boss. Cost: Can burn bridges, trigger discipline, damage reputation, and lead to termination.
- **Coping tactic —** Shutting out — Silent treatment, refusal of eye contact, third-party communication, or minimal contact. Cost: Looks emotionally immature/unreasonable and can lead to formal discipline or termination.
- **Relationship phase —** Honeymoon Period — New job/promotion/transfer/manager; both parties are on best behavior and early warning signs are rationalized or ignored.
- **Relationship phase —** Internal Alarm — A surprising incident produces a bodily “uh-oh” response; the employee senses a problem but often explains it away.
- **Relationship phase —** Restart, Try Harder — Employee works harder, becomes more agreeable, stays out of the way, or otherwise tries to restore the original goodwill.
- **Relationship phase —** Disappointment — Pattern becomes undeniable; the boss is not who the employee hoped. Hurt, anger, confusion, avoidance, and negative talk increase.
- **Relationship phase —** Rehearsing and Rehashing — The employee repeatedly reviews meetings, emails, and conversations to reconstruct what went wrong and seeks validation from others.
- **Relationship phase —** Anger and Blame — Victimization and resentment dominate; sulking, gloating, bad-mouthing, retaliation, and revenge fantasies become more likely.
- **Relationship phase —** Emotional Pain Turns Physical — Chronic strain appears as sleep problems, headaches, neck/back pain, digestive problems, TMJ, fatigue, blood pressure changes, or weight/appetite changes (examples given by the book).
- **Relationship phase —** Burnout — Chronic mental, physical, and emotional exhaustion; low self-esteem, depression/anger, unhealthy coping, isolation, suspicious thinking, and a trapped feeling make even job searching harder.
- **Boss type —** Chronic Critic — Compulsively finds flaws in work, behavior, presentation, or results; success is quickly followed by another correction.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Chronic Critic — The boss’s need to criticize is not a reliable measure of your worth or competence.
- **Boss type —** Rule Changer — Treats decisions, schedules, priorities, and policies as provisional and changes them without adequate notice.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Rule Changer — Treat plans as provisional; consistency is not this boss’s operating mode.
- **Boss type —** Yeller — Uses loud, angry, demeaning eruptions at predictable and unpredictable moments.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Yeller — The rage is a recurring boss pattern; do not treat an outburst as a precise diagnostic statement about you.
- **Boss type —** Underminer — Delegates responsibility, then overrides it, duplicates it, reassigns it, or fails to communicate the authority that was supposedly given.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Underminer — The boss’s trust and control problems apply broadly; retraction of authority is not proof of your incompetence.
- **Boss type —** “I’m Always Right” — Needs ideas, opinions, decisions, and policies to be treated as correct; disagreement becomes a contest that must be won.
- **Boss-specific rule —** “I’m Always Right” — The boss equates being wrong with being weak and cannot tolerate appearing weak.
- **Boss type —** “You Threaten Me” — Encourages employee performance until the employee receives independent recognition, then minimizes, blocks, or punishes the employee’s visibility.
- **Boss-specific rule —** “You Threaten Me” — This boss has difficulty tolerating other people’s success when it competes with his or her own status.
- **Boss type —** Grandiose — Centers conversation and recognition on personal expertise, accomplishments, connections, and stories; listening is weak.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Grandiose — The grandiosity functions to reinforce self-image; under the bluster the book posits fear of being found lacking.
- **Boss type —** Control Freak — Requires visibility into nearly everything, approves decisions personally, and dislikes surprises.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Control Freak — The controlling behavior is driven by perfectionism and fear of losing control, not simply by your performance.
- **Boss type —** Love-Struck — Boss seeks emotional or sexual closeness through flirtation, special attention, private contact, or preferential companionship.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Love-Struck — The boss’s feelings are not obligations; they do not have to be acted upon.
- **Boss type —** Calculating Confidant — Creates apparent intimacy, draws out private information, then uses that information as professional leverage or judgment.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Calculating Confidant — The closeness serves information advantage; keep sensitive personal material outside the relationship.
- **Boss type —** Tell-All — Overshares personal dramas and recruits staff as listeners, supporters, and sometimes practical substitutes for the boss’s own work.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Tell-All — You can care without becoming the boss’s emotional hand-holder or operational substitute.
- **Boss type —** Liar, Liar — Makes false promises, alters facts, denies prior statements, or engages in more serious unethical deception.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Liar, Liar — Treat important claims as needing verification rather than assuming ordinary reliability.
- **Boss type —** Sacred Cow — Protected or promoted through tenure, loyalty, relationships, or family connections; risk-averse and invested in existing systems.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Sacred Cow — Stop organizing your strategy around the hope that the protected boss will disappear.
- **Boss type —** Checked Out — Physically or psychologically absent because of personal crisis, addiction, disengagement, or chronic preoccupation.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Checked Out — Operate as if you are temporarily in a leaderless environment and create structure where you can.
- **Boss type —** Spineless — Avoids taking positions that could provoke disapproval; listens, nods, but does not enforce, defend, or intervene.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Spineless — Do not base your safety plan on the expectation that this boss will suddenly become confrontational on your behalf.
- **Boss type —** Artful Dodger — Protects a friendly public image by avoiding unpleasant feedback directly and using intermediaries to deliver bad news or enforce decisions.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Artful Dodger — Do not assume pleasant face-to-face feedback equals the boss’s complete view.
- **Boss type —** Junior — Younger or less experienced manager with strong current/technical skills who may discount seasoned employees’ experience.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Junior — Different experience profiles can coexist; depersonalization requires respecting what the younger boss genuinely brings.
- **Boss type —** Former Colleague — A former peer/friend becomes your manager; information, authority, accountability, and social boundaries change.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Former Colleague — Treat the change as a role transition, not evidence that the former colleague has necessarily betrayed you.
- **Boss type —** Unconscious Discriminator — Book’s term for a boss who expresses bias or treats an employee unfairly without recognizing the prejudice involved.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Unconscious Discriminator — The book advises distinguishing ignorance from deliberate malice while still recognizing the behavior as unfair.
- **Boss type —** Persecutor — Selects an employee as a recurring target/scapegoat for hostile scrutiny, exclusion, or blame that is disproportionate to performance.
- **Boss-specific rule —** Persecutor — Do not make endless self-correction the strategy; the boss may rotate targets independent of performance.
- **Employee authority profile —** Nurturer — Emotionally responsible for others; caring, helpful, service-oriented; notices needs and volunteers.
- **Profile vulnerability —** Nurturer — Overfunctioning, blurred boundaries, rescuing, and saying yes when overloaded.
- **Employee authority profile —** Harmonizer — Warm team player and bridge-builder; adapts to others and tries to lower interpersonal tension.
- **Profile vulnerability —** Harmonizer — Conflict avoidance, over-accommodation, and indirect communication.
- **Employee authority profile —** Star — Visible, ambitious, ownership-seeking performer; wants growth, leadership, and recognition.
- **Profile vulnerability —** Star — Pushing too hard for visibility, ownership, or acknowledgement when feeling constrained.
- **Employee authority profile —** Challenger — Independent, competitive, fairness-oriented questioner of authority; speaks uncomfortable truths and challenges the status quo.
- **Profile vulnerability —** Challenger — Turning disagreements into power struggles or overcommunicating dissent.
- **Employee authority profile —** Observer — Quiet, skeptical, process-oriented evaluator who needs time to understand before acting.
- **Profile vulnerability —** Observer — Withholding, delaying, or disappearing from view while waiting for enough certainty.
- **Employee authority profile —** Worker Bee — Quiet expert and diligent backstage worker; wants clear work, autonomy, and minimal interpersonal conflict.
- **Profile vulnerability —** Worker Bee — Avoiding visibility, difficult conversations, questions, and self-advocacy.
- **Employee authority profile —** Storyteller — Connects through detailed conversation and narrative; wants access, clarity, listening, and acknowledgment.
- **Profile vulnerability —** Storyteller — Overexplaining, repeating, and trying to talk through moments when brevity or silence would work better.
- **Employee authority profile —** Disarmer — Independent, efficient communicator who uses humor to reduce tension; egalitarian and sensitive to condescension.
- **Profile vulnerability —** Disarmer — Masking upset with humor, then withdrawing or becoming sharp when respect feels absent.
- **Employee authority profile —** Coaster — Relaxed, relationship-oriented employee who sees work as one part of life and avoids unnecessary pressure.
- **Profile vulnerability —** Coaster — Underperformance, avoiding responsibility, and relying on charm when demands rise.

- **Distinctive contribution —** Two-sided classification: boss type × employee authority style — The 20 boss behaviors are crossed with 10 employee Boss Baggage Profiles, producing a highly granular claim that the same boss requires different depersonalization and dealing tactics depending on the employee’s habitual relationship to authority.

- **Distinctive contribution —** The ten coping tactics as false-control strategies — The book explicitly names revenge fantasies, gloating, gossip, confrontation, retaliation, and shutout alongside more familiar rumination/avoidance/self-doubt, showing how legitimate distress can produce behavior that damages the employee’s own position.

- **Distinctive contribution —** Boss Baggage as a diagnostic bridge — The framework explains “Why does this boss affect me so much?” through expectations, needs, and fears rather than only through boss pathology.
- **Distinctive contribution —** Expectation / need / fear separation — This is one of the book’s most reusable distinctions: expectations can be adjusted, needs need alternative fulfillment, fears need behavioral disconfirmation.
