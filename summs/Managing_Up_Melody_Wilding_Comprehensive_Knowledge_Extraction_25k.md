# Managing Up — Comprehensive Knowledge Extraction

**Author:** Melody Wilding, LMSW  
**Primary subject:** Managing upward relationships, organizational influence, self-advocacy, boundaries, feedback, networking, visibility, advancement, compensation, and career exits  
**Extraction purpose:** High-recall extraction for multi-source synthesis  
**Source edition:** Currency, 2025

---

## 1. Book in one model

Melody Wilding defines **managing up** as strategically navigating relationships with people who hold more positional power than you, especially your boss. Her model rejects the older idea that managing up means flattering authority, blindly pleasing a manager, absorbing their incompetence, or behaving like an obedient order-taker.

The book’s central argument is:

> You cannot control your boss, the hierarchy, or every organizational constraint, but you can substantially increase your agency by becoming more deliberate about how you gather information, adapt communication, create value, protect your capacity, build influence, advocate for yourself, and decide when to leave.

The model can be reconstructed as a ten-stage system:

**Understand what matters → understand how people operate → take ownership → protect your capacity → speak truth upward → diversify your relationships → make your value visible → engineer advancement → negotiate compensation → exit deliberately when the system no longer serves you.**

These stages are organized as ten recurring “conversations”:

1. **The Alignment Conversation** — understand goals, priorities, politics, and what success means.
2. **The Styles Conversation** — decode how your boss communicates, decides, relates, and reacts.
3. **The Ownership Conversation** — identify valuable problems and responsibly take initiative.
4. **The Boundaries Conversation** — say no, renegotiate workload, and protect time without destroying credibility.
5. **The Feedback Conversation** — give constructive criticism upward and influence behavior above you.
6. **The Networking Conversation** — build social capital beyond your immediate manager.
7. **The Visibility Conversation** — make your work and impact memorable without becoming obnoxiously self-promotional.
8. **The Advancement Conversation** — align career ambitions with organizational needs and build a case for greater scope.
9. **The Money Conversation** — negotiate compensation based on value, market evidence, and future ROI.
10. **The Quitting Conversation** — decide whether to leave and transition without needlessly burning bridges.

The book is cumulative. Each conversation increases leverage for later ones:

**Alignment → better work choices → stronger results → ownership → greater credibility → easier boundary-setting and feedback → stronger network → more visibility → better advancement options → more compensation leverage → more genuine freedom to stay or leave.**

Wilding’s broader psychological thesis is that workplace helplessness is often maintained by a self-reinforcing loop:

**Unclear power dynamics → overthinking / guessing → passive coping → weak information → more dependence on the boss → lower confidence → still less direct advocacy.**

Her alternative loop is:

**Curiosity → better information → small assertive actions → improved outcomes → more credibility and self-trust → greater willingness to advocate → more agency.**

A second major loop concerns perception:

**Good work that is invisible → low recognition → fewer opportunities → less organizational power.**

The book’s remedy is not empty self-promotion but:

**Strategically visible, promotable work + narrative framing + social proof → stronger reputation → greater access and sponsorship → larger opportunities.**

A third loop concerns boundaries:

**Automatic yes → overload → lower-quality work / exhaustion → weaker judgment → more reactive work → still less capacity to advocate.**

The interruption is:

**Clarify the request → assess leverage → negotiate trade-offs → preserve high-value capacity.**

A fourth loop concerns career development:

**Wait to be noticed → boss remains unaware of aspirations → no opportunity appears → employee assumes advancement is unavailable.**

The interruption is:

**Seed aspirations → clarify readiness criteria → build evidence → secure advocates → present a business case → repeatedly revisit.**

The book is therefore best understood not as a collection of scripts, but as an **agency architecture for unequal-power relationships**. The scripts are implementation tools for the deeper system.

## 2. Core worldview and foundational principles

### 2.1 Managing up is not servitude

Old model:
- please the boss;
- keep your head down;
- never disagree;
- anticipate needs without clarification;
- compensate for bad leadership;
- flatter authority.

Wilding’s modern model:
- understand power realistically;
- adapt without erasing yourself;
- advocate for needs;
- build influence;
- challenge when necessary;
- leave when conditions are irredeemable.

The target is **partnership without pretending hierarchy does not exist**.

---

### 2.2 Positional power matters, but it is not the only kind of power

Formal rank shapes:
- who approves;
- who allocates resources;
- who evaluates;
- who controls access;
- who can block or accelerate advancement.

But informal power also comes from:
- specialized expertise;
- tenure;
- relationships;
- credibility;
- access to information;
- visibility;
- being relied on;
- control of scarce resources;
- social proof;
- reputation.

Managing up means understanding both.

---

### 2.3 Your boss is a person under pressure

Wilding repeatedly encourages an investigative mindset.

Instead of:
> “Why is my boss doing this to me?”

Ask:
- What pressures are they under?
- What does their boss care about?
- What are they afraid of?
- What incentives shape their behavior?
- What style do they prefer?
- What are they trying to protect?
- What is invisible to me?

This reduces personalization and creates more strategic responses.

---

### 2.4 Agency comes from replacing mind-reading with information

A recurring failure mode is assuming:
- the boss dislikes you;
- your project is unimportant;
- you are underperforming;
- promotion is impossible;
- a short email signals anger;
- a canceled meeting means rejection.

The book repeatedly substitutes **questions, evidence, and experiments** for interpretation.

Mechanism:

**Ambiguity → story-making → anxiety.  
Clarification → information → action.**

---

### 2.5 Flex, don’t fawn

This phrase captures the book’s stance on adaptation.

**Flexing**
- adjust medium;
- pace;
- level of detail;
- tone;
- timing;
- framing.

**Fawning**
- abandon your own needs;
- become submissive;
- flatter;
- agree automatically;
- tolerate harmful behavior.

The goal is **adaptive assertiveness**.

---

### 2.6 Managing up is an investment in your own work experience

The author explicitly reframes the common objection:

> “Why should I have to manage my boss?”

The practical answer:
Because managing up can improve:
- autonomy;
- clarity;
- workload;
- influence;
- relationships;
- recognition;
- compensation;
- career direction.

It is therefore self-advocacy, not unpaid caretaking of management.

## 3. Chapter 1 — The Alignment Conversation

**Purpose:** Stop guessing what matters and connect your work to organizational priorities.

### 3.1 Core problem: misalignment

Misalignment occurs when:
- you optimize work the boss does not value;
- you misunderstand success criteria;
- strategic priorities change without your awareness;
- your boss and their boss want different things;
- your role is disconnected from the organization’s core goals.

Consequences:
- wasted effort;
- rework;
- second-guessing;
- weak visibility;
- expendability;
- missed advancement.

Wilding’s own layoff is used as the foundational case: positive performance reviews did not protect a role built around a project lacking senior-level strategic support.

### 3.2 Definition

**Alignment conversation:** ongoing inquiry into how your work fits the bigger picture and whether you and your manager agree on what success means.

### 3.3 Cast a one-year vision

Before aligning outwardly, define what you want.

Exercise:
Imagine your ideal workday one year from now.

Prompts:
- Which key relationship has improved?
- Which skill have you mastered?
- What project are you leading?
- Which organizational change have you prepared for?
- What accomplishments would you tell a mentor about?
- What work feels energizing?

Purpose:
- distinguish your direction from your boss’s;
- identify overlaps;
- identify gaps;
- create a filter for later ownership, visibility, and advancement choices.

### 3.4 Get inside the boss’s head

Questions to identify goals and pressures:
- What goals matter most right now?
- Why now?
- What metrics does your boss discuss with you?
- What does your boss’s boss care about?
- What keeps you up at night?
- What trends matter?

Questions to define success:
- How does my work contribute?
- How am I measured?
- What does good performance look like?
- What does great performance look like?
- What would you like me to accomplish in three, six, or twelve months?
- What assumptions about this role should I challenge?

Questions to clarify priorities:
- What do you wish you had more time for?
- What would make the next ninety days feel successful?
- Here is how I’m spending time—does it match your priorities?
- Which of these tasks should receive more or less focus?

### 3.5 Dig deeper rather than accepting vague answers

If the boss says:
> “Growth.”

Ask:
- How are we measuring growth?
- Which benchmark matters?
- And what else?

Wilding cautions that **“why?” can sometimes feel accusatory** when a boss is already defensive. Alternatives:
- “What’s driving this?”
- “What would progress look like?”
- “What else?”

### 3.6 Mirroring

Borrowed from Chris Voss:
repeat the last few meaningful words.

Boss:
> “Customer service scores are slipping.”

You:
> “Customer service scores?”

Purpose:
- prompt elaboration;
- show listening;
- uncover root cause;
- reveal hidden priorities.

### 3.7 Skip-level meetings

Managing up should extend beyond the direct manager.

Benefits:
- broader strategic context;
- increased visibility;
- early awareness of inconsistencies;
- stronger senior-level relationship.

Risk:
The direct boss may perceive bypassing.

Mitigation:
- be transparent;
- explain developmental purpose;
- seek endorsement;
- emphasize complementing rather than circumventing.

Questions for a skip-level:
- What are the organization’s strategic priorities?
- How should our team support them?
- Which changes are coming?
- How is the team evaluated?
- What capabilities should we develop?
- What common alignment failures do teams make?

### 3.8 Get it in writing

Use short meeting share-outs containing:
- main takeaways;
- actions;
- owner;
- deadline.

Benefits:
- reduce interpretation drift;
- create accountability;
- expose misalignment early;
- make you a trusted information node.

### 3.9 Power map

The book includes a 2×2 influence-interest matrix.

#### Quadrant 1 — High influence, high interest
**Manage closely.**
- involve frequently;
- communicate detail;
- surface risks early;
- seek active input.

#### Quadrant 2 — High influence, low interest
**Keep satisfied.**
- concise updates;
- contact at decision points;
- frame around their interests.

#### Quadrant 3 — Low influence, high interest
**Keep informed.**
- lightweight updates;
- use expertise;
- invite contribution where useful.

#### Quadrant 4 — Low influence, low interest
**Monitor.**
- preserve relationships;
- invest little active energy.

Update the map roughly twice yearly or when organizational power shifts.

### 3.10 Control-freak boss

Tactics:
- show rough drafts early;
- reinforce their final authority;
- pose ideas as questions;
- overshare status;
- preempt criticism;
- ask directly for positive feedback too.

Underlying mechanism:

**Visibility + reassurance → reduced uncertainty → less intrusive control.**

### 3.11 Constantly shifting priorities

Tactics:
- diagnose cause;
- distinguish real direction from passing thought;
- use a parking lot for repeated ideas;
- ask how new request fits current goals;
- name trade-offs;
- quantify cost;
- identify shared objectives.

A useful heuristic:
For highly idea-generative bosses, wait until an idea appears repeatedly before treating it as direction unless explicitly told otherwise.

### 3.12 Severe misalignment

Wilding suggests a pragmatic **70/30 alignment** idea:
be aligned on the critical 70 percent and tolerate differences on lower-value matters.

If disagreement matters:
- understand the boss’s view;
- propose an experiment;
- produce ROI evidence;
- talk to sympathetic superiors carefully;
- consider rotation, transfer, or eventual exit.

### 3.13 Alignment failure loop

**Guess priorities → invest heavily → boss changes direction → feel betrayed → overwork to prove value → still no clarity.**

Interruption:
**Ask → document → reprioritize → revisit.**

## 4. Chapter 2 — The Styles Conversation

**Purpose:** Decode how others operate and make interaction less personal, less confusing, and more effective.

### 4.1 The 4Cs model

Wilding organizes style around two dimensions:
- **dominance** — how strongly a person seeks control;
- **sociability** — how much they prioritize emotional expression and relationship.

This creates four types:

1. Commander
2. Cheerleader
3. Caretaker
4. Controller

These are heuristics, not fixed identities. People can blend styles.

---

### 4.2 Commander

**High dominance, low sociability**

Motivations:
- results;
- goals;
- control;
- competition;
- winning.

Fears:
- wasting time;
- helplessness;
- dependence;
- incompetence;
- failure.

Strengths:
- confident;
- direct;
- decisive;
- strategic;
- accountable.

Weaknesses:
- impatient;
- insensitive;
- overbearing;
- critical;
- confrontational.

Do not automatically take personally:
- nitpicking;
- short replies;
- skipped pleasantries;
- strong questioning;
- selective delegation.

Best tactics:
- BLUF: bottom line up front;
- concise email;
- explicit CTA;
- regular brief updates;
- data;
- results language;
- ROI;
- feasibility;
- candid early conflict.

Useful language:
- “My recommendation is…”
- “Based on the data…”
- “Here’s what I believe we should do…”

---

### 4.3 Cheerleader

**High dominance, high sociability**

Motivations:
- positivity;
- popularity;
- impact;
- collaboration;
- experimentation.

Fears:
- rejection;
- disapproval;
- losing influence;
- stagnation;
- negativity.

Strengths:
- open;
- trusting;
- energetic;
- optimistic;
- visionary.

Weaknesses:
- flighty;
- superficial;
- overcommitted;
- impulsive;
- weak prioritization.

Do not automatically take personally:
- canceled meetings;
- shifting priorities;
- vague feedback;
- forgotten follow-through.

Best tactics:
- reschedule proactively;
- redirect to agreed priorities;
- give options;
- use upbeat framing;
- emphasize support and consensus;
- polish external-facing materials;
- pair difficult feedback with positive framing.

---

### 4.4 Caretaker

**Low dominance, high sociability**

Motivations:
- harmony;
- stability;
- connection;
- support;
- appreciation.

Fears:
- misunderstanding;
- overwhelm;
- change;
- letting others down;
- underappreciation.

Strengths:
- empathetic;
- patient;
- nurturing;
- dependable;
- calm.

Weaknesses:
- overprotective;
- accommodating;
- indecisive;
- conflict-avoidant;
- risk-averse.

Do not automatically take personally:
- frequent checking;
- hoarding stretch work;
- weak advocacy;
- late delivery of bad news.

Best tactics:
- provide context;
- make requests gently;
- introduce ideas early;
- explain people impact;
- provide risk mitigation;
- make criticism easier to deliver;
- propose gradual transitions.

---

### 4.5 Controller

**Low dominance, low sociability**

Motivations:
- order;
- efficiency;
- autonomy;
- accuracy;
- improvement.

Fears:
- unpredictability;
- disorder;
- spontaneity;
- vulnerability;
- mistakes.

Strengths:
- analytical;
- disciplined;
- methodical;
- reserved;
- determined.

Weaknesses:
- rigid;
- impersonal;
- demanding;
- uncreative;
- uncommunicative.

Do not automatically take personally:
- rejected new ideas;
- multiple reviews;
- dense information;
- emotional distance.

Best tactics:
- formal communication;
- structure;
- data;
- systems;
- quality criteria;
- pre-mortems;
- concise message + detailed appendix;
- schedule feedback rather than ambush.

### 4.6 Style matching principle

A proposal can fail because it is presented in a style that produces uncertainty or irritation for the decision-maker.

Same proposal:
- Commander → results;
- Cheerleader → possibility/social proof;
- Caretaker → stability/people;
- Controller → evidence/process.

### 4.7 Me Manual

A self-assessment of:
- information processing;
- decision style;
- communication;
- task management;
- schedule;
- work environment;
- energy;
- feedback preferences;
- conflict style;
- nonnegotiables.

Distinguish:
- **must-have**
- **preference**

Review yearly.

### 4.8 Share selectively

Do not dump a list of requirements on the boss.

Instead:
- test one low-stakes preference;
- relate it to a success;
- link it to an upcoming change;
- frame it as solving a work problem.

### 4.9 Social exchange and small accommodations

Wilding uses social exchange theory and the minimax idea:
relationships tend to expand when changes produce benefit.

Sequence:
**Small request → successful accommodation → positive outcome → greater trust → larger request becomes easier.**

### 4.10 Flex, don’t fawn

Adapt:
- pacing;
- tone;
- emotional intensity;
- body language;
- structure.

But preserve:
- core needs;
- values;
- identity;
- legitimate boundaries.

### 4.11 Observe high performers

Study:
- how they communicate with the boss;
- what medium they use;
- when they interrupt;
- how much context they provide;
- phrases they use;
- what unwritten rules they understand.

### 4.12 Digital body language

Observe:
- emoji usage;
- punctuation;
- response timing;
- preferred channel;
- cc/reply-all behavior;
- formal vs. informal openings;
- message structure.

Use observation as hypothesis, then confirm.

### 4.13 Quirk vs red flag

Process:
1. label behavior, not person;
2. assume positive intent initially;
3. try a small adjustment;
4. track impact;
5. see whether pattern affects others;
6. assess long-term cost.

### 4.14 Toxic jerk

Indicators:
- personal insults;
- public humiliation;
- threats;
- information withholding;
- scapegoating;
- credit hoarding;
- chronic favoritism;
- abuse.

Short-term self-preservation:
- reduce exposure;
- shift to written communication;
- create a paper trail;
- use safe/public settings;
- gray-rock;
- emotionally detach;
- protect energy;
- build exit plan.

The book explicitly distinguishes difficult style from abusive behavior.

## 5. Chapter 3 — The Ownership Conversation

**Purpose:** Stop waiting for permission to improve obvious problems.

### 5.1 Ownership mindset

Instead of:
> “Someone should fix this.”

Ask:
> “Is this a worthwhile problem I can help solve?”

Ownership means:
- identify;
- investigate;
- build support;
- propose;
- execute;
- communicate progress.

It is not:
- reckless overreach;
- unilateral redesign;
- stealing authority.

### 5.2 Five worthwhile problem types

#### 1. Bothersome bottlenecks
- approval delays;
- hiring delays;
- resource delays;
- broken tools;
- manual work;
- missing data.

#### 2. Neglected needs
- skills gaps;
- customer problems;
- regulatory obligations;
- overlooked projects.

#### 3. Feedback patterns
- repeated employee complaint;
- customer request;
- meeting problem;
- knowledge-sharing friction.

#### 4. Upcoming projects
- planning needs;
- documentation;
- forecasting;
- stakeholder coordination.

#### 5. Innovation opportunities
- partnerships;
- new markets;
- new services;
- new capabilities.

Best target:
**A problem that reduces your stress and also solves pain for influential people.**

### 5.3 Build buy-in before acting

Good idea alone is insufficient.

Risk:
**Sudden change + no prework → defensive resistance.**

### 5.4 Pre-suasion

Borrowed from Robert Cialdini.

Techniques:
- seek feedback before proposal;
- show competitor activity;
- create FOMO;
- tease research;
- connect to prior success;
- activate pain/pleasure;
- invoke shared values;
- shape sensory context where appropriate.

Purpose:
make the eventual idea feel like a logical next step.

### 5.5 Foot-in-the-door

Start with:
- trial;
- pilot;
- limited scope;
- first phase.

Small acceptance lowers perceived risk and builds momentum.

### 5.6 SCQA proposal

**Situation** — context.  
**Complication** — what is going wrong.  
**Question** — what must be solved.  
**Answer** — proposed path forward.

This structure turns frustration into an action-oriented case.

### 5.7 Assertiveness ladder

Wilding provides language ranging from:
- “Would you be open to hearing my thoughts?”
to:
- “I’ll be heading up [project].”

The deeper rule:
**Match assertiveness to your power, role, culture, and established credibility.**

### 5.8 Operational transparency

Keep others in the loop:
- comparison tables;
- dashboards;
- weekly updates;
- progress bars;
- thought-process notes;
- workshops.

Mechanism:
**Open loops create uncertainty.  
Transparent progress restores control and lowers defensiveness.**

### 5.9 Credit others

Ownership should not become credit capture.

Methods:
- shout-outs;
- named contribution;
- footnotes;
- attributed findings.

### 5.10 Play to strengths

Wilding discusses **unconscious competence**:
skills can be so practiced they feel ordinary to the person who has them.

Questions:
- What comes easily to me?
- What do others repeatedly seek my help with?
- What do I solve unusually fast?
- What complementary skills should I partner with?

### 5.11 Concise communication

Use:
- 3–5 key points;
- executive summary;
- bullets;
- numbered structures;
- pillars/steps/keys;
- strong verbs;
- appropriate medium.

Sensitive, controversial, interactive:
→ conversation.

Technical, reference-heavy:
→ written.

### 5.12 Resistance tactics

- door-in-the-face;
- encourage debate;
- role reversal;
- worst-possible-idea brainstorm;
- partner with resistors;
- keep stalled issue alive;
- quantify cost of inaction;
- acknowledge jealousy without shrinking.

### 5.13 New employee

Do not instantly “fix everything.”

First:
- understand decision culture;
- identify whether your role exists for innovation, maintenance, growth, support, or compliance;
- get 1–2 early wins;
- use appreciative inquiry;
- use “we” and “I wonder.”

### 5.14 When you screw up

Steps:
1. assess damage;
2. warn boss early;
3. apologize directly;
4. explain intent without using it as excuse;
5. repair;
6. create process improvement;
7. allow space if relational damage is high.

## 6. Chapter 4 — The Boundaries Conversation

**Purpose:** Protect capacity while preserving professional equity.

### 6.1 Core problem: too helpful

Chronic over-contribution can create:
- stress;
- role overload;
- work-family conflict;
- burnout;
- “organizational citizenship fatigue.”

It may also signal weak prioritization, hurting advancement.

### 6.2 Boundary categories

Primary focus:
- time;
- tasks.

Examples:
- non-promotable work;
- excess assignments;
- extended hours;
- vacation intrusion;
- unrealistic deadlines;
- unnecessary meetings;
- constant interruptions.

### 6.3 Four Feelings Test

Track moments of:
- resentment;
- frustration;
- tension;
- discomfort.

For each:
- What was asked?
- Why did I react?
- What did I wish I had said?

Use recurring emotional friction as a signal of boundary problems.

### 6.4 Boundary triage questions

- How much does this affect well-being/productivity?
- One-time or chronic?
- Enforceable?
- How disruptive would boundary be?
- Can I explain why it benefits others too?
- What is cost of not setting it?

### 6.5 Pushback power

Assess leverage across four dimensions:

#### Tenure / position
More seniority, expertise, tenure → more leverage.

#### Relationship with boss
Trust, mutual respect, consultation → more leverage.

#### Boss style
Boss open to workload discussion → more leverage.

#### Culture/context
Flexible precedent, well-being norms, feedback channels → more leverage.

### 6.6 Dig before saying no

For extra assignment:
- Why me?
- How does this support our goals?
- Where does it fit relative to current work?
- Temporary or permanent?

For long hours:
- What is urgent?
- What warrants after-hours reply?
- What is consequence of waiting?

For deadline:
- What drives the date?
- What can change: scope, time, resources?

For meeting:
- What decisions require me?
- Can I answer asynchronously?
- Can someone closer attend?
- Can I get summary?

### 6.7 Four forms of strategic concession

#### Conditional yes
Agree, but label as exception.

#### Trade-off
“If I do Y, what should move?”

#### Deferral
Pause before commitment.

#### Referral
Redirect to a better-suited person.

### 6.8 Frame boundary around boss style

Commander:
- efficiency;
- achievement.

Cheerleader:
- reputation;
- long-term impact.

Caretaker:
- well-being;
- sustainability.

Controller:
- quality;
- accuracy.

### 6.9 WII-FM

**What’s In It For Me?**

Make the boundary matter to the boss:
- quality;
- speed;
- retention;
- error reduction;
- focus;
- team sustainability.

### 6.10 Pushback to your pushback

Common reactions:
- frustration;
- guilt;
- minimizing;
- comparison.

Best response:
- acknowledge;
- restate;
- offer a workable alternative;
- return to outcomes.

### 6.11 Do not undermine your own no

Avoid:
- excess apology;
- hedging;
- overexplaining;
- nervous backtracking.

Replace apology with gratitude where appropriate.

Speak more slowly.
Allow silence after boundary.

### 6.12 Peer boundaries

Methods:
- announce capacity early;
- hard stops;
- office hours;
- shared FAQ/wiki;
- route recurring questions to system.

### 6.13 Broken-record technique

When boss refuses refusal:
calmly repeat boundary.

Do not escalate tone.

### 6.14 Dead air

Silence can force the other person to generate options rather than you automatically capitulating.

### 6.15 Always-on cultures

Tactics:
- acknowledge receipt without solving immediately;
- detailed status messages;
- schedule only 80% capacity;
- preserve 20% buffer;
- define emergencies;
- publish response windows;
- delay sending after-hours messages.

## 7. Chapter 5 — The Feedback Conversation

**Purpose:** Speak upward about harmful patterns without making it a personality attack.

### 7.1 When to speak

Ask:
- Has it happened repeatedly?
- Are consequences meaningful?
- Do I have facts?
- Am I ready to help solve it?
- Have I changed what I can control?

Exception:
racist, bigoted, discriminatory, or seriously offensive behavior warrants clearer immediate boundaries.

### 7.2 Timing framework

#### Immediate: 0–4 hours
For:
- safety;
- legal;
- financial;
- cascading operational risk.

#### Next business day: 24–48 hours
For:
- emotionally charged but nonemergency events.

#### Week or longer
For:
- sensitive patterns needing evidence.

### 7.3 Private over public

Default:
- upward feedback privately;
- avoid humiliating the boss in front of clients or higher-ups unless immediate correction is necessary.

### 7.4 ABC model

Wilding asks you to move from:

**A — Assumption**
“Boss is a micromanager.”

to:

**B — Behavior**
“Boss requires cc on minor supply orders.”

to:

**C — Change**
“Agree which categories genuinely require approval.”

The feedback conversation should mainly contain B + C, not A.

### 7.5 Gather receipts

Potential evidence:
- email;
- timeline;
- metrics;
- meeting notes;
- survey;
- change logs;
- client feedback.

Purpose:
turn “I feel like…” into “Here is what happened.”

### 7.6 Micro-yes

Open with a narrow permission request:
- “Can I offer input?”
- “Could we revisit the client conversation?”

Small agreement can increase receptivity.

### 7.7 Make it about their goals

Match feedback framing to style:
- Commander → results;
- Cheerleader → reputation/impact;
- Caretaker → harmony/support;
- Controller → process/logic.

### 7.8 Depersonalize

Use:
- specific examples;
- process;
- outcome;
- proposed adjustment.

Avoid:
- overbearing;
- lazy;
- weak;
- sensitive;
- dictator;
- selfish.

Shift:
**me vs you → us vs problem.**

### 7.9 Upward empathy

After giving feedback:
- ask what pressures they face;
- ask what you cannot see;
- look for constraints;
- offer support.

This can convert criticism into partnership.

### 7.10 Secure next steps

Ask:
- “Can I count on you to…?”
- “How should I remind you if this happens again?”
- “Can we revisit this in eight weeks?”

Follow up in writing.

### 7.11 Let it settle

Minor changes:
~two weeks.

Bigger changes:
a month or more.

Track, then course-correct.

### 7.12 Positive reinforcement

Acknowledge actual improvement:
- specific;
- genuine;
- tied to impact.

The book cites a rough **5:1 positive-to-critical feedback** idea as a relationship heuristic.

### 7.13 Escalation

If repeated efforts fail:
- document;
- go to skip-level when operationally appropriate;
- go to HR for policy violations, harassment, or retaliation.

### 7.14 Retaliation protection

- confirm decisions by email;
- take minutes;
- collect positive feedback;
- use shared tools that timestamp contribution;
- widen audience;
- refuse isolation;
- ask boss for examples behind petty criticism.

### 7.15 Offensive behavior

Tactics:
- name it;
- ask what they meant;
- request repetition;
- provide alternative wording;
- appeal to stated values;
- discuss privately if delayed processing is needed;
- use perspective shift.

## 8. Chapter 6 — The Networking Conversation

**Purpose:** Build social capital so career information and support do not depend on one boss.

### 8.1 Social capital

Definition:
the access, information, resources, advocacy, and opportunities that flow through relationships.

A strong network helps you:
- learn informal rules;
- understand power;
- bypass bureaucracy;
- access resources;
- anticipate change;
- receive referrals;
- gain sponsorship.

### 8.2 Mere exposure

Increase contact:
- affinity groups;
- cross-functional work;
- internal communities;
- company events;
- online forums;
- strategic seating;
- shared physical spaces.

### 8.3 Who matters

Wilding suggests three groups:

#### Decision-makers
- boss;
- boss’s peers;
- senior leaders;
- clients;
- board members.

#### Powerful peers
- cross-functional collaborators;
- colleagues in other locations;
- influential ICs.

#### Behind-the-scenes badasses
- HR;
- IT;
- finance;
- administration;
- facilities.

### 8.4 Introductions

Short:
name + team + boss/context + focus.

Long:
role + expertise + who you help + result + current initiative.

### 8.5 Small ask first

Do not ask a near-stranger for:
- major sponsorship;
- job;
- large resource commitment.

Start with information:
- trends;
- culture;
- process;
- templates;
- introductions;
- vendor recommendations;
- access pathways;
- internal history.

### 8.6 Outreach email

Good subject lines:
- personalized;
- mutual connection;
- commonality;
- reference their work.

Body:
- roughly ≤200 words;
- why them;
- what you want;
- small time ask;
- relevant reciprocal value if genuine.

Follow-up:
- after ~10 days to two weeks;
- maximum about two follow-ups.

### 8.7 Networking meeting structure

1. Light opening.
2. Transition deliberately.
3. Give 1–3 minutes of context.
4. Make info-ask.
5. Listen more than talk.
6. Share relevant credibility.
7. Notice openings.
8. Close on time.
9. Set next step.

### 8.8 Do not over-defer

Avoid:
- “I can’t believe you’d meet with me!”
- excessive gratitude;
- acting subservient.

Use “meeting of minds” language.

### 8.9 Close with confidence

Depending on interest:
- simply stay connected;
- own follow-up;
- adapt original ask;
- make direct request;
- give them space to say no.

### 8.10 Micro-gives

Relationship maintenance by:
- resource;
- invitation;
- introduction;
- useful feedback;
- thoughtful unexpected value.

### 8.11 Double opt-in introduction

Before connecting two people:
- ask each separately;
- explain relevance;
- only introduce after both agree.

### 8.12 Keep relationships warm

Use:
- reminders;
- quarterly follow-up;
- update on advice;
- personal milestone;
- occasional face-to-face.

### 8.13 Attract people to you

- optimize LinkedIn;
- engage with others’ posts;
- publish expertise;
- join industry groups;
- attend offline gatherings;
- ask “Who else should I know?”

### 8.14 Weak ties

Weak ties often create access to social worlds and information unavailable through close contacts.

### 8.15 Gossip

Distinguish:
**information exchange vs. gossip trading.**

Tactics:
- empathize and redirect;
- state boundary;
- say something positive;
- show disinterest;
- never write something you would fear being forwarded.

## 9. Chapter 7 — The Visibility Conversation

**Purpose:** Make contributions discoverable and memorable.

### 9.1 Visibility is not bragging

The purpose is to:
- connect contribution to outcomes;
- shape reputation;
- make future capability visible.

### 9.2 Availability heuristic

Others evaluate partly based on what they can readily recall about you.

If useful work is invisible:
- others may attribute results elsewhere;
- hidden effort does not influence career decisions.

### 9.3 Promotable work

Visibility should focus on work that:
1. supports strategic direction;
2. exposes you to important stakeholders;
3. affects the bottom line or core mission.

Warning:
What you repeatedly highlight can pigeonhole you.

### 9.4 Align visibility with one-year vision

Ask:
- What do I want to be known for?
- What work should I stop highlighting?
- What adjacent skills support the role I want?
- Which assignments create stories that move me toward my goal?

### 9.5 Storytelling

Short professional stories improve memorability.

### 9.6 Story bank

Spend ~15 minutes weekly.

Maintain roughly 4–6 high-value stories.

Capture:
- challenge;
- action;
- outcome;
- why it mattered;
- your contribution.

Look for:
- sales;
- productivity;
- efficiency;
- cost savings;
- new responsibility;
- strategic alliances;
- learning;
- innovation;
- advocacy;
- deadlines;
- client impact.

Add:
- numbers;
- testimonials;
- external recognition;
- senior praise.

### 9.7 Casual visibility

Use 30 seconds / 2–5 sentences.

Instead of:
> “Busy.”

Say:
> what you’re doing + why it matters + result.

Tailor to listener.

### 9.8 Own your one-on-ones

Start with:
- recent wins;
- strategic progress;
- praise;
- measurable impact.

Do not turn the conversation into raw task status.

### 9.9 “Try 3 then ask me”

Before escalating a problem:
try ~three solutions.

Then:
- explain diagnosis;
- explain attempted fixes;
- ask for guidance.

This creates visibility into invisible thinking.

### 9.10 Meeting storytelling frameworks

#### Problem–Action–Solution
Problem → your action → measurable outcome.

#### Before–After–Bridge
Before → improved state → what created the change.

#### Future–Focus–Forward
Future trend → implication → proposed action.

#### We–Then–Me
Team contribution first → personal role second.

### 9.11 Command the room

Tactics:
- speak second or third;
- ask useful questions if you lack a new point;
- interject between topics;
- remove hedging;
- say “I’ll research and return” when unsure;
- reclaim ignored ideas;
- contrast when disagreeing;
- circle back after meeting.

### 9.12 Internal newsletter / update

Monthly or quarterly:
- achievements;
- insights;
- upcoming priorities;
- CTA.

Use carefully according to culture and with boss alignment.

### 9.13 Nominate yourself

Visibility opportunities:
- awards;
- conferences;
- panels;
- company blog;
- webinars;
- internal training.

### 9.14 Credit stealing

Response ladder:
1. calmly restate attribution;
2. ask before accusing;
3. invoke fairness;
4. set boundary;
5. protect intellectual property;
6. document ideas;
7. present important concepts in broader settings.

### 9.15 Boss excludes you

Tactics:
- ask how attendance decisions are made;
- do things in real time rather than rely on “I’ll remember”;
- insert yourself through drafting;
- strategically cc;
- remain positive and solution-focused.

### 9.16 Visibility-confidence loop

**Name contribution → internalize competence → greater confidence → more visible participation → greater opportunity.**

## 10. Chapter 8 — The Advancement Conversation

**Purpose:** Turn career ambition into an organizationally compelling case.

### 10.1 Advancement is broader than promotion

Includes:
- title;
- promotion;
- stretch project;
- larger scope;
- team change;
- location change;
- new role;
- lateral move;
- job crafting.

### 10.2 Career growth is not automatic

Invisible constraints:
- budget;
- headcount;
- timing;
- politics;
- manager incentive to retain you;
- senior priorities;
- economy.

### 10.3 Match wants to company needs

Before pitching:
- identify gaps;
- understand boss priorities;
- understand senior leadership priorities;
- understand desired leadership traits;
- know budget cycle;
- monitor market/economy;
- inspect competitor hiring.

### 10.4 Learn the real advancement system

Ask people who advanced:
- How does promotion work?
- What tenure matters?
- Which skills?
- What experience?
- Who advocated?
- Who approves?
- How did they prepare?
- Can roles be customized?
- What obstacles occur?

### 10.5 Seed aspirations early

Do not wait until review.

Mention:
- during one-on-one;
- after a project;
- after praise;
- casually with senior colleague.

Purpose:
familiarize manager with your future before asking for a decision.

### 10.6 Read bids for support

Signals:
- curiosity;
- future-focused feedback;
- bigger assignments;
- influential meetings;
- introductions.

If boss stays vague:
ask explicitly.

### 10.7 Put cards on table

Questions:
- What proves I’m ready?
- What skills are missing?
- What experience?
- What timeline?
- What political constraints?
- Who else needs to agree?

### 10.8 When criteria conflict

Weight input according to actual decision power:
- direct boss;
- promotion committee;
- senior leader;
- HR.

Do not blindly follow popular advice if the local system works differently.

### 10.9 Advancement game plan

Create:
- goal;
- timeframe;
- organizational benefit;
- development gaps;
- training;
- stretch assignments;
- mentors;
- proof milestones.

Use **future pacing**:
help boss visualize value after you advance.

### 10.10 Track progress

Use periodic reviews and story-bank evidence.

### 10.11 Common objections

If timeline seems unrealistic:
- find middle ground.

If boss questions readiness:
- show evidence.

If skill gap exists:
- show learning plan.

If stakeholder fit is questioned:
- show how you’ll listen and adapt.

### 10.12 Company struggling

Use downturn to become useful:
- efficiency;
- profitability;
- stability;
- reputation;
- automation;
- retention.

Negotiate future commitments where possible.

### 10.13 Boss distracted

- reschedule;
- use async shared document;
- remind during brief natural moments.

### 10.14 “Approval is happening” but nothing happens

Ask:
- What stages remain?
- Who approves?
- What documents are needed?
- Can I help prepare evidence?

### 10.15 Stonewalling

Options:
- clarify with HR;
- document moving goalposts;
- seek sponsor;
- build alternative pathway.

### 10.16 Surround sound

Ask respected others to:
- share positive feedback;
- endorse;
- mention contribution;
- support promotion.

Multiple credible advocates increase social proof.

### 10.17 Make yourself dispensable

A boss may block advancement because losing you creates pain.

Reduce loss aversion:
- train successor;
- automate;
- document;
- redistribute work.

### 10.18 Favoritism

Tactics:
- study what favorite does well;
- build relationship with them;
- ask for similar opportunities;
- highlight complementary strengths.

### 10.19 Job crafting

Three forms:

#### Task crafting
Change what you do.

#### Relational crafting
Change who you work with.

#### Cognitive crafting
Change how you interpret purpose.

Use current job as testing ground for future career.

## 11. Chapter 9 — The Money Conversation

**Purpose:** Negotiate compensation as a business issue, not a personal plea.

### 11.1 Money avoidance

Common fears:
- appearing greedy;
- shame from rejection;
- relational backlash;
- being seen as ungrateful;
- believing meaningful work and good pay are incompatible.

### 11.2 Meaning and money are not mutually exclusive

Wilding challenges:
> “I can have meaning or money.”

Her alternative:
fair compensation can improve:
- satisfaction;
- commitment;
- retention;
- energy.

### 11.3 Negotiation is data gathering even when rejected

If you ask and hear no, you still learn:
- salary process;
- priorities;
- how contribution is perceived;
- timing;
- hidden constraints.

### 11.4 Bias and backlash

Women and marginalized employees may face higher interpersonal penalties for assertive negotiation.

The book encourages:
- asking about fairness systems;
- auditing disparities;
- normalizing compensation discussion;
- advocating for others.

### 11.5 Timing

Look at:
- earnings;
- profit;
- contracts;
- hiring;
- industry health;
- talent shortages;
- recruiter interest.

Bad timing does not mean never; it may mean sequence.

### 11.6 Build business case

Evidence:
- quantifiable accomplishments;
- team performance;
- cost savings;
- revenue;
- engagement;
- retention;
- speed;
- compliance;
- market share;
- talent pipeline.

If exact numbers unavailable:
- estimate range;
- use frequency;
- benchmark;
- qualitative case studies;
- testimonials.

### 11.7 Future ROI

Include what additional compensation helps enable:
- expanded role;
- more revenue;
- more client acquisition;
- leadership capacity;
- business continuity.

### 11.8 Compensation deck

Potential sections:
- track record;
- results;
- future growth;
- projected ROI;
- market comparison.

### 11.9 Ask ranges

Author’s rough heuristics:

- **5–10%** — junior / conservative context.
- **10–15%** — mid-level / strong track record.
- **15–25%** — senior / underpaid / scope expanded / high-demand skill.

These are source heuristics, not universal market rules.

### 11.10 Market research

Sources:
- salary sites;
- recruiters;
- job listings;
- HR salary bands;
- peers.

If responsibilities exceed title:
benchmark next-level work, not only current title.

### 11.11 Discuss compensation with peers

Possible framing:
- compare public market range;
- offer reciprocal disclosure;
- keep discussion respectful.

### 11.12 Set meeting

Give advance notice.

Keep money meeting ~20–30 minutes unless complex.

### 11.13 Ask directly

Structure:
1. gratitude;
2. purpose;
3. track record;
4. future value;
5. market evidence;
6. number;
7. silence.

### 11.14 Silence after number

Wilding emphasizes:
**Deliver number and stop.**

Rationale:
- conveys confidence;
- prevents nervous discounting;
- creates space for response.

### 11.15 Resistance

Budget:
- ask what is possible;
- deferred raise;
- performance incentive.

Policy/timing:
- ask for exceptions/precedent;
- schedule next review.

Need more proof:
- define milestones.

Fairness to peers:
- discuss contribution differentiation.

Uncertainty:
- ask for noncash benefits.

### 11.16 Layered options

Prepare several packages:
- higher cash;
- cash + review date;
- lower cash + vacation;
- lower cash + flexibility;
- title/equity/development/access.

This reduces freezing.

### 11.17 Nonmonetary compensation

Potential:
- equity;
- bonus;
- title;
- office;
- remote work;
- flexible hours;
- vacation;
- development budget;
- transportation;
- home-office stipend;
- meetings/access;
- new responsibilities.

### 11.18 If rejected

Ask:
- rationale in writing;
- future review date;
- concrete milestone;
- explicit future support.

### 11.19 Practice talking about money

Wilding frames repeated low-stakes exposure as habituation.

Practice:
- bills;
- markets;
- personal financial conversations;
- advocating for team budget.

### 11.20 External offer as leverage

Only use when:
- genuine offer;
- willing to accept;
- strong rapport;
- low retaliation risk;
- culturally normal.

Risks:
- perceived disloyalty;
- trust damage;
- being sidelined.

Ask for match:
state preference to stay.

If counteroffer is not enough:
negotiate package.

Be prepared to walk.
Do not bluff.

Use sparingly.

## 12. Chapter 10 — The Quitting Conversation

**Purpose:** Know when managing up has reached its limit and exit without unnecessary damage.

### 12.1 Quitting is part of managing up

Leaving can be the highest-agency move when:
- repeated conversations fail;
- boss remains abusive;
- advancement is structurally blocked;
- compensation stays misaligned;
- values diverge;
- health/capacity deteriorates;
- job no longer supports growth.

The book frames quitting not as defeat but as a legitimate strategic decision.

### 12.2 Stay-or-go decision

Before resigning:
- assess whether problem is role, boss, team, company, or industry;
- identify what has already been tried;
- distinguish temporary strain from stable pattern;
- compare internal transfer or job crafting;
- revisit one-year vision.

### 12.3 Flash forward

Imagine future self if:
- you stay another year;
- you leave.

Questions:
- Which choice is more aligned with future you?
- What gets better?
- What worsens?
- What opportunities open or close?

### 12.4 Worst-case scenario

Walk through feared outcome:
- money;
- gap;
- reference;
- identity;
- job search;
- benefits;
- uncertainty.

Purpose:
make fear concrete enough to plan against.

### 12.5 Play defense before resigning

Before giving notice:
- review company policies;
- understand notice requirements;
- secure personal copies of legitimate employment records you are allowed to retain;
- protect confidential information;
- organize finances;
- line up references where possible;
- prepare for immediate access removal.

### 12.6 Break news one-on-one

Be direct:
- “I’m resigning.”
- state final day.

Rehearse answer to:
> “Why?”

Keep forward-focused:
- growth;
- new challenge;
- fit;
- health;
- family;
- different environment.

Do not:
- unload grievances;
- overexplain;
- apologize excessively;
- boast about new perks.

### 12.7 Boss reaction

Possible:
- shock;
- sadness;
- disappointment;
- anger.

Response:
- stay calm;
- reaffirm decision;
- redirect toward transition.

### 12.8 Put resignation in writing

Letter:
- date;
- final day;
- appreciation;
- commitment to handover.

Keep it simple.

### 12.9 Counteroffer

A counteroffer may include:
- pay;
- title;
- flexibility;
- promises.

Wilding cautions against treating it automatically as a solution.

Assess:
- Why did you want to leave?
- Does offer solve root cause?
- Why did change require resignation?
- What is likelihood promise survives?
- How will trust change?

### 12.10 Transition tactfully

Provide:
- project status;
- deadlines;
- stakeholders;
- recurring processes;
- files;
- decision history;
- outstanding risks;
- recommended next steps.

### 12.11 Knowledge transfer

Aim to leave the system operable without you.

Useful:
- handoff document;
- successor meeting;
- process guide;
- contact list;
- outstanding commitments.

### 12.12 Do not become a dumping ground after notice

If new requests keep appearing:
- redirect;
- negotiate;
- say what can realistically be completed;
- focus on transition-critical work.

### 12.13 Communication strategy

Coordinate:
- boss;
- HR;
- team;
- clients;
- external stakeholders.

Avoid telling everyone before boss knows.

Shape narrative:
- appreciative;
- forward-looking;
- professional.

### 12.14 Exit interview

Be deliberate.

Useful feedback:
- patterns;
- facts;
- actionable organizational issues.

Avoid:
- emotional score-settling;
- speculative character attacks;
- disclosures that can unnecessarily damage references.

Exception:
serious policy violations, harassment, discrimination, safety, or legal concerns should be handled honestly through appropriate channels.

### 12.15 Express gratitude

Thank:
- manager where appropriate;
- collaborators;
- mentors;
- direct reports;
- supporters.

Specific gratitude preserves network strength.

### 12.16 Leave on bad terms without making them worse

If relationship is poor:
- stay factual;
- fulfill legitimate transition obligations;
- preserve documentation;
- avoid public retaliation;
- keep network ties beyond boss.

### 12.17 Get references

Ask people who:
- know your work;
- can speak concretely;
- have enough credibility to matter.

Do not rely on a hostile manager as sole reference.

### 12.18 Create closure

Leaving can trigger:
- relief;
- grief;
- anger;
- doubt;
- identity loss.

Use reflection:
- What did I learn?
- What do I want next?
- What pattern will I not repeat?
- What relationship do I want to preserve?

## 13. Principles and mechanisms

### 13.1 Alignment reduces wasted work
Correct target matters more than raw effort.

### 13.2 Questions create both information and rapport
Curiosity can improve likability while reducing uncertainty.

### 13.3 Mirroring encourages elaboration
Repeating key words prompts additional detail without demanding it.

### 13.4 Transparency reduces anxiety
People resist less when they can see process and progress.

### 13.5 Adaptation is strongest when reciprocal
Healthy managing up is not one-way accommodation.

### 13.6 Style mismatch creates false interpersonal conclusions
Shortness may reflect efficiency rather than dislike; vagueness may reflect big-picture thinking rather than disrespect.

### 13.7 Small wins build political capital
Early evidence increases latitude for later initiative.

### 13.8 Pre-suasion changes receptivity before the ask
Context shapes how a proposal is interpreted.

### 13.9 Smaller requests reduce resistance
Pilots and trials lower perceived risk.

### 13.10 Boundaries can increase credibility
Prioritization signals judgment.

### 13.11 Emotional discomfort does not mean boundary is wrong
Fear often reflects power asymmetry, not poor logic.

### 13.12 Overexplaining weakens leverage
Too much justification invites negotiation against your own reasons.

### 13.13 Silence is a negotiation tool
Dead air can prevent premature concession.

### 13.14 Behavior-focused feedback lowers ego threat
Specific conduct is easier to change than global identity.

### 13.15 Upward empathy improves persuasion
Understanding boss constraints allows mutually useful solutions.

### 13.16 Documentation converts subjective conflict into observable evidence
Receipts matter when power is uneven.

### 13.17 Social capital diversifies dependence
A single boss should not be your only source of information, validation, opportunity, or protection.

### 13.18 Weak ties increase access to novel information
Close networks often contain redundant information.

### 13.19 Visibility affects opportunity through memory
Decision-makers cannot reward what they do not recall.

### 13.20 Stories increase memorability
Narrative makes contribution easier to retain than raw task lists.

### 13.21 Visibility should be directional
Highlighting the wrong work can create the wrong career.

### 13.22 Advancement is easier when framed as solving organizational pain
Personal ambition becomes strategically relevant.

### 13.23 Future pacing helps people imagine benefit
The boss is more likely to support change when they can visualize the resulting upside.

### 13.24 Sponsors reduce sole dependence on boss
Advocacy from multiple power holders creates resilience.

### 13.25 Making yourself dispensable can accelerate promotion
Being indispensable in the current role can become a trap.

### 13.26 Compensation negotiations are business cases
Personal need is not the primary justification.

### 13.27 Asking produces information even when the answer is no
Negotiation creates data.

### 13.28 External offers create leverage but also signal exit risk
Use sparingly and only if real.

### 13.29 Counteroffers may solve symptoms, not causes
Money cannot repair every values, culture, or leadership problem.

### 13.30 Leaving well preserves future optionality
Departure is a reputational event.

## 15. Diagnostic rules and decision criteria

- **If you work hard but results are repeatedly reworked,** check alignment before effort.
- **If your boss’s replies feel cold,** inspect style before assuming rejection.
- **If a new idea excites you,** ask whether it solves an influential stakeholder’s problem.
- **If you feel powerless,** identify what information you have not yet gathered.
- **If the boss changes priorities constantly,** quantify the trade-off instead of merely complaining.
- **If your boss has a new idea once,** capture it; if it repeatedly reappears, treat it more seriously.
- **If you disagree on method but agree on goals,** do not turn every difference into a principle fight.
- **If your manager’s behavior harms dignity,** do not rationalize it as “style.”
- **If you are new,** build credibility before trying to overhaul everything.
- **If a problem is both painful to you and strategically useful to solve,** it is a strong ownership candidate.
- **If a proposal causes immediate resistance,** reduce scope or pre-suade.
- **If you keep accepting work while resenting it,** run the Four Feelings Test.
- **If a boss asks for new work,** clarify what moves off the list.
- **If you have low pushback power,** use conditional yes, trade-offs, or deferrals.
- **If the boundary requires long justification,** you may be undermining yourself.
- **If your manager bulldozes,** lower your pace and repeat rather than match aggression.
- **If a criticism is based on a label,** convert it to behavior and requested change.
- **If the issue is high-risk legally or financially,** give feedback quickly.
- **If you have not changed what you control,** do that before blaming upward.
- **If your boss reacts defensively,** explore pressure before assuming refusal.
- **If feedback produces no change,** follow up before escalating.
- **If retaliation begins,** document immediately and widen your support network.
- **If your career depends on one person,** your network is too narrow.
- **If you are asking a stranger for a big favor,** shrink the first request.
- **If outreach is ignored twice,** stop chasing.
- **If someone talks to you only when they need something,** do not model your networking on them.
- **If your work is invisible,** create a story bank.
- **If visibility highlights work you do not want more of,** change the narrative.
- **If you stay silent in meetings and feel overlooked,** increase contribution before blaming exclusion.
- **If a colleague takes credit,** clarify promptly but without unnecessary hostility.
- **If you want promotion,** find the actual decision criteria before building your case.
- **If your manager praises you but blocks promotion,** ask what specifically prevents advancement.
- **If advancement creates a replacement problem,** solve that too.
- **If formal promotion is unavailable,** negotiate interim scope, title, project, or experience.
- **If compensation is below market,** quantify both gap and value.
- **If salary is rejected,** negotiate noncash benefits and a review date.
- **If you use an external offer,** be willing to accept it.
- **If repeated money or advancement discussions produce nothing,** seriously evaluate exit.
- **If you want to quit after one bad week,** distinguish acute strain from persistent pattern.
- **If the root cause is a toxic boss but company is good,** consider transfer before resignation.
- **If the root cause is company-wide values or culture,** internal transfer may not solve it.
- **If a counteroffer appears,** ask whether it solves the original reason you wanted to leave.
- **If you resign,** tell your boss before the broader network.
- **If you leave angry,** do not turn the exit interview into revenge.

## 18. Important distinctions and taxonomies

### Managing up vs. pleasing the boss
- **Managing up:** strategic mutual effectiveness.
- **Pleasing:** approval as primary objective.

### Flexing vs. fawning
- **Flexing:** adaptation.
- **Fawning:** self-abandonment.

### Style difference vs. red flag
- **Style:** workable preference mismatch.
- **Red flag:** repeated harmful effect.
- **Abuse:** dignity/safety violation.

### Ownership vs. overstepping
- **Ownership:** solve useful problem with stakeholder awareness.
- **Overstepping:** act beyond authority without context or buy-in.

### Promotable vs. non-promotable work
- **Promotable:** strategic, visible, high-value.
- **Non-promotable:** maintenance/admin with low strategic visibility.

### Boundary vs. refusal
- **Boundary:** defines conditions under which you can contribute sustainably.
- **Refusal:** one possible implementation.

### Conditional yes vs. automatic yes
- **Conditional:** cooperative but bounded.
- **Automatic:** unexamined compliance.

### Feedback vs. complaint
- **Feedback:** specific behavior + impact + change.
- **Complaint:** dissatisfaction without path forward.

### Assumption vs. behavior
- “Micromanager” = assumption.
- “Requires cc on every minor email” = behavior.

### Upward empathy vs. excusing harm
- **Empathy:** understand constraint.
- **Excuse:** eliminate accountability.

### Social capital vs. transactional networking
- **Social capital:** reciprocal relationship capacity.
- **Transactional networking:** contact used only when needed.

### Visibility vs. bragging
- **Visibility:** useful contextualized contribution.
- **Bragging:** status display without audience relevance.

### Team credit vs. personal credit
Use **We–Then–Me** to preserve both.

### Promotion vs. advancement
Advancement includes:
- scope;
- role;
- project;
- team;
- location;
- title;
- skill.

### Sponsor vs. mentor
- **Mentor:** advice.
- **Sponsor:** uses influence on your behalf.

### Salary vs. total compensation
Total package may include:
- cash;
- bonus;
- equity;
- flexibility;
- vacation;
- development;
- title;
- access.

### Counteroffer vs. root-cause solution
More money can retain you without fixing the reason you wanted to leave.

### Difficult boss vs. toxic boss
The first may be managed; the second may require protection and exit.

## 21. Integrated conceptual map

The ten conversations form five deeper capability layers.

### Layer 1 — Sensemaking
**Alignment + Styles**

Questions:
- What matters?
- Who has power?
- What does success mean?
- How does this person operate?

Output:
**Accurate model of the environment.**

### Layer 2 — Agency
**Ownership + Boundaries**

Questions:
- What should I take on?
- What should I not take on?
- Where can I create value?
- What capacity must I protect?

Output:
**Controlled expansion rather than passive compliance or chaotic overwork.**

### Layer 3 — Influence
**Feedback + Networking**

Questions:
- How do I shape behavior above me?
- Who else needs to know me?
- Where can I get information and advocacy?

Output:
**Influence distributed beyond formal authority.**

### Layer 4 — Reputation and mobility
**Visibility + Advancement**

Questions:
- What should I be known for?
- What future role does that reputation support?
- What evidence proves readiness?
- Who will sponsor me?

Output:
**Career optionality.**

### Layer 5 — Value capture and exit
**Money + Quitting**

Questions:
- Am I being paid fairly for the value I create?
- Can the system meet my needs?
- If not, is leaving now the higher-agency move?

Output:
**Economic and career self-determination.**

### System loop

**Clarity → contribution → credibility → influence → visibility → opportunity → leverage → autonomy.**

### Failure loop

**Ambiguity → guessing → overwork → low recognition → resentment → passive coping → lower leverage.**

### Boundary loop

**Overcommit → burnout → weaker work → fear of disappointing → overcommit.**

Interrupt with:
**trade-offs + pushback + explicit prioritization.**

### Visibility loop

**Visible strategic results → stronger reputation → better assignments → more strategic results.**

### Network loop

**Small contact → useful exchange → social capital → information/access → better decisions → more useful exchanges.**

### Advancement loop

**Aspiration → criteria → evidence → advocates → larger scope → stronger evidence.**

### Exit loop

When other loops remain blocked:
**repeated failed attempts → evidence of structural constraint → strategic exit → new environment with higher fit.**

The full book’s deepest message can be summarized as:

> Power at work is not binary. You may not control the hierarchy, but you can continuously increase your room to maneuver by understanding the system, making your value legible, setting limits, building relationships, and making deliberate asks.
