# 169 Ways to Score Points with Your Boss — Comprehensive Knowledge Extraction

**Author:** Alan R. Schonberg, with Robert L. Shook and Donna G. Estreicher, Ph.D.  
**Primary subject:** Managing upward, workplace effectiveness, career advancement, professional reputation, and boss–employee relations  
**Extraction purpose:** High-recall extraction for multi-source synthesis  
**Source edition:** Contemporary Books, 1998

> **Epistemic note:** This extraction represents what the book claims and recommends. The book is a practitioner-oriented collection of career heuristics derived from Alan Schonberg's executive/recruiting experience, a survey of roughly 3,500 Management Recruiters International professionals, and input from client executives and HR leaders. It is not presented as a scientifically validated model. Some advice reflects 1990s corporate norms and is flagged where its framing is especially dated, coercive, risky, or context-dependent.

## 1. Book in one model

The book's central proposition is that career success depends not only on technical performance but on becoming an unusually valuable, low-friction, trustworthy person for one's boss to manage. The boss is treated as the employee's most consequential internal customer because the boss controls assignments, reputation, advancement, compensation, and access. At the same time, the book repeatedly calls the external customer the company's “ultimate boss,” because customer value sustains the organization that pays both employee and manager.

Its implicit model is:

**Understand the customer, company, culture, and boss → align with the boss's priorities and reduce the boss's workload/risk → perform reliably and beyond baseline expectations → communicate clearly and protect trust → make contributions visible without appearing self-centered → build relationships, skills, and organizational knowledge → receive more trust, autonomy, responsibility, sponsorship, and advancement → continue adapting.**

The book treats workplace reputation as cumulative. Small behaviors—punctuality, returning calls, note-taking, gratitude, discretion, courtesy, preparation, follow-through—operate as repeated signals about character and competence. Large achievements matter, but the authors argue that bosses often decide whom they trust through dozens of small observations.

The employee is therefore asked to manage several things simultaneously:

- **Business value:** serve customers, understand the whole company, contribute to profit and strategic priorities.
- **Execution:** prepare, focus, finish, follow through, solve problems, manage time, and deliver quality.
- **Upward management:** learn the boss's priorities, boundaries, moods, idiosyncrasies, information needs, and status concerns.
- **Relationship capital:** be courteous, likeable, loyal, appreciative, supportive, and well-networked.
- **Reputation:** be visible but modest; promote accomplishments without bragging; speak up without becoming argumentative.
- **Adaptability:** keep skills current, accept change, learn continuously, and avoid becoming rigid or obsolete.
- **Character:** maintain integrity, discretion, reliability, resilience, and a team orientation.
- **Career agency:** know one's market value, seek feedback and responsibility, cultivate mentors, plan a career, and train successors.

The book is highly hierarchical. It frequently assumes that the employee should adapt to the boss rather than expect the boss to adapt to the employee. At its best, this yields useful advice about perspective-taking, timing, communication, and organizational reality. At its most dated, it can drift into deference, impression management, excessive loyalty, unpaid overwork, or suppressing legitimate dissent. Those tensions are important to preserve because they are part of the book's actual intellectual identity.

## 2. Core concepts, models, and frameworks

### 2.1 The boss-as-customer model

The most explicit organizing idea appears in Tip 159: treat the boss like one's best customer. The book says the boss has two recurring needs:

1. **Make the boss's work and life easier.**
2. **Make the boss look good.**

The employee is conceptualized as continuously “selling” a service—his or her own work. Past performance does not permanently secure the relationship; the employee must keep providing value, anticipating needs, and improving the product.

### 2.2 The external customer as the ultimate boss

Tip 1 establishes a second level of the model: every role should be traced to the end customer. Customer satisfaction is not only the sales department's responsibility. Accounting, operations, administration, logistics, and management should ask how their work affects customer value. The book repeatedly returns to the idea that exceptional customer service is one of the safest ways to make the boss look good because external praise carries more credibility than self-promotion.

### 2.3 “Points” as reputation capital

The title's metaphor is not formalized mathematically, but the book treats workplace impressions as an accumulating balance. Employees gain “points” through reliability, contribution, tact, preparation, support, visibility, discretion, and initiative; they lose them through lateness, gossip, poor judgment, public confrontation, weak follow-through, or self-centered behavior. Reputation is therefore path-dependent: one dramatic act matters, but repeated micro-signals create a durable expectation about what kind of employee someone is.

### 2.4 Career self-security through adaptability

The introduction rejects the idea that security comes from expecting one employer to provide a lifelong position. Instead, career security comes from continuously developing **marketable, transferable skills** and adapting as technology, organizations, roles, and employers change. The authors call downsizing “rightsizing” and argue that people who remain current can preserve employability even when specific jobs disappear.

### 2.5 Visibility without grandstanding

A recurring tension is that good work must be visible, but overt self-promotion can damage credibility. The book's preferred pattern is:

**perform exceptionally → create natural visibility → allow customers, mentors, publications, projects, or results to amplify the achievement → use direct self-promotion selectively and subtly.**

This theme links Tips 17, 43, 93, 105, 119, 142, and 143.

### 2.6 Trust as reduced managerial burden

Many tips are mechanisms for becoming easier to supervise: clarify assignments at the beginning, then work independently; follow through; admit mistakes early; report bad news; keep confidences; meet deadlines; take notes; return calls; and manage time. The practical payoff is that the boss can delegate with lower monitoring cost and lower fear of unpleasant surprises.

### 2.7 The informal organization

The book distinguishes the formal organization from the organization that actually operates day to day. Three informal systems matter:

- **Unwritten rules:** culturally enforced expectations that may never appear in policy.
- **Office politics:** the real distribution of influence, precedents, alliances, and decision routes.
- **The grapevine:** informal information that may be distorted but can also reveal weak signals before formal channels do.

Effective employees observe all three rather than relying only on job descriptions or organization charts.

### 2.8 Boss calibration

The book repeatedly asks employees to learn the particular boss rather than apply universal tactics mechanically. Important boss-specific variables include:

- priorities;
- mood and timing;
- decision boundaries;
- communication preferences;
- idiosyncrasies;
- status and ego sensitivities;
- reading habits;
- values;
- tolerance for autonomy;
- need for information;
- expectations about schedule, dress, and protocol.

### 2.9 Team-credit logic

The authors treat excessive credit-seeking as a threat to team functioning and boss relations. Recommended tactics include saying “we,” praising others publicly, sharing credit, avoiding limelight theft, training successors, supporting corporate wins, and accepting that a supported adequate idea may outperform a technically superior but unsupported one.

### 2.10 Information-flow model

Good upward management depends on controlling both the quality and route of information:

- ask questions before acting;
- give the boss important ideas first;
- communicate bad news early;
- never punish messengers;
- keep confidential information confidential;
- avoid gossip;
- listen for what people want and need;
- separate useful information from irrelevant chatter;
- understand when the informal grapevine contains a signal worth investigating.

### 2.11 Career-capital model

The book treats career advancement as the interaction of:

**performance + skills + visibility + boss sponsorship + network + reputation + successor readiness + organizational fit.**

A high performer can still be trapped if nobody can replace him or if his accomplishments are invisible. A visible networker can still fail if performance is weak. Advancement requires the bundle.

### 2.12 Values and integrity

The book asks employees to know the organization's real values, not merely its slogans, and to hire/associate with people who embody them. It also treats personal integrity as non-negotiable. This creates a notable internal tension with some of its stronger loyalty/deference advice: at several points the book tells employees to protect the boss or company, but Tip 152 says integrity should never be compromised.

## 3. Principles and mechanisms

### 3.1 Reliability compounds

A boss does not infer dependability from one promise; dependability emerges from repeated small commitments kept. Following through on calls, deadlines, meetings, notes, assignments, and customer requests gradually reduces perceived risk. This is why the book places seemingly mundane behaviors beside strategic career advice.

### 3.2 Reduced supervision creates leverage

When employees clarify expectations once and then solve routine problems independently, the boss saves attention. Saved managerial attention is treated as value. Employees who require repeated instructions or reassurance impose a coordination tax; employees who think ahead become candidates for more responsibility.

### 3.3 Reciprocity matters upward as well as downward

Praise, gratitude, encouragement, sympathy, support, and courtesy are not portrayed as one-way obligations from bosses to staff. Bosses are human and respond to recognition too. The book repeatedly predicts a reciprocity loop: employees who make bosses feel appreciated and supported become more valued and are more likely to receive support in return.

### 3.4 Timing changes the same message's reception

A good request at a bad moment may fail. The authors therefore separate **content** from **timing**. Raises, proposals, criticism, and difficult conversations should be introduced when the boss is mentally available rather than rushed, angry, overloaded, or preoccupied.

### 3.5 Protecting face reduces defensive resistance

Public correction, humiliation, upstaging, difficult surprise questions, and arguments threaten status. Private criticism, respectful disagreement, allowing others to save face, and letting the boss receive legitimate recognition reduce defensiveness. The book assumes that preserving dignity improves cooperation.

### 3.6 Visibility and modesty must be calibrated

Too little visibility leaves contributions unknown; too much self-promotion creates a braggart/“prima donna” reputation. The solution is evidence-based visibility: results, customer praise, internal publications, cross-functional contributions, mentor advocacy, and measured self-reporting.

### 3.7 The best dissent is useful dissent

The book is not purely conformist. It says a boss has little need for someone who always agrees. Useful dissent combines conviction with tact: speak up, bring alternatives, avoid needless argument, distinguish principle from preference, and disagree privately when status is at stake.

### 3.8 Early clarification prevents expensive rework

Asking questions before beginning an assignment is framed as professionalism, not weakness. Pretending to understand protects ego in the moment but risks wasted time, money, and credibility later.

### 3.9 Early error disclosure contains damage

Mistakes are inevitable; concealment is the bigger danger. Admitting an error early lets the organization correct course while the problem is still small. If someone else exposes the mistake, the employee may appear either unaware or dishonest.

### 3.10 Bad-news safety improves organizational sensing

Punishing people who bring unpleasant information trains them to hide it. Rewarding accurate bad-news reporting keeps information channels open and lets problems be addressed earlier. This is one of the book's strongest causal mechanisms and anticipates later ideas about psychological safety and error reporting.

### 3.11 Relationship capital expands options

Networks create information, access, referrals, advocacy, and future mobility. The book treats relationship-building as a long-duration investment. Courtesy to people without current power is especially important because rank and relevance can change.

### 3.12 Adaptability prevents obsolescence

Continuous learning, current industry knowledge, technology literacy, openness to ideas, varied skills, and willingness to change all target the same risk: becoming useful only in yesterday's environment.

### 3.13 Failure can be separated from identity

A failed idea, rejection, missed promotion, or poor outcome is treated as an event rather than a permanent identity judgment. The book advocates learning, regrouping, and trying again rather than construing rejection of an idea as rejection of the person.

### 3.14 Culture is behavioral, not declarative

Written values and policies matter less than what the organization repeatedly rewards, tolerates, and does. The authors advise observing actual practice to infer real priorities.

### 3.15 Attention is an economic resource

Time, meetings, calls, conversations, documents, and projects all compete for scarce attention. Brevity, focus, agendas, prioritization, simplicity, and finishing at the point of diminishing returns are all applications of the same principle: protect attention from low-value consumption.

## 4. Strategies, tactics, and techniques

The 169 items below preserve the book's numbered structure while compressing narrative examples and repetition.

### Tips 1–25: value, execution, and first-order professionalism

**1. Be Customer Driven.** Trace your work to the end customer, even if you never interact with customers directly. Ask how your role can improve customer satisfaction. The book treats the customer as the organization's ultimate boss because customer demand ultimately funds employment.

**2. Remember That Time Is Money.** Treat paid time as an economic resource rather than an entitlement. Be punctual and avoid habitual early departures or time-wasting. The authors go further and favor arriving early/staying late to signal commitment; this “extra hours” norm should be interpreted in the context of 1998 work culture.

**3. Look the Part.** Dress appropriately for the role, industry, and level you aspire to. The mechanism is impression formation: appearance becomes a proxy for judgment, professionalism, and fit. The specific conservative-dress examples are dated, but the general principle is contextual signaling.

**4. Read Your Company's Annual Report from Cover to Cover.** Study the annual report, footnotes, and—where relevant—public filings such as the 10-K. Goal: understand strategy, performance, financial position, and details well enough that outsiders do not know the company better than you do.

**5. Learn to Handle a Computer.** Original 1998 recommendation: computer literacy is a baseline employability skill at every level. Generalizable principle: understand the technologies that structure your work even if you do not operate them constantly; technological illiteracy impairs communication and judgment.

**6. Be Prepared When You Attend a Meeting.** Read the agenda/materials, prepare comments and supporting data, arrive on time, take notes, attend to speakers, stay relevant, control emotion, and do not undermine the boss publicly. Preparation converts meeting time from passive attendance into useful contribution.

**7. Deliver More than You Promise.** Keep commitments first, then exceed them where realistic: beat rather than merely meet deadlines or quotas. Avoid overpromising. The book's shorthand is that baseline performance preserves the job while extra value supports advancement.

**8. Promote and Use Your Company's Products.** Where reasonable, use and advocate for the employer's offerings. The authors treat this as evidence of pride and as a way to experience the product as a customer, which may reveal improvements.

**9. Be Prudent with Your Expense Account.** Spend company money as carefully as your own. Extravagance signals weak judgment; padding expenses signals untrustworthiness and may jeopardize employment. The underlying principle is stewardship of resources one does not personally own.

**10. Be a Good Note Taker.** Bring a notebook to conversations with the boss and meetings. Capture assignments, promises, decisions, and important details. Note-taking reduces memory failures and signals attention.

**11. Never Say Anything Negative About Your Former Company or Boss.** The book views badmouthing past employers as a reputational warning: the current boss may infer that you are difficult or will later criticize them too. Generalizable point: describe prior problems factually and professionally rather than using new colleagues as an audience for resentment.

**12. If Your Boss Turns Down Your Suggestion, Don't Take It Personally.** Distinguish rejection of an idea from rejection of self. The boss may possess information you lack. Useful follow-up questions: What do you think? What else should I know? What would you do first? How do you see the end result?

**13. Make Your Customers Rave About You.** Deliver service good enough to create loyalty, repeat business, referrals, and unsolicited positive feedback. Third-party customer praise is treated as especially powerful because it validates the employee without self-promotion.

**14. Follow Through.** Reliability is built through accumulation of small completions. Return calls, send promised information, meet deadlines, and attend commitments. Consistent follow-through makes the employee safe to depend on.

**15. Become an Active Member of a Professional Organization.** Do more than join: attend seminars, take courses, exchange ideas, and network with peers. Purpose: prevent complacency, stay current, and increase professional identity and marketability.

**16. Always View Your Job as Important.** Treat even low-status work as meaningful and perform it in a way that expands its value. The author's mechanism is partly self-image: if you respect your work and enlarge its contribution, others are more likely to notice its importance.

**17. Write an Article for Your Company Newsletter.** Contribute useful internal writing tied to projects, expertise, or company progress. Have it professionally edited. This creates constructive visibility for both employee and department.

**18. Take Corporate “Good News” Personally.** Celebrate organizational wins even when you did not personally cause them. The authors use this as a test of team identification and warn against the “what's in it for me?” response to collective success.

**19. Consider No Task Beneath You.** Leaders should be willing to perform small or menial tasks when doing so helps the work move. Initiative on trivial obstacles is treated as evidence that one will also take ownership of larger problems.

**20. Praise Others in Public.** Praise publicly and criticize privately. Make recognition specific and sincere. Upward praise is included: bosses also respond to acknowledgment.

**21. Learn to Work with Less Supervision.** Ask enough questions initially to understand the assignment, then research and solve ordinary issues independently. The aim is autonomy without ambiguity.

**22. Telephone Courtesy with Your Boss.** Give the boss's call priority, avoid putting the boss on hold unnecessarily, return calls quickly, and maintain professional voicemail/answering messages. The technology is dated; the responsiveness principle is not.

**23. Get to the Point—Brevity Is a Virtue.** Respect the boss's scarce attention. State the issue, evidence, and request concisely rather than treating access to the boss as an invitation to narrate everything you know.

**24. Recognize When a Job Is Finished.** Stop polishing when additional effort yields negligible value. Perfectionism can become waste when it delays other work and the customer/boss cannot detect or value the marginal improvement.

**25. Never Hold a Grudge.** Do not let resentment become a persistent workplace behavior. The book does not say mistreatment must be ignored; it allows a private conversation about harm. Distinction: address the issue if needed, then avoid sustaining an interpersonal feud that disrupts work.

### Tips 26–55: hierarchy, ideas, visibility, communication, and judgment

**26. Go Through the Chain of Command.** Learn the organization's formal hierarchy and normally respect it. The book states this almost absolutely; in modern contexts, legitimate exceptions can include ethics, safety, harassment, protected reporting, or formal escalation routes.

**27. Live in the Present.** When joining a new employer, stop constantly comparing it with the old one. Ask questions, learn the current team's system, contribute quickly, and share prior experience without implying that the former company did everything better.

**28. Never Confront Your Boss in the Presence of Others.** Handle disagreement privately when possible. Public confrontation threatens dignity and authority, increasing the chance that the interaction becomes a status contest rather than problem solving.

**29. Use Flattery Sparingly.** Distinguish sincere praise from exaggerated flattery. Excessive praise lowers the credibility of genuine compliments because it creates suspicion about motive.

**30. Restaurant Etiquette.** At business meals: order reasonably, do not disappear to socialize elsewhere, avoid complaining, do not choose conspicuously expensive items, thank the payer, minimize phone use, treat service staff respectfully, tip appropriately, attend to tablemates, and protect confidentiality by speaking quietly.

**31. Give Only Your Best Ideas.** Outside true brainstorming, filter ideas before presenting them. Too many weak options consume decision time and can cause your judgment to be evaluated by the worst suggestion in the set.

**32. Be a Jack-of-All-Trades.** Build adjacent skills and understand multiple areas of the business so you can see the larger system and cover gaps when needed. The book favors breadth because it sees narrow specialists as more vulnerable to technological or organizational change; modern synthesis should balance this with the value of deep expertise.

**33. Adopt a Lifetime Self-Improvement Plan.** Continue learning even after success. Read about the industry, attend seminars, exchange ideas, and deliberately search for better methods. The mechanism is anti-obsolescence.

**34. Learn a Second Language.** Choose a language relevant to customers, markets, geography, or company strategy. The specific language priorities are time-bound; the general rule is to acquire communication capabilities that expand commercial usefulness.

**35. Be a Take-Charge Person.** Initiate rather than wait passively. Take ownership of problems and create momentum. The authors interpret initiative as a visible signal of leadership potential.

**36. Eagerly Accept Any Offer to Be Compensated on a Performance Basis.** The author argues that strong performers should welcome pay tied to results because it signals confidence and lets output determine earnings. This is an author heuristic, not a universally superior compensation model; risk-sharing, measurement quality, and job type matter.

**37. Eight Things Your Boss Won't Appreciate.** Avoid visible countdowns to vacation/retirement, provocative office reading that sends an unwanted signal, chain letters, selling side products to the boss, recruiting coworkers into side ventures, abusing casual-dress norms, overly familiar endearments, and excessive personal calls. The deeper theme is boundary judgment.

**38. See the Big Picture, but Pay Attention to Details.** Hold the long-term goal while executing the small steps that make it real. Strategic perspective without operational follow-through is empty; detail without direction is equally dangerous.

**39. Share Your Boss's Priorities.** Learn what the boss treats as important—short-term profits, long-term growth, punctuality, formality, detail, customer service, etc.—and align effort accordingly. Different bosses weight the same work differently.

**40. Subscribe to The Wall Street Journal.** Original tactic: read a major business newspaper, especially if the boss does. Generalizable rule: maintain broad business literacy and track developments in your industry and competitors.

**41. Learn from Your Mistakes.** Expect errors in risk-taking work and treat them as feedback. The important variable is not a zero-error record but whether the person admits, learns, and changes behavior.

**42. Ask Questions.** Professionals probe before acting. Use questions to clarify expected outcomes, constraints, and assumptions. Trying to look knowledgeable by not asking can be more costly than displaying temporary uncertainty.

**43. Be Visible.** Produce excellent work first, then ensure it is discoverable. Useful visibility channels include customer praise, internal publications, cross-functional work, community leadership, mentor advocacy, and selective self-reporting. Avoid grandstanding.

**44. Keep Yourself in Good Physical Condition.** The book links fitness to stamina, confidence, appearance, alertness, and lower absenteeism. Treat the specific medical claims as author assertions; the practical recommendation is to protect health and energy because depleted capacity impairs work.

**45. Never Get into an Argument with Your Boss.** Do not turn disagreement into a quarrel or repeated devil's advocacy. You can disagree, but an adversarial power struggle is unlikely to improve the decision and can damage the relationship.

**46. Don't Try to Impress Your Boss with Constant Conversation.** Being informative is different from occupying the boss's attention. Limit conversation to matters that add value; repeated unsolicited commentary can become interruption rather than insight.

**47. Send Your Boss an Occasional News Article.** Share a relevant article with a short personal note when it genuinely fits the boss's interests or business concerns. Do it sparingly; overuse turns thoughtfulness into noise.

**48. Polish Your Writing Skills.** Weak writing can cap advancement because memos, proposals, and correspondence become evidence of judgment and clarity. Use courses, books, editing help, or tutoring if necessary.

**49. Be Loyal to Your Boss.** The book recommends not criticizing the boss behind their back, defending them against unfair attacks, and disagreeing privately. This reflects its strong loyalty ethic; it should not be interpreted as a requirement to conceal misconduct or suppress protected concerns.

**50. Practice Common Courtesy with Everyone.** Treat people respectfully regardless of rank. Courtesy to lower-status workers is especially diagnostic because it reveals whether civility is genuine or strategic.

**51. Realize That the Squeaky Wheel Does Not Always Get the Grease.** Chronic complaining can make a person easier to dismiss rather than harder to ignore. Influence the boss with constructive arguments rather than repetitive pressure and negativity.

**52. Admit Your Mistakes.** Surface errors before someone else does, take responsibility, and correct them while damage is containable. Concealment creates a second problem: loss of trust.

**53. Watch Your Body Language.** Respect is communicated nonverbally. Avoid crowding, trapping people physically, slumping, looking around while they speak, restless legs, or fiddling with objects. Use attentive posture and visual focus.

**54. Stay Current.** Keep pace with industry, culture, tools, and new methods. Being “out of touch” reduces credibility and makes others doubt your capacity to lead future-oriented work.

**55. Accept the Blame, Even When It's Not Your Fault.** The book sometimes advises quietly taking the heat when the boss uses you as cover, expecting later appreciation. This is one of its most problematic recommendations: it may reward deception, distort accountability, or expose the employee to formal risk. Preserve it as an author-specific tactic, not as a general best practice.

### Tips 56–85: effort, feedback, relationships, culture, and self-management

**56. Work Overtime.** The book treats voluntary long hours as a signal of ambition, loyalty, and commitment, particularly for inexperienced employees. It specifically warns against complaining about sacrificed personal time. This reflects a 1998 executive-work norm; modern application should separate genuine surge effort from chronic overwork, exploitation, or burnout.

**57. Be a Good Sport and Occasionally Pick Up the Tab.** On nonbusiness meals with the boss, do not assume the senior person must always pay. Occasionally offer to cover the meal, especially if you invited the boss for a personal occasion. Mechanism: reciprocity and avoiding entitlement.

**58. Don't Fake It—Be Sure You Understand the Assignment.** If an assignment is unclear, say so before beginning. Asking for a few minutes of clarification can prevent hours or days of rework. Protecting face by pretending to understand is framed as false pride that costs the employer resources.

**59. Put Yourself in Your Boss's Shoes.** Perspective-take deliberately. Ask what you would do if responsible for the entire decision, including employee needs, customer demands, constraints, and profitability. This reduces simplistic judgments about managerial choices.

**60. Send Your Boss a Complimentary Memo.** Occasionally put sincere praise in writing. A short note gives the boss recognition that can be revisited later. The authors stress sincerity; otherwise the tactic becomes obvious ingratiation.

**61. If You Can't Say Something Nice About Someone, Don't Say Anything.** Avoid habitual badmouthing and gossip. The book's mechanism is both social and strategic: criticism creates animosity, weakens teamwork, and tells the boss that you may also gossip about confidential matters or about the boss.

**62. Keep Your Private Life Private.** The book recommends keeping personal problems—and even much personal good news—out of work, both to protect a strong professional image and to avoid distracting others. This is a particularly rigid 1998 boundary norm; modern teams often permit appropriate personal disclosure, but selective boundaries remain useful.

**63. Accept Criticism Graciously.** Assuming a reasonable boss and work-related feedback, do not react defensively. Acknowledge the criticism, delay your response if emotionally activated, consider the content, thank the boss for useful feedback, and improve the behavior. Distinguish criticism of work from rejection of self.

**64. Learn to Read Your Boss's Moods and Recognize That Timing Is Essential.** Do not raise a salary request, promotion, pet project, or other demanding topic when the boss is rushing, behind schedule, preparing a major speech, freshly criticized, in conflict, losing key staff, or entering/leaving a vacation. Same request + different timing can produce different results.

**65. Get in the Habit of Saying “We” Instead of “I.”** Use collective language for shared problems and successes. “We” recruits others into ownership, shares credit, and can make change easier because people support ideas they helped create.

**66. Have a Passion for Your Job.** The book argues that genuine enthusiasm increases energy, productivity, persistence, and social contagion. If work is chronically hated, the author suggests the mismatch drains energy and well-being. Passion is treated as both an internal performance resource and an external signal.

**67. Seek a Mentor Your Boss Respects.** Choose an experienced adviser whose judgment carries weight with the boss. If that is unavailable, consider asking the boss to mentor you. Listening to the boss's career story also reveals what qualities the boss admires and rewards.

**68. Learn to Work Around Your Boss's Idiosyncrasies.** Stop expecting recurring quirks to disappear. Identify predictable triggers and preferences, then adapt where the cost is reasonable. This is a pragmatic acceptance strategy rather than an attempt to reform personality.

**69. Pay Close Attention to Your Company's Unwritten Rules.** Observe conventions that are culturally enforced even if no policy states them: response times, forms of address, customer rituals, dress, meeting norms, or decision customs. Also notice written rules that are routinely ignored. The real operating system is partly informal.

**70. Seek Increased Responsibility.** Do not wait for advancement while performing only the current minimum. Volunteer for additional assignments, help train others, or create useful responsibilities. The book treats expanded responsibility as both evidence and rehearsal for promotion; workload limits still matter.

**71. If a Joke Is Not Funny and Not Offensive, Laugh Anyway.** The authors recommend at least a warm smile to prevent the awkwardness of an unreceived joke, especially from the boss. The underlying mechanism is social smoothing; the tactic sits near the line between courtesy and inauthentic ingratiation.

**72. Make Business Lunches Productive.** Use an agenda, start important discussion early, and avoid consuming the entire meal with small talk or menu decisions. The goal is to treat lunch as a business meeting in a relaxed setting rather than a meeting that accidentally runs out of time.

**73. Be Sure to Attend Your Boss's Big Speech.** If the event is unusual and important, show up as a supportive face. Offer to listen to a rehearsal, provide constructive input before the event, and afterward find something sincere to acknowledge. The tactic targets emotional support during a high-stakes moment.

**74. When a VIP Enters the Room, Stand Up to Shake Hands.** The book treats standing as a traditional sign of respect and protocol. This is culturally and temporally contingent; the broader idea is to understand etiquette conventions that communicate respect in the relevant setting.

**75. Take a Stand When Necessary.** Supporting the boss does not mean becoming a yes-person. Offer alternatives and state convictions when something matters. Distinguish principles, where firmness may be necessary, from matters of taste where compromise is easier.

**76. Have a Clear Understanding of Your Company's Values.** Identify both stated and enacted values. If management says one thing but repeatedly does another, behavior is the more reliable evidence. The authors advise leaving if core personal and company values are fundamentally incompatible.

**77. Be a Team Player.** Optimize for the shared goal rather than individual credit. Use your role and special skills to help the group win. Ego-driven attempts to monopolize recognition weaken collaboration and therefore reduce value to the boss.

**78. Be Coachable.** Maintain a desire to learn, give the boss some presumption of competence, ask questions, listen, remain flexible, and avoid reflexive second-guessing. The book treats coachability as evidence of growth potential rather than mere obedience.

**79. Understand That Life Isn't Fair.** Promotions, layoffs, bonuses, and opportunities may be influenced by factors outside merit or control. The book's intended lesson is resilience: do not let every unfair outcome become an identity wound. However, the advice can become harmful if used to normalize discrimination, abuse, or violations that should be challenged.

**80. Get a Life (After Work).** Work performance benefits from recovery, play, relationships, and varied experiences. The book recommends walks, exercise, healthy lunches away from the office, short getaways, calling friends, scenic variation, restaurants, films/books, museums, and concerts. Some brain-chemistry/health claims are asserted without detailed evidence.

**81. Be a Good Backup Person for Your Boss.** Learn enough to cover important functions when the boss is absent, make reasonable decisions independently, and protect continuity. This can make the employee indispensable. The book also advocates “blind loyalty” in defending the boss; that part should not override integrity or factual accuracy.

**82. Don't Just Complain—Offer Solutions.** When surfacing a problem, bring research, alternatives, or a proposed fix. Problem identification alone is often information the boss already has; solution-generation converts complaint into contribution.

**83. Ask for Periodic Performance Reviews.** Do not wait until year-end to discover that performance is off course. Schedule interim feedback, document achievements, and correct issues early. The shorter the feedback loop, the cheaper the course correction.

**84. Motivate the Motivator.** Bosses also experience setbacks and doubt. Notice when the boss needs encouragement and offer specific, credible support. The employee becomes more valuable by contributing emotional energy rather than only consuming it.

**85. Control Your Anger.** Diagnose the trigger, avoid impulsive reaction, cool down before speaking, identify situations that reliably provoke you, monitor bodily signs of arousal, distinguish the current trigger from displaced emotion, check fatigue/hunger/mood, and return with a constructive solution.

### Tips 86–110: communication, information, social skill, and organizational awareness

**86. Never Ask Your Boss Tough Questions During an Important Presentation Unless You Know He Can Answer Them.** Do not use public Q&A to display your cleverness at the boss's expense. If a surprise question could embarrass the boss, raise it privately. The mechanism is face protection, though modern high-integrity contexts may require difficult public questions when stakes demand them.

**87. Seek Simplicity.** Prefer clear language and uncomplicated solutions over jargon, acronyms, or complexity used to display expertise. Communication succeeds when the receiver understands; sophistication that obstructs comprehension is failure.

**88. Make a Generous Donation During Your Company's United Way Fund-Raising Drive.** The author describes charitable participation and leadership of company campaigns as signals of citizenship and team spirit, and explicitly admits judging employees partly by donation levels. This is a highly dated and potentially coercive norm; charitable giving should not be treated as a legitimate employment-performance metric.

**89. If You Share a Secretary, Don't Monopolize Her Time.** Coordinate shared administrative support fairly, respect others' priorities, do not assign personal errands, and self-serve when the assistant is overloaded. The terminology reflects the period; the general rule is not to treat shared support capacity as exclusively yours.

**90. Improve Your Listening Skills.** Make eye contact, concentrate, be willing to learn, listen for information and rapport, notice phrases that reveal wants/needs/problems, do not interrupt, pause before replying, and signal attention. Listening is treated as at least as important as speaking.

**91. Don't Spill Your Guts to the Company Therapist.** The book distinguishes an employer-paid evaluator from a private therapist. If the professional's role is to assess promotion readiness or report findings to management, do not assume ordinary therapeutic confidentiality. Modern specifics depend on role, consent, law, and policy; always clarify confidentiality boundaries rather than relying on this book's broad rule.

**92. Manage Your Time Effectively.** Build buffer for unexpected work; focus on one task at a time; define precise deadlines; use specific appointment times; handle documents once when possible; limit phone duration; keep and prioritize a written to-do list. Time management is treated as direct stewardship of employer resources.

**93. When You Have a Great Idea, Tell Your Boss Before Anyone Else.** Give the boss first access rather than bypassing the hierarchy. The book even advises letting go if the boss receives more credit than you would like, provided the boss knows the idea originated with you. That credit advice is highly context-dependent.

**94. Increase Your Walking Pace.** The author claims that brisk walking creates an impression of purpose, urgency, and energy. This is primarily an image-management heuristic with weak evidentiary grounding; its general form is that visible tempo can become a social cue about engagement.

**95. Learn the Names of Customers, Coworkers, Members of the Boss's Family, and the Boss's Friends.** Remembering names communicates attention and makes important people feel recognized. If you forget a name, ask again rather than pretending. The book treats relational memory as a small investment with high goodwill return.

**96. Strive to Understand How the Whole Company Works.** Avoid departmental tunnel vision. Broader knowledge increases mobility, reveals how your role connects to the system, and enables “cross-pollination” of ideas between functions.

**97. Get into the Habit of Speaking Up.** Expertise that stays silent cannot help the organization. Rehearse your point, use notes, stay on the main issue, listen to counterarguments, integrate useful ideas, and make room for quieter people. The book frames speaking up as a responsibility, not merely a personality preference.

**98. Always Express Gratitude When Your Boss Does Something on Your Behalf.** Thank the boss for both major and small acts: raises, promotions, invitations, opportunities, or requests for input. The authors explicitly predict reciprocity—people tend to do more for those who appreciate them.

**99. Express Congratulations or Sympathy When Appropriate.** Acknowledge others' achievements, losses, and disappointments. The book treats sincere congratulations as evidence of self-confidence and team orientation because another person's success does not threaten you.

**100. Read the Publications Your Boss Reads.** Track the trade journals, industry publications, and information sources that shape the boss's worldview. This creates shared context and keeps the employee current on trends, competitors, and emerging issues.

**101. Return Phone Calls Promptly.** Responsiveness communicates that other people's time and concerns matter. If a prompt return is impossible, arrange a later time and acknowledge the delay rather than silently ignoring the caller.

**102. Be on Time.** Punctuality is treated as respect for other people's time and as a signal of organization/self-control. Chronic lateness creates reputational damage beyond the minutes lost.

**103. Be Sensitive to Others' Problems.** Do not turn the office into therapy, but do not become emotionally indifferent either. Brief concern, support at lunch, or availability after work can preserve humanity and loyalty when colleagues or the boss face personal distress.

**104. Provide Criticism Privately.** Public criticism humiliates and can damage group morale. Give constructive feedback behind closed doors, calmly explain the concern, and listen to the other person's account.

**105. Be Modest.** Let results and other people do much of the talking. Bragging can signal insecurity and poor team orientation. This tip creates an intentional tension with Tip 142 (“Never Undersell Yourself”): self-advocacy is needed, but boastfulness is not.

**106. Allow Others to Save Face.** When people already know they made a mistake, avoid unnecessary humiliation. Letting someone retain dignity creates goodwill and reduces resentment.

**107. Keep Business in the Office.** Protect confidential company information in restaurants, public spaces, and home conversations. Do not disclose inside information to make yourself look important. Confidentiality is both a trust and information-security issue.

**108. Keep Small Talk to a Minimum.** Distinguish relationship-building from idle chatter that consumes work time. Use deadlines, time limits, meeting cutoff times, business-focused conversation, standing rather than settling into a long visit, and boundaries on personal calls/messages.

**109. Never Burn Bridges.** Maintain relationships even when someone can no longer help you immediately. Express gratitude, keep in touch, and avoid discarding former allies. Today's junior contact may later become influential, but the deeper principle is durable reciprocity rather than purely transactional networking.

**110. Be Sensitive to Office Politics.** Learn who actually has influence, what precedents govern promotions/bonuses/severance, which committees matter, and where formal titles misrepresent real power. The book advises realism about the shadow organization without explicitly endorsing political manipulation.

### Tips 111–140: boundaries, change, career positioning, and boss support

**111. Do Not Drink.** Avoid alcohol during the workday. The book argues that even mild impairment can hurt alertness and judgment and that there is little career upside. It also advises learning to decline social pressure to drink.

**112. Learn to Compromise.** Most workplace decisions require give and take. Protect core principles, but do not treat every preference as non-negotiable. The authors emphasize that an adequate idea with broad support may be more executable than a technically superior idea nobody accepts.

**113. Don't Try to Be Popular.** Management sometimes requires saying no, rejecting ideas, disciplining performance, or making decisions some people dislike. Likeability is useful, but the need for approval should not prevent necessary action. The book also acknowledges legitimate family boundaries as reasons to decline some boss requests.

**114. Always Use Appropriate, Professional Language.** Avoid profanity, offensive remarks, and unexplained jargon that outsiders cannot understand. The section also contains dated gender stereotypes about women and “femininity”; preserve the useful communication norm without adopting that framing.

**115. Show Loyalty by Keeping Confidences.** If entrusted with sensitive information, protect it. Confidentiality failures destroy trust and may create legal/business risk. Once the boss concludes you cannot keep a confidence, access to important information disappears.

**116. With a New Boss, Find Your Parameters.** Early in the relationship, clarify decision rights and boundaries: which decisions can you make alone, what requires approval, how much initiative is welcome, and what reporting is expected? Prepared questions reduce later friction.

**117. Be Open to New Ideas.** Resist complacency and listen seriously to alternatives. Your idea and another person's idea may combine into something better. Openness also increases reciprocity: people are more likely to consider your ideas when you consider theirs.

**118. Develop Your People Skills.** Technical skill is insufficient for management. Continue improving listening, rapport, empathy, influence, conflict handling, and interpersonal communication. The authors predict that as technology grows, distinctly human relational capability becomes more valuable rather than less.

**119. Take the Initiative in Solving an Irate Customer's Problem.** Treat customer recovery as everyone's job, even when the complaint falls outside your formal remit. Solve the problem without immediately seeking praise; the book prefers the customer to tell management how well you handled it.

**120. Help Your Boss Stay on Schedule.** When the boss is behind on something important, step in where useful and organize your work around the critical need. The principle is to protect high-value managerial time; the book's willingness to normalize overtime is context-dependent.

**121. Don't Rush Through a Job in an Effort to Impress Your Boss.** Efficiency is valuable only if quality survives. Do not cut planning, research, or proofreading merely to finish fast. The authors expect the lasting impression of the output to outweigh the short-term impression of speed.

**122. Remember Special Occasions.** Remember birthdays, anniversaries, awards, illnesses, and other meaningful occasions with cards, notes, lunch, flowers, or modest gifts. This reflects a more personal 1990s office culture; modern appropriateness depends on organizational norms and boundaries.

**123. Tell Your Boss What a Great Day You're Having.** Share positive energy rather than only bringing problems. The author treats mood as socially contagious and believes visible optimism improves climate. The strong causal claims about “positive things happening” are motivational rather than rigorously evidenced.

**124. Maintain a Positive Attitude.** The book links optimism with energy, enjoyment, inspiration, direction, and health. Its robustly reusable point is that persistent negativity carries interpersonal and motivational costs; the physiological claims are asserted rather than demonstrated.

**125. Never Make an Idle Threat to Your Boss.** Do not repeatedly threaten to quit to force a raise, promotion, or concession unless you are prepared to leave. Bluffing erodes trust and may cause the boss to begin replacing you.

**126. Be Reserved at Company Parties.** A company social event is still connected to work. Avoid excessive alcohol, oversharing, unwanted advances, and controversial arguments. Enjoy yourself without doing something that changes colleagues' professional perception of you the next morning.

**127. Stay Focused.** Give the highest-priority task concentrated attention instead of fragmenting effort across everything at once. Break large goals into sequential steps, but keep the long-term objective visible.

**128. Be a Good Corporate Citizen.** Participate in community or charitable activity, preferably deeply in one meaningful cause rather than superficially in many. The author frames such work as civic obligation, corporate reputation-building, networking, and a positive promotion signal. His proposed time commitments are unusually heavy and not a universal standard.

**129. Plan Time Off When It's Good for Your Boss, and Let Her Know in Advance.** Coordinate planned absence early so coverage can be arranged. The book suggests substantial advance notice and even coordinating vacation dates around the boss's schedule. The enduring principle is operational continuity, not subordination of all personal leave to managerial convenience.

**130. Train Someone to Replace You in Case You Get Promoted.** Succession readiness prevents your current indispensability from becoming a promotion barrier. Share knowledge and develop backup capability rather than hoarding it for job security.

**131. Don't Try to Be the Office Peacock.** Dress professionally without making conspicuous fashion display the center of attention. The detailed style rules are period-specific; the general idea is that appearance should support the work rather than become a distraction or status competition.

**132. Befriend Your Boss's Secretary.** The book highlights the influence of the boss's administrative gatekeeper: access, scheduling, context, and informal impressions can all matter. Treat administrative staff with warmth and respect rather than as low-status service providers. The job title and gender assumptions are dated.

**133. Develop a Healthy Belief in the Impossible.** Do not reject difficult goals merely because precedent says they cannot be achieved. The book uses technology and sports breakthroughs to argue that prior limits can be outdated. This is a motivational heuristic, not a reason to ignore physical or economic constraints.

**134. Update Your Spouse or Companion on Pertinent Business Information When Attending a Business Event.** Give a companion enough nonconfidential context to participate comfortably at business functions. The section also claims bosses may judge promotion candidates partly by their spouses, a notably dated and potentially discriminatory assumption.

**135. Know Your Worth.** Before negotiating compensation, research market rates and prepare evidence of accomplishments plus future goals. Argue from business value, not personal expenses. Do not underestimate your value, and leave room to negotiate downward from a defensible opening request.

**136. Remember, Your Boss Is Lonely at the Top.** Senior leaders may be informationally isolated because subordinates filter bad news or say what they think the boss wants to hear. Becoming a trusted source of candid reality can create unusual value and closeness.

**137. If the Company Has Not Yet Developed a Mission Statement, Volunteer to Help Write One.** Study other mission statements, interview people at multiple levels about real values, synthesize recurring themes, and keep the result concise. The exercise is both strategic work and a way to learn the organization deeply.

**138. Don't Become Married to a Particular Project or Idea.** Separate identity from the method you proposed. Listen to alternatives, combine useful elements, and adapt when circumstances change. Rigidity can block both innovation and other people's willingness to consider your ideas.

**139. See the Forest Through the Trees.** Details matter, but not all details deserve equal attention. Use clear objectives and agendas to prevent meetings/projects from being derailed by minor points unrelated to the goal.

**140. Don't Steal the Limelight from Your Boss.** When recognition legitimately belongs to the boss, do not turn the moment into your own performance. The book interprets upstaging as poor etiquette and insecurity. This is distinct from suppressing your achievements altogether.

### Tips 141–169: resilience, management craft, reputation, and bottom-line orientation

**141. Maintain a Sense of Humor.** Humor can create perspective, reduce tension, and help people connect. Use it carefully: avoid material that could offend, and calibrate to a boss who may prefer a more serious environment.

**142. Never Undersell Yourself.** Make sure meaningful accomplishments are known. Accept deserved praise and act as a measured advocate for your own value. Humility that consistently hides evidence of competence can become career self-sabotage.

**143. Never Oversell Yourself.** The counterweight to Tip 142: do not exaggerate capability or boast so much that expectations outrun performance. Give an accurate account of strengths and limitations so assignments fit actual capacity.

**144. Go the Extra Mile.** Build a reputation for doing more than the bare job description when customers or the team need it. The book strongly associates extra effort with promotability and includes overtime as evidence; apply with attention to sustainability and fair workload.

**145. Don't Take Yourself Too Seriously.** Take the work seriously without becoming self-important. Promotions should not destroy approachability. Arrogance reduces communication because others stop engaging candidly.

**146. Ask for Help.** Seek expertise wherever it resides—boss, peer, subordinate, or frontline worker. Asking someone lower in the hierarchy can both solve the problem and signal respect for their competence.

**147. Don't Rely on Intuition Unless You're Absolutely Positive You're Right.** The book treats intuition as accumulated experience expressed through a “gut” signal, but advises using it proportionally to its historical accuracy and the stakes involved. For high-stakes decisions when confidence is weak, use evidence and/or the boss's judgment rather than hunch alone.

**148. Delegate.** Assign lower-value or specialized work to capable people so managerial attention stays on higher-priority tasks. Delegation does not transfer accountability: the quality of delegated work still reflects on the manager. Select and develop strong people.

**149. Don't Shoot the Messenger—Reward Him for Bad News.** Explicitly welcome unfavorable information, ask questions, and verify facts. If messengers are punished, they learn to hide problems and leaders become surrounded by yes-people. Early bad news is often cheaper than late surprise.

**150. Don't Resist Change—Embrace It.** Recognize that refusing change is itself a choice. Adapt to changing products, technology, staff, markets, and structures; ideally anticipate where conditions are going rather than react after the fact.

**151. Develop Your Networking Skills.** Build relationships inside and outside the company, including deliberate contacts who can broaden your career. Record useful contact information and stay in touch. The book encourages collecting personal details; modern practice should respect privacy and avoid treating relationships as a database of exploitable favors.

**152. Never Tolerate a Lack of Integrity.** Management behavior sets moral tone. False claims, unfair treatment, shoddy value, and self-serving executive behavior corrode pride and culture. The book explicitly says never compromise personal integrity.

**153. Never Tolerate Disloyalty.** The authors recommend visibly defending the employer against hostile remarks and interpret silence as possible disloyalty. This is one of the book's strongest loyalty prescriptions and should be bounded by Tip 152: legitimate criticism, whistleblowing, and ethical dissent are not equivalent to betrayal.

**154. Unclutter Your Cluttered Desk.** Organize the workspace to reduce search time and avoid projecting disorder. The book pays special attention to matching a boss who strongly values neatness; the deeper mechanism is operational organization plus visible signaling.

**155. Be Likeable.** Warmth, friendliness, and respect make others more willing to cooperate. The book argues that when qualifications are equal, relationships can become the tiebreaker. Likeability here means pleasant interaction, not avoiding all unpopular decisions.

**156. Don't Be a Creature of Habit.** Experiment with ways to improve routine tasks rather than performing them mindlessly. Variation can reveal better methods and prevent boredom from becoming stagnation.

**157. Surround Yourself with People Who Reflect the Values of Your Company.** When hiring or staffing teams, assess value fit through substantive interviews rather than resumes alone. The authors expect stronger alignment to reduce turnover and friction; a modern caveat is that “value fit” should not become demographic or personality homogeneity.

**158. Know When to Say No.** Avoid the “slow no” that keeps others waiting and wastes time. When the answer is no, say it clearly and explain the reason; decisiveness lets the other party adapt or make a better case.

**159. Treat Your Boss Like You Treat Your Best Customer.** Continuously understand needs, anticipate problems, make the boss's work easier, and help the boss succeed. Do not assume yesterday's performance permanently secures today's value.

**160. Schedule Appointments with Your Boss.** For important matters, request dedicated time rather than dropping in while the boss is distracted. A scheduled meeting signals seriousness, respects time, and lets both sides prepare. Do not dilute the tactic with trivial appointments.

**161. Take Ownership.** Act as if you have a stake in the organization's success even without equity. Pride and stewardship motivate better decisions than an attitude of doing only enough to collect a paycheck.

**162. Inform Your Boss About Your Career Plan.** Write an intermediate (roughly 3–5 year) and long-range (10–20 year) career plan, discuss it with the boss, seek advice, and periodically review progress. Involving the boss can turn career ambition into a shared development project.

**163. Don't Be a Prima Donna.** High performance does not entitle someone to contemptuous behavior, endless exceptions, or policy bending. Eventually a comparably talented team player becomes more attractive to management.

**164. Be Generous with the Gifts You Give Your Boss.** The book recommends reciprocating gifts and choosing thoughtful, hobby-related, useful, not-too-personal items. This is culturally and policy-dependent; many modern organizations restrict gifts to managers because of conflict-of-interest or favoritism concerns.

**165. Never Underestimate the Power of the Grapevine.** Rumors can be distorted, but informal observations sometimes detect operational trouble before leaders do. Do not treat hearsay as fact; use it as a weak signal to investigate. Also recognize how easily confidential information leaks.

**166. Block Out Irrelevant Remarks and Concentrate on What's Important.** Extract the essential issue from rambling conversation and guide meetings back to objectives. This is attentional filtering: acknowledge noise without letting it determine the agenda.

**167. Avoid Awkward Food When Dining Out.** At high-stakes business meals, choose food that is easy to eat neatly and does not make you conspicuous. The specific etiquette is minor, but it illustrates the book's recurring concern with avoiding preventable impression risks.

**168. Don't Allow Failure to Defeat You.** Risk and failure are linked. Learn, regroup, and separate failed outcomes from personal identity. Use rejection and criticism as information where valid; continue pursuing worthwhile goals rather than making one setback permanent.

**169. Go for the Bottom Line.** Understand how your work contributes to financial sustainability. Profit is not portrayed as the only stakeholder goal, but as a necessary condition for serving employees, customers, suppliers, shareholders, and communities over time. Eliminate low-value activity and prioritize work that improves durable business results.

## 5. Diagnostic rules, heuristics, and decision criteria

The book is not built as a diagnostic manual, but its 169 tips imply a large set of “if X, then Y” rules.

### 5.1 When you are unsure what the boss wants

- **If the assignment is ambiguous, ask before acting** rather than fake understanding (#42, #58).
- **If the boss is new, clarify parameters early**: decision rights, approval thresholds, initiative boundaries, and communication expectations (#116).
- **If priorities conflict, infer the boss's ranking explicitly** rather than assuming your own priorities are shared (#39).
- **If the boss frequently behaves in a quirky but stable way, treat the pattern as a constraint to work around** rather than repeatedly expecting it to disappear (#68).

### 5.2 When deciding whether to speak up

- **If relevant expertise is being withheld by fear, speak**; silence can deprive the organization of information (#97).
- **If disagreement is about principle or material risk, take a stand** and offer an alternative (#75).
- **If disagreement is becoming a public status contest, move it private** (#28, #45, #104).
- **If you are tempted to ask a difficult public question mainly to demonstrate cleverness, do not put the boss on the spot** (#86).
- **If you are carrying bad news, deliver it early and factually** (#52, #149).

### 5.3 When deciding whether to ask or act independently

- **At the beginning of a task:** ask enough questions to know the target (#42, #58).
- **During ordinary execution:** research and solve what you reasonably can without repeatedly returning for instructions (#21).
- **If the action crosses an unclear authority boundary:** check the boss's parameters (#116).
- **If the boss is absent and continuity genuinely requires action:** use your role as backup and make the best defensible decision (#81).

### 5.4 When managing visibility

- **If excellent work is invisible, create a legitimate channel for it to be seen** (#17, #43, #142).
- **If self-promotion is becoming the center of the interaction, stop; let results and third parties amplify you** (#105, #119, #143).
- **If recognition belongs to the boss or team, share the spotlight** (#20, #65, #77, #140).

### 5.5 When handling criticism or failure

- **If criticism arrives and emotion spikes, acknowledge it and defer your response until you can think clearly** (#63).
- **If criticism is valid, use it to change behavior** (#41, #63, #83, #168).
- **If a mistake is yours, disclose it before someone else does** (#52).
- **If an idea is rejected, do not automatically infer personal rejection** (#12, #168).

### 5.6 When protecting relationships

- **If criticism must be delivered, do it privately** (#104).
- **If someone makes an error and humiliation adds no value, let them save face** (#106).
- **If resentment persists, address the issue privately rather than sustain a grudge** (#25).
- **If a request deserves a no, give a clear no rather than a prolonged maybe** (#158).
- **If the relationship has no current instrumental value, do not burn the bridge anyway** (#109).

### 5.7 When deciding what information to share

- **If information is confidential, keep it out of public and home conversations** (#107, #115).
- **If a rumor matters, treat it as a lead to verify rather than established fact** (#165).
- **If the boss is informationally isolated, provide candid reality rather than telling only what the boss wants to hear** (#136, #149).
- **If a company-paid evaluator is assessing you for management, clarify the evaluator's reporting/confidentiality role before disclosing sensitive personal information** (#91).

### 5.8 When considering career advancement

- **If you want more responsibility, demonstrate capacity before title** (#19, #35, #70).
- **If you are too indispensable to replace, develop a successor** (#130).
- **If you want a raise, research market value and prepare achievement evidence** (#135).
- **If you want sponsorship, share a concrete career plan with the boss and request feedback** (#162).
- **If your skills are becoming narrow or outdated, expand and refresh them** (#15, #32, #33, #54, #96, #118, #150).

### 5.9 When evaluating organizational fit

- Compare **stated values** with **actual practices** (#76).
- Observe **unwritten rules** and **office politics** before assuming policy documents describe reality (#69, #110).
- If core organizational values fundamentally conflict with your own, the book recommends leaving rather than living in chronic incongruence (#76, #152).

## 6. Exercises, drills, and practices

### 6.1 Meeting preparation checklist (#6)

Before a meeting:
- read the agenda and distributed material;
- prepare key comments;
- gather supporting evidence/data;
- arrive on time or early;
- bring materials and a note-taking method.

During the meeting:
- give speakers full attention;
- maintain appropriate eye contact;
- participate rather than sit unprepared;
- keep remarks relevant;
- control emotion during disagreement;
- do not publicly undermine the boss.

### 6.2 Four questions after an idea is rejected (#12)

Use rejection to gather decision information:
1. What do you think?
2. Is there anything else I should know?
3. What would you do first?
4. How do you see the end result?

Function: shift from hurt/defense to learning the boss's model of the problem.

### 6.3 Boss-priority mapping (#39, #64, #68, #116)

Create a one-page map with:
- top outcomes the boss repeatedly rewards;
- behaviors the boss is unusually sensitive to;
- preferred decision/communication style;
- approval boundaries;
- recurring moods or bad timing windows;
- stable idiosyncrasies;
- information the boss wants proactively;
- topics best handled privately.

Update it as evidence accumulates.

### 6.4 Anger-control protocol (#85)

When activated:
1. Identify the immediate cause.
2. Do not respond impulsively.
3. Decide whether a response is needed.
4. Allow a cooling-off period.
5. Note recurring triggers.
6. Detect bodily arousal signals.
7. Ask whether the reaction belongs to this event or is displaced from another stressor.
8. Check fatigue, hunger, and mood.
9. Return with a constructive solution rather than raw anger.

### 6.5 Coachability practice (#78)

Regularly audit whether you:
- still have a desire to learn;
- can grant that the boss may know something you do not;
- avoid reflexive second-guessing;
- ask enough questions;
- listen before rebutting;
- stay flexible when new information arrives.

### 6.6 Listening drill (#90)

In a conversation, deliberately:
- focus visually on the speaker;
- summarize the main point internally;
- listen for “I want,” “I need,” “I'm looking for,” and “I have a problem with”;
- wait a beat after the other person finishes before speaking;
- prohibit interruption;
- use small signals that show attention.

### 6.7 Time-management system (#92)

- Maintain a prioritized written task list.
- Build buffer for unexpected work.
- Single-task until a sensible stopping point.
- Replace vague deadlines with exact ones.
- Use precise appointment times where useful.
- Process incoming items to a decision rather than repeatedly reshuffling them.
- Put explicit limits on telephone/meeting duration.

### 6.8 Speaking-up drill (#97)

Before raising an important point:
- mentally rehearse it;
- use notes if useful;
- state the main point first;
- do not let emotional detours pull you away from it;
- listen for information that should modify your position;
- deliberately invite quieter people into the discussion.

### 6.9 “Slow no” prevention (#158)

When you know the answer is no:
- make the decision promptly;
- state it clearly;
- provide the reason;
- allow the other person to restate the case if new evidence exists;
- do not use “maybe” merely to avoid discomfort.

### 6.10 Mission-statement exercise (#137)

- Collect examples from other organizations.
- Interview people across levels about what the company actually values.
- Identify recurring themes.
- Draft a concise statement that captures purpose/values.
- Test whether actual management decisions are consistent with it.

### 6.11 Raise-preparation exercise (#135)

Before asking for more pay:
- research the market value of comparable work;
- document measurable achievements;
- identify responsibilities already performed above level;
- state future goals/value you expect to create;
- choose a defensible opening number;
- avoid using personal expenses as the business justification.

### 6.12 Career-plan exercise (#162)

Write:
- a 3–5 year target state;
- a 10–20 year direction;
- skills/roles needed to get there;
- likely next steps;
- risks/gaps.

Then discuss the plan with the boss, request feedback, and periodically review progress.

### 6.13 Networking maintenance system (#109, #151)

For meaningful contacts:
- record role/contact information and legitimate context about interests;
- follow up after meeting;
- occasionally share a relevant article or congratulations;
- maintain relationships when no immediate favor is needed;
- diversify contacts inside/outside the company.

Use modern privacy judgment: relationship-building should not become covert collection of unnecessary personal data.

## 7. Failure modes, warnings, and anti-patterns

### 7.1 The “minimum job description” trap

The authors repeatedly warn against doing only what the written role explicitly requires. Failure mode: literal scope compliance without ownership → missed customer/team needs → low perceived initiative → weak advancement prospects. Their remedy is responsible extra-role behavior. Modern boundary: “extra” should not mean accepting structurally exploitative workload indefinitely.

### 7.2 Overwork as identity

Tips 2, 56, 70, 120, 128, and 144 can collectively produce a dangerous reading: more hours always equals more commitment. Yet Tip 80 explicitly argues for recovery and life outside work. The coherent version is **surge when value demands it, but maintain enough recovery to remain effective**. The book itself does not fully resolve this tension.

### 7.3 Loyalty without integrity

Tips 49, 55, 81, 93, and 153 can push toward protecting the boss even when facts are uncomfortable. Tip 152 says integrity must never be compromised. The safer conceptual boundary is: loyalty can mean fair representation, discretion, and support; it does not require lying, accepting false blame, hiding wrongdoing, or suppressing legitimate escalation.

### 7.4 Ingratiation masquerading as relationship skill

Praise, complimentary memos, laughter at weak jokes, gifts, remembering occasions, supporting speeches, and attention to the boss's preferences can become manipulative if performed without sincerity. The book occasionally recognizes this: flattery ceases to work when it looks phony. Later synthesis should separate **genuine prosocial behavior** from **instrumental sycophancy**.

### 7.5 Invisible excellence

Assumption: great work will inevitably be noticed. The book itself contradicts this through Tips 17, 43, 142, and 162. Failure mode: strong contribution + zero visibility → weak recognition. Remedy: build evidence-based visibility without bragging.

### 7.6 Self-promotion overload

The reverse failure mode is treating every action as an opportunity to broadcast excellence. This creates “office peacock,” prima-donna, bragging, and overselling problems. Remedy: let work, customers, mentors, and results carry much of the message.

### 7.7 Chronic complaining

Complaining without solutions, repeated “squeaky wheel” pressure, gossip, and idle negativity create a reputation cost independent of whether the underlying issue is real. Remedy: distinguish problem reporting from emotional repetition; add facts, options, and proposed action.

### 7.8 Public ego contests

Publicly confronting the boss, arguing, upstaging, surprise questioning, or criticism can convert a substantive issue into a status struggle. Remedy: move the difficult content into a private, prepared conversation when possible.

### 7.9 Fake certainty

Pretending to understand, refusing help, relying on a hunch without calibration, or avoiding questions to look competent creates hidden error risk. Remedy: clarify, ask, verify, and use expertise wherever it exists.

### 7.10 Perfectionism and overprocessing

Polishing work beyond the point of value wastes scarce attention. Conversely, rushing sacrifices quality. The optimum is neither “perfect” nor “fastest”; it is sufficient quality at the appropriate cost and deadline.

### 7.11 Information hoarding and filtering

When people punish bad news, hide mistakes, gossip, or keep key knowledge only to protect their own job, the organization becomes less adaptive. The book favors open upward sensing combined with strict confidentiality where information is legitimately sensitive.

### 7.12 Unwritten-rule blindness

A technically competent employee can fail by ignoring cultural expectations, real influence structures, or precedents. The book treats organizational literacy as a professional skill, not office trivia.

### 7.13 Dated or high-risk advice to treat cautiously

Particularly context-dependent items include:
- accepting blame for the boss's mistake (#55);
- normalizing chronic unpaid overtime (#56, #144);
- judging employees by charitable giving (#88);
- treating company-paid psychological evaluation with broad suspicion without first clarifying the professional's actual role (#91);
- advice that effectively says never bypass the chain of command (#26), which cannot supersede legitimate ethics/safety/reporting channels;
- strong norms of keeping virtually all personal life private (#62);
- traditional etiquette/gender/spouse assumptions (#74, #114, #134);
- gifting the boss (#122, #164), which may violate modern policy;
- “blind loyalty” language (#81) and strong disloyalty policing (#153).

## 8. Important distinctions and taxonomies

### Customer vs. boss
- **Customer:** ultimate economic source of the firm's viability.
- **Boss:** immediate organizational evaluator and allocator of opportunity.
- **Why it matters:** pleasing the boss without serving customers is fragile; serving customers while making the boss's job harder can also limit advancement. The book tries to align the two.

### Praise vs. flattery
- **Praise:** sincere recognition tied to something real.
- **Flattery:** exaggerated or instrumental approval.
- **Critical difference:** credibility. Overuse makes even sincere praise suspect.

### Likeable vs. popular
- **Likeable:** respectful, warm, cooperative, pleasant to work with (#155).
- **Popular:** avoiding decisions that upset anyone (#113).
- **Critical difference:** a likeable person can still say no or make unpopular decisions.

### Modesty vs. underselling
- **Modesty:** not monopolizing credit or bragging (#105).
- **Underselling:** allowing meaningful achievements to remain invisible (#142).
- **Critical difference:** communicate evidence of value without making self-celebration the main event.

### Self-promotion vs. overselling
- **Self-promotion:** making genuine achievements discoverable.
- **Overselling:** exaggerating capability or promising more than can be delivered.
- **Why it matters:** overselling creates assignments you cannot execute and destroys trust.

### Dissent vs. argument
- **Dissent:** relevant alternative, respectfully stated (#75, #97).
- **Argument:** adversarial attempt to defeat the boss in a power contest (#45).
- **Critical difference:** dissent improves the decision; argument often shifts attention to winning.

### Small talk vs. networking
- **Small talk:** conversation with no meaningful relational/business purpose in the book's strict framing (#108).
- **Networking:** deliberate long-term relationship-building and mutual usefulness (#109, #151).
- **Caveat:** the book underestimates how ordinary social conversation can itself create trust.

### Big picture vs. detail
- **Big picture:** objective, strategy, destination.
- **Detail:** operational steps that realize it.
- **Failure on either side:** strategy without execution or detail without direction (#38, #139).

### Speed vs. haste
- **Speed:** efficient use of time.
- **Haste:** sacrificing planning, research, or quality merely to finish first (#121).

### Flexibility vs. lack of principle
- **Flexibility:** compromise and adaptation on method/preference (#112, #117, #138, #150).
- **Principle:** values that should not be traded away (#75, #152).

### Informal signal vs. verified fact
- **Grapevine information:** potentially useful early signal (#165).
- **Verified information:** evidence sufficient for action.
- **Critical difference:** listen to rumors without automatically believing them.

### Delegation vs. abdication
- **Delegation:** transfer execution while retaining responsibility (#148).
- **Abdication:** assuming the subordinate's error is no longer yours.
- **Why it matters:** the manager remains accountable for people selected, instructions given, and follow-up.

### Failure vs. identity
- **Failure:** an outcome from an attempt.
- **Being a failure:** global identity conclusion.
- **Critical difference:** the first can produce learning; the second encourages withdrawal (#168).

## 9. Evidence and notable claims

### 9.1 Source basis

The introduction says Alan Schonberg began by surveying roughly **3,500 Management Recruiters International professionals** who interacted daily with bosses and job seekers, then also sought input from client bosses including CEOs, senior managers, and HR vice presidents. The result is presented as a collection of field-tested expert opinions rather than a formal research study.

### 9.2 Evidence categories in the book

**Practitioner/survey heuristics:** Most of the 169 recommendations fall here. They reflect accumulated executive and recruiting judgment about what bosses notice and reward.

**Anecdotal/historical illustrations:** Thomas Edison, Roger Bannister, Wayne Gretzky, Abraham Lincoln, corporate name changes, and other stories are used to make principles memorable. They are examples, not causal evidence that the tactic guarantees career advancement.

**Behavioral/common-sense mechanisms:** Several claims have plausible mechanisms even though the book does not formally test them—for example, punctuality signals respect, early error disclosure enables earlier correction, or punishing bad-news messengers suppresses information.

**Normative recommendations:** Many rules are statements about what a “good” employee should do: loyalty, community service, hard work, courtesy, modesty, discretion, and support of the boss.

### 9.3 Notable empirical-sounding claims that are not substantiated in detail

The book presents as fact or near-fact claims such as:
- physical fitness increasing alertness and mental responsiveness;
- play changing brain chemistry and reducing physical symptoms;
- positive thinking producing energy and better health;
- humor producing beneficial physiological changes;
- physically unfit people being more illness-prone;
- certain social behaviors reliably influencing promotion.

The text generally does not supply citations, effect sizes, study designs, or boundaries for these claims. They should therefore be stored as **claims made by the book**, not as externally verified conclusions.

### 9.4 Historical/contextual evidence status

Several tactics are inseparable from 1998 corporate life: voicemail/answering machines, printed newspaper clippings, company newsletters, secretaries as gatekeepers, conservative dress codes, United Way corporate drives, and expectations around spouses at business events. Their mechanisms—responsiveness, visibility, information sharing, gatekeeper relations, cultural fit—may remain reusable even where the implementation is obsolete.

### 9.5 Tensions that should survive later synthesis

The book contains real internal contradictions rather than a perfectly unified doctrine:
- **Work overtime** vs. **get a life after work**.
- **Be loyal / protect the boss** vs. **never compromise integrity**.
- **Be modest** vs. **never undersell yourself**.
- **Support the boss** vs. **take a stand**.
- **Respect chain of command** vs. **speak up and deliver bad news**.
- **Keep private life private** vs. **be sensitive to others' personal problems**.

These are best treated as calibration problems, not errors to delete.

## 10. Distinctive contributions of this book

### 10.1 Boss as an internal customer

The “treat your boss like your best customer” frame unifies many otherwise scattered tactics. It converts managing up from obedience into continuous value delivery: learn needs, reduce friction, anticipate problems, and prove value repeatedly.

### 10.2 Micro-behavioral reputation architecture

Rather than relying on a few grand career moves, the book catalogs tiny behaviors that cumulatively shape trust: note-taking, returning calls, timing, introductions, gratitude, dining etiquette, confidentiality, punctuality, and follow-through. This high granularity is especially useful for later skill-tree construction.

### 10.3 Formal + informal organization

The combination of chain of command, unwritten rules, office politics, gatekeepers, company values, and grapevine information creates a practical model of organizations as more than formal structures.

### 10.4 Reduced-managerial-burden principle

Many tips can be compressed into: **become the person who requires less supervision while giving the boss better information and fewer surprises.** This is a powerful reusable managerial mechanism.

### 10.5 Face and status management

The book pays unusual attention to how public disagreement, upstaging, surprise questions, praise, private criticism, and credit-sharing affect status. Some of this is overly deferential, but the underlying insight—that perceived status threat alters receptivity—is valuable.

### 10.6 Calibration pairs

Several seemingly contradictory tip pairs define useful skill boundaries:
- visible, not boastful;
- confident, not oversold;
- loyal, not unethical;
- assertive, not argumentative;
- flexible, not unprincipled;
- hard-working, not permanently work-absorbed;
- independent, but willing to ask for help.

### 10.7 Promotion as a systems problem

The book recognizes that promotion depends on more than current output. The employee must have visibility, breadth, networks, sponsor support, marketable skills, and a successor who can absorb the current role.

### 10.8 The boss's information isolation

Tips 136 and 149 together form a useful leadership insight: bosses can become isolated when people filter reality; employees gain value by being safe, credible channels for uncomfortable information.

## 11. Integrated conceptual map

### 11.1 Main value loop

**Customer needs**  
→ define what the company must accomplish  
→ **company strategy/values/priorities**  
→ shape the boss's goals and pressures  
→ **employee learns boss + organization**  
→ clarifies assignments, prioritizes, prepares, executes, communicates  
→ **reliable performance + fewer surprises**  
→ boss spends less time supervising/correcting  
→ **trust and autonomy increase**  
→ employee receives more responsibility and visibility  
→ **career capital grows**  
→ employee can create more customer/business value.

### 11.2 Reputation loop

**Small observable behavior** (punctuality, courtesy, follow-through, discretion, preparation)  
→ repeated signal about competence/character  
→ boss forms expectation (“this person is reliable / risky / thoughtful / difficult”)  
→ boss allocates access, information, assignments, and trust accordingly  
→ those opportunities generate further evidence  
→ reputation strengthens.

### 11.3 Information loop

**Employee asks questions + listens + reports bad news + admits mistakes**  
→ boss receives more accurate information earlier  
→ problems are corrected sooner  
→ fewer unpleasant surprises  
→ boss trusts employee more  
→ boss delegates more and shares more context  
→ employee's judgment improves.

The negative version is: punish bad news / hide mistakes / gossip / filter reality → weak sensing → late surprise → distrust → more control and less autonomy.

### 11.4 Career-growth loop

**Continuous learning + broader organizational knowledge + network + mentor + feedback**  
→ higher capability and opportunity awareness  
→ stronger performance/visibility  
→ more responsibility  
→ new experiences/skills  
→ greater marketability and advancement options.

### 11.5 Relationship-regulation loop

**Boss mood/status/priority is read accurately**  
→ employee chooses better timing, brevity, and delivery  
→ boss becomes less defensive/interrupted  
→ employee's ideas receive fairer consideration  
→ relationship develops trust  
→ more candid disagreement and autonomy become possible.

### 11.6 Core intervention points

If the boss relationship is not working, the book would typically ask:
1. Am I creating enough customer/business value?
2. Am I reliable and easy to supervise?
3. Do I understand the boss's actual priorities and boundaries?
4. Am I communicating clearly, briefly, and at the right time?
5. Am I visible enough without looking self-centered?
6. Am I protecting trust, confidentiality, and dignity?
7. Am I adapting and keeping my capabilities current?
8. Do I have networks, mentors, and career options?
9. Is the real problem a mismatch in values or integrity rather than technique?

The book's deepest reusable message is not “flatter the boss.” It is that **managing upward is an ongoing design problem involving value, reliability, information, social perception, power, timing, and career optionality.**


## 12. Atomic knowledge inventory

This section is intentionally redundant. It is a dense retrieval index for later multi-book synthesis: each numbered item preserves the book's original tip identity while reducing it to its central actionable knowledge unit.

### 12.1 Overarching concepts and mechanisms

- **Framework — Boss as customer.** Treat the direct boss as an internal customer whose recurring needs, constraints, priorities, and evaluation criteria must be understood and served without abandoning ethics or organizational purpose.
- **Framework — Customer as ultimate boss.** The book repeatedly traces job security and company survival back to the ability to create and retain paying customers.
- **Principle — Make the boss's work easier.** Reduce avoidable supervision, follow through, prepare well, communicate clearly, solve problems, and protect the boss from preventable surprises.
- **Principle — Help the boss look effective.** Produce work that improves team results and allows the boss to credibly present the unit as competent; do not confuse this with deception or surrendering deserved credit.
- **Mechanism — Reputation compounds.** Punctuality, courtesy, discretion, responsiveness, preparedness, reliability, judgment, and small acts of ownership accumulate into a durable trust signal.
- **Mechanism — Trust expands autonomy.** Reliable delivery and sound judgment lower perceived supervision risk, which can lead to more discretion, responsibility, and advancement.
- **Mechanism — Visibility converts contribution into career capital.** Valuable work must become legible to decision makers through results, participation, communication, customers, writing, and cross-functional contribution.
- **Distinction — Visibility vs. self-promotion.** The book favors being seen for useful contribution while warning against monopolizing attention, exaggerating accomplishments, or stealing the boss's spotlight.
- **Principle — Align with priorities before optimizing locally.** Learn what the boss and organization actually value so that technically good work is directed at the outcomes that matter.
- **Principle — Manage attention as a scarce resource.** Brevity, agendas, preparation, focus, simplicity, time management, and stopping when marginal value disappears protect managerial and organizational attention.
- **Principle — Pair big-picture thinking with execution detail.** Understand the whole system and bottom line while still completing the specific tasks that make strategy real.
- **Principle — Build transferable career security.** Continuous learning, technology competence, communication, professional networks, breadth, adaptability, and current industry knowledge reduce dependence on one job.
- **Principle — Learn the informal organization.** Formal charts matter, but unwritten rules, office politics, gatekeepers, informal networks, and the grapevine shape how work actually gets done.
- **Principle — Protect dignity during disagreement.** Challenge ideas privately and constructively, allow people to save face, control anger, and avoid converting substantive disagreements into public status contests.
- **Principle — Loyalty has an ethical boundary.** Confidentiality, support, and not undermining the boss are valued, but the book also says not to tolerate lack of integrity; loyalty should not require dishonesty or misconduct.
- **Principle — Initiative should reduce rather than create managerial burden.** Bring solutions, take ownership, solve customer problems, and act within understood parameters rather than generating avoidable cleanup work.
- **Heuristic — When uncertain, clarify before executing.** Questions, notes, examples, performance reviews, scheduled meetings, and explicit parameters reduce preventable errors.
- **Heuristic — When change arrives, learn before resisting.** Adaptability is treated as a core career skill; understand the new requirement, build the needed capability, and avoid defining yourself by the old method.
- **Failure mode — Career-limiting self-focus.** Chronic complaining, grudges, gossip, popularity seeking, peacocking, prima-donna behavior, overselling, and treating every corporate event as “what's in it for me?” weaken trust.
- **Failure mode — False busyness.** Long hours, perfect polishing, endless meetings, conversation, clutter, or activity are not substitutes for relevant output and bottom-line contribution.
- **Evidence status — Practitioner heuristics.** The 169 recommendations are presented as advice informed by the authors' surveys, executive experience, anecdotes, and observation; the book does not establish each tip through controlled causal research.

### 12.2 The 169 tips as atomic units

- **Tactic 1 — Be Customer Driven.** Trace your work to the end customer, even if you never interact with customers directly.
- **Tactic 2 — Remember That Time Is Money.** Treat paid time as an economic resource rather than an entitlement.
- **Tactic 3 — Look the Part.** Dress appropriately for the role, industry, and level you aspire to.
- **Tactic 4 — Read Your Company's Annual Report from Cover to Cover.** Study the annual report, footnotes, and—where relevant—public filings such as the 10-K.
- **Tactic 5 — Learn to Handle a Computer.** Original 1998 recommendation: computer literacy is a baseline employability skill at every level.
- **Tactic 6 — Be Prepared When You Attend a Meeting.** Read the agenda/materials, prepare comments and supporting data, arrive on time, take notes, attend to speakers, stay relevant, control emotion, and do not undermine the boss publicly.
- **Tactic 7 — Deliver More than You Promise.** Keep commitments first, then exceed them where realistic: beat rather than merely meet deadlines or quotas.
- **Tactic 8 — Promote and Use Your Company's Products.** Where reasonable, use and advocate for the employer's offerings.
- **Tactic 9 — Be Prudent with Your Expense Account.** Spend company money as carefully as your own.
- **Tactic 10 — Be a Good Note Taker.** Bring a notebook to conversations with the boss and meetings.
- **Tactic 11 — Never Say Anything Negative About Your Former Company or Boss.** The book views badmouthing past employers as a reputational warning: the current boss may infer that you are difficult or will later criticize them too.
- **Tactic 12 — If Your Boss Turns Down Your Suggestion, Don't Take It Personally.** Distinguish rejection of an idea from rejection of self.
- **Tactic 13 — Make Your Customers Rave About You.** Deliver service good enough to create loyalty, repeat business, referrals, and unsolicited positive feedback.
- **Tactic 14 — Follow Through.** Reliability is built through accumulation of small completions.
- **Tactic 15 — Become an Active Member of a Professional Organization.** Do more than join: attend seminars, take courses, exchange ideas, and network with peers.
- **Tactic 16 — Always View Your Job as Important.** Treat even low-status work as meaningful and perform it in a way that expands its value.
- **Tactic 17 — Write an Article for Your Company Newsletter.** Contribute useful internal writing tied to projects, expertise, or company progress.
- **Tactic 18 — Take Corporate “Good News” Personally.** Celebrate organizational wins even when you did not personally cause them.
- **Tactic 19 — Consider No Task Beneath You.** Leaders should be willing to perform small or menial tasks when doing so helps the work move.
- **Tactic 20 — Praise Others in Public.** Praise publicly and criticize privately. Make recognition specific and sincere.
- **Tactic 21 — Learn to Work with Less Supervision.** Ask enough questions initially to understand the assignment, then research and solve ordinary issues independently.
- **Tactic 22 — Telephone Courtesy with Your Boss.** Give the boss's call priority, avoid putting the boss on hold unnecessarily, return calls quickly, and maintain professional voicemail/answering messages.
- **Tactic 23 — Get to the Point—Brevity Is a Virtue.** Respect the boss's scarce attention. State the issue, evidence, and request concisely rather than treating access to the boss as an invitation to narrate everything you know.
- **Tactic 24 — Recognize When a Job Is Finished.** Stop polishing when additional effort yields negligible value.
- **Tactic 25 — Never Hold a Grudge.** Do not let resentment become a persistent workplace behavior.
- **Tactic 26 — Go Through the Chain of Command.** Learn the organization's formal hierarchy and normally respect it.
- **Tactic 27 — Live in the Present.** When joining a new employer, stop constantly comparing it with the old one.
- **Tactic 28 — Never Confront Your Boss in the Presence of Others.** Handle disagreement privately when possible. Public confrontation threatens dignity and authority, increasing the chance that the interaction becomes a status contest rather than problem solving.
- **Tactic 29 — Use Flattery Sparingly.** Distinguish sincere praise from exaggerated flattery. Excessive praise lowers the credibility of genuine compliments because it creates suspicion about motive.
- **Tactic 30 — Restaurant Etiquette.** At business meals: order reasonably, do not disappear to socialize elsewhere, avoid complaining, do not choose conspicuously expensive items, thank the payer, minimize phone use, treat service staff respectfully, tip appropriately, attend to tablemates, and protect confidentiality by speaking quietly.
- **Tactic 31 — Give Only Your Best Ideas.** Outside true brainstorming, filter ideas before presenting them.
- **Tactic 32 — Be a Jack-of-All-Trades.** Build adjacent skills and understand multiple areas of the business so you can see the larger system and cover gaps when needed.
- **Tactic 33 — Adopt a Lifetime Self-Improvement Plan.** Continue learning even after success. Read about the industry, attend seminars, exchange ideas, and deliberately search for better methods.
- **Tactic 34 — Learn a Second Language.** Choose a language relevant to customers, markets, geography, or company strategy.
- **Tactic 35 — Be a Take-Charge Person.** Initiate rather than wait passively. Take ownership of problems and create momentum.
- **Tactic 36 — Eagerly Accept Any Offer to Be Compensated on a Performance Basis.** The author argues that strong performers should welcome pay tied to results because it signals confidence and lets output determine earnings.
- **Tactic 37 — Eight Things Your Boss Won't Appreciate.** Avoid visible countdowns to vacation/retirement, provocative office reading that sends an unwanted signal, chain letters, selling side products to the boss, recruiting coworkers into side ventures, abusing casual-dress norms, overly familiar endearments, and excessive personal calls.
- **Tactic 38 — See the Big Picture, but Pay Attention to Details.** Hold the long-term goal while executing the small steps that make it real.
- **Tactic 39 — Share Your Boss's Priorities.** Learn what the boss treats as important—short-term profits, long-term growth, punctuality, formality, detail, customer service, etc.—and align effort accordingly.
- **Tactic 40 — Subscribe to The Wall Street Journal.** Original tactic: read a major business newspaper, especially if the boss does.
- **Tactic 41 — Learn from Your Mistakes.** Expect errors in risk-taking work and treat them as feedback.
- **Tactic 42 — Ask Questions.** Professionals probe before acting. Use questions to clarify expected outcomes, constraints, and assumptions.
- **Tactic 43 — Be Visible.** Produce excellent work first, then ensure it is discoverable.
- **Tactic 44 — Keep Yourself in Good Physical Condition.** The book links fitness to stamina, confidence, appearance, alertness, and lower absenteeism.
- **Tactic 45 — Never Get into an Argument with Your Boss.** Do not turn disagreement into a quarrel or repeated devil's advocacy.
- **Tactic 46 — Don't Try to Impress Your Boss with Constant Conversation.** Being informative is different from occupying the boss's attention.
- **Tactic 47 — Send Your Boss an Occasional News Article.** Share a relevant article with a short personal note when it genuinely fits the boss's interests or business concerns.
- **Tactic 48 — Polish Your Writing Skills.** Weak writing can cap advancement because memos, proposals, and correspondence become evidence of judgment and clarity.
- **Tactic 49 — Be Loyal to Your Boss.** The book recommends not criticizing the boss behind their back, defending them against unfair attacks, and disagreeing privately.
- **Tactic 50 — Practice Common Courtesy with Everyone.** Treat people respectfully regardless of rank. Courtesy to lower-status workers is especially diagnostic because it reveals whether civility is genuine or strategic.
- **Tactic 51 — Realize That the Squeaky Wheel Does Not Always Get the Grease.** Chronic complaining can make a person easier to dismiss rather than harder to ignore.
- **Tactic 52 — Admit Your Mistakes.** Surface errors before someone else does, take responsibility, and correct them while damage is containable.
- **Tactic 53 — Watch Your Body Language.** Respect is communicated nonverbally. Avoid crowding, trapping people physically, slumping, looking around while they speak, restless legs, or fiddling with objects.
- **Tactic 54 — Stay Current.** Keep pace with industry, culture, tools, and new methods.
- **Tactic 55 — Accept the Blame, Even When It's Not Your Fault.** The book sometimes advises quietly taking the heat when the boss uses you as cover, expecting later appreciation.
- **Tactic 56 — Work Overtime.** The book treats voluntary long hours as a signal of ambition, loyalty, and commitment, particularly for inexperienced employees.
- **Tactic 57 — Be a Good Sport and Occasionally Pick Up the Tab.** On nonbusiness meals with the boss, do not assume the senior person must always pay.
- **Tactic 58 — Don't Fake It—Be Sure You Understand the Assignment.** If an assignment is unclear, say so before beginning.
- **Tactic 59 — Put Yourself in Your Boss's Shoes.** Perspective-take deliberately. Ask what you would do if responsible for the entire decision, including employee needs, customer demands, constraints, and profitability.
- **Tactic 60 — Send Your Boss a Complimentary Memo.** Occasionally put sincere praise in writing. A short note gives the boss recognition that can be revisited later.
- **Tactic 61 — If You Can't Say Something Nice About Someone, Don't Say Anything.** Avoid habitual badmouthing and gossip. The book's mechanism is both social and strategic: criticism creates animosity, weakens teamwork, and tells the boss that you may also gossip about confidential matters or about the boss.
- **Tactic 62 — Keep Your Private Life Private.** The book recommends keeping personal problems—and even much personal good news—out of work, both to protect a strong professional image and to avoid distracting others.
- **Tactic 63 — Accept Criticism Graciously.** Assuming a reasonable boss and work-related feedback, do not react defensively.
- **Tactic 64 — Learn to Read Your Boss's Moods and Recognize That Timing Is Essential.** Do not raise a salary request, promotion, pet project, or other demanding topic when the boss is rushing, behind schedule, preparing a major speech, freshly criticized, in conflict, losing key staff, or entering/leaving a vacation.
- **Tactic 65 — Get in the Habit of Saying “We” Instead of “I.”.** Use collective language for shared problems and successes.
- **Tactic 66 — Have a Passion for Your Job.** The book argues that genuine enthusiasm increases energy, productivity, persistence, and social contagion.
- **Tactic 67 — Seek a Mentor Your Boss Respects.** Choose an experienced adviser whose judgment carries weight with the boss.
- **Tactic 68 — Learn to Work Around Your Boss's Idiosyncrasies.** Stop expecting recurring quirks to disappear. Identify predictable triggers and preferences, then adapt where the cost is reasonable.
- **Tactic 69 — Pay Close Attention to Your Company's Unwritten Rules.** Observe conventions that are culturally enforced even if no policy states them: response times, forms of address, customer rituals, dress, meeting norms, or decision customs.
- **Tactic 70 — Seek Increased Responsibility.** Do not wait for advancement while performing only the current minimum.
- **Tactic 71 — If a Joke Is Not Funny and Not Offensive, Laugh Anyway.** The authors recommend at least a warm smile to prevent the awkwardness of an unreceived joke, especially from the boss.
- **Tactic 72 — Make Business Lunches Productive.** Use an agenda, start important discussion early, and avoid consuming the entire meal with small talk or menu decisions.
- **Tactic 73 — Be Sure to Attend Your Boss's Big Speech.** If the event is unusual and important, show up as a supportive face.
- **Tactic 74 — When a VIP Enters the Room, Stand Up to Shake Hands.** The book treats standing as a traditional sign of respect and protocol.
- **Tactic 75 — Take a Stand When Necessary.** Supporting the boss does not mean becoming a yes-person.
- **Tactic 76 — Have a Clear Understanding of Your Company's Values.** Identify both stated and enacted values. If management says one thing but repeatedly does another, behavior is the more reliable evidence.
- **Tactic 77 — Be a Team Player.** Optimize for the shared goal rather than individual credit.
- **Tactic 78 — Be Coachable.** Maintain a desire to learn, give the boss some presumption of competence, ask questions, listen, remain flexible, and avoid reflexive second-guessing.
- **Tactic 79 — Understand That Life Isn't Fair.** Promotions, layoffs, bonuses, and opportunities may be influenced by factors outside merit or control.
- **Tactic 80 — Get a Life (After Work).** Work performance benefits from recovery, play, relationships, and varied experiences.
- **Tactic 81 — Be a Good Backup Person for Your Boss.** Learn enough to cover important functions when the boss is absent, make reasonable decisions independently, and protect continuity.
- **Tactic 82 — Don't Just Complain—Offer Solutions.** When surfacing a problem, bring research, alternatives, or a proposed fix.
- **Tactic 83 — Ask for Periodic Performance Reviews.** Do not wait until year-end to discover that performance is off course.
- **Tactic 84 — Motivate the Motivator.** Bosses also experience setbacks and doubt. Notice when the boss needs encouragement and offer specific, credible support.
- **Tactic 85 — Control Your Anger.** Diagnose the trigger, avoid impulsive reaction, cool down before speaking, identify situations that reliably provoke you, monitor bodily signs of arousal, distinguish the current trigger from displaced emotion, check fatigue/hunger/mood, and return with a constructive solution.
- **Tactic 86 — Never Ask Your Boss Tough Questions During an Important Presentation Unless You Know He Can Answer Them.** Do not use public Q&A to display your cleverness at the boss's expense.
- **Tactic 87 — Seek Simplicity.** Prefer clear language and uncomplicated solutions over jargon, acronyms, or complexity used to display expertise.
- **Tactic 88 — Make a Generous Donation During Your Company's United Way Fund-Raising Drive.** The author describes charitable participation and leadership of company campaigns as signals of citizenship and team spirit, and explicitly admits judging employees partly by donation levels.
- **Tactic 89 — If You Share a Secretary, Don't Monopolize Her Time.** Coordinate shared administrative support fairly, respect others' priorities, do not assign personal errands, and self-serve when the assistant is overloaded.
- **Tactic 90 — Improve Your Listening Skills.** Make eye contact, concentrate, be willing to learn, listen for information and rapport, notice phrases that reveal wants/needs/problems, do not interrupt, pause before replying, and signal attention.
- **Tactic 91 — Don't Spill Your Guts to the Company Therapist.** The book distinguishes an employer-paid evaluator from a private therapist.
- **Tactic 92 — Manage Your Time Effectively.** Build buffer for unexpected work; focus on one task at a time; define precise deadlines; use specific appointment times; handle documents once when possible; limit phone duration; keep and prioritize a written to-do list.
- **Tactic 93 — When You Have a Great Idea, Tell Your Boss Before Anyone Else.** Give the boss first access rather than bypassing the hierarchy.
- **Tactic 94 — Increase Your Walking Pace.** The author claims that brisk walking creates an impression of purpose, urgency, and energy.
- **Tactic 95 — Learn the Names of Customers, Coworkers, Members of the Boss's Family, and the Boss's Friends.** Remembering names communicates attention and makes important people feel recognized.
- **Tactic 96 — Strive to Understand How the Whole Company Works.** Avoid departmental tunnel vision. Broader knowledge increases mobility, reveals how your role connects to the system, and enables “cross-pollination” of ideas between functions.
- **Tactic 97 — Get into the Habit of Speaking Up.** Expertise that stays silent cannot help the organization.
- **Tactic 98 — Always Express Gratitude When Your Boss Does Something on Your Behalf.** Thank the boss for both major and small acts: raises, promotions, invitations, opportunities, or requests for input.
- **Tactic 99 — Express Congratulations or Sympathy When Appropriate.** Acknowledge others' achievements, losses, and disappointments. The book treats sincere congratulations as evidence of self-confidence and team orientation because another person's success does not threaten you.
- **Tactic 100 — Read the Publications Your Boss Reads.** Track the trade journals, industry publications, and information sources that shape the boss's worldview.
- **Tactic 101 — Return Phone Calls Promptly.** Responsiveness communicates that other people's time and concerns matter.
- **Tactic 102 — Be on Time.** Punctuality is treated as respect for other people's time and as a signal of organization/self-control.
- **Tactic 103 — Be Sensitive to Others' Problems.** Do not turn the office into therapy, but do not become emotionally indifferent either.
- **Tactic 104 — Provide Criticism Privately.** Public criticism humiliates and can damage group morale.
- **Tactic 105 — Be Modest.** Let results and other people do much of the talking.
- **Tactic 106 — Allow Others to Save Face.** When people already know they made a mistake, avoid unnecessary humiliation.
- **Tactic 107 — Keep Business in the Office.** Protect confidential company information in restaurants, public spaces, and home conversations.
- **Tactic 108 — Keep Small Talk to a Minimum.** Distinguish relationship-building from idle chatter that consumes work time.
- **Tactic 109 — Never Burn Bridges.** Maintain relationships even when someone can no longer help you immediately.
- **Tactic 110 — Be Sensitive to Office Politics.** Learn who actually has influence, what precedents govern promotions/bonuses/severance, which committees matter, and where formal titles misrepresent real power.
- **Tactic 111 — Do Not Drink.** Avoid alcohol during the workday. The book argues that even mild impairment can hurt alertness and judgment and that there is little career upside.
- **Tactic 112 — Learn to Compromise.** Most workplace decisions require give and take. Protect core principles, but do not treat every preference as non-negotiable.
- **Tactic 113 — Don't Try to Be Popular.** Management sometimes requires saying no, rejecting ideas, disciplining performance, or making decisions some people dislike.
- **Tactic 114 — Always Use Appropriate, Professional Language.** Avoid profanity, offensive remarks, and unexplained jargon that outsiders cannot understand.
- **Tactic 115 — Show Loyalty by Keeping Confidences.** If entrusted with sensitive information, protect it. Confidentiality failures destroy trust and may create legal/business risk.
- **Tactic 116 — With a New Boss, Find Your Parameters.** Early in the relationship, clarify decision rights and boundaries: which decisions can you make alone, what requires approval, how much initiative is welcome, and what reporting is expected?
- **Tactic 117 — Be Open to New Ideas.** Resist complacency and listen seriously to alternatives. Your idea and another person's idea may combine into something better.
- **Tactic 118 — Develop Your People Skills.** Technical skill is insufficient for management. Continue improving listening, rapport, empathy, influence, conflict handling, and interpersonal communication.
- **Tactic 119 — Take the Initiative in Solving an Irate Customer's Problem.** Treat customer recovery as everyone's job, even when the complaint falls outside your formal remit.
- **Tactic 120 — Help Your Boss Stay on Schedule.** When the boss is behind on something important, step in where useful and organize your work around the critical need.
- **Tactic 121 — Don't Rush Through a Job in an Effort to Impress Your Boss.** Efficiency is valuable only if quality survives. Do not cut planning, research, or proofreading merely to finish fast.
- **Tactic 122 — Remember Special Occasions.** Remember birthdays, anniversaries, awards, illnesses, and other meaningful occasions with cards, notes, lunch, flowers, or modest gifts.
- **Tactic 123 — Tell Your Boss What a Great Day You're Having.** Share positive energy rather than only bringing problems.
- **Tactic 124 — Maintain a Positive Attitude.** The book links optimism with energy, enjoyment, inspiration, direction, and health.
- **Tactic 125 — Never Make an Idle Threat to Your Boss.** Do not repeatedly threaten to quit to force a raise, promotion, or concession unless you are prepared to leave.
- **Tactic 126 — Be Reserved at Company Parties.** A company social event is still connected to work.
- **Tactic 127 — Stay Focused.** Give the highest-priority task concentrated attention instead of fragmenting effort across everything at once.
- **Tactic 128 — Be a Good Corporate Citizen.** Participate in community or charitable activity, preferably deeply in one meaningful cause rather than superficially in many.
- **Tactic 129 — Plan Time Off When It's Good for Your Boss, and Let Her Know in Advance.** Coordinate planned absence early so coverage can be arranged.
- **Tactic 130 — Train Someone to Replace You in Case You Get Promoted.** Succession readiness prevents your current indispensability from becoming a promotion barrier.
- **Tactic 131 — Don't Try to Be the Office Peacock.** Dress professionally without making conspicuous fashion display the center of attention.
- **Tactic 132 — Befriend Your Boss's Secretary.** The book highlights the influence of the boss's administrative gatekeeper: access, scheduling, context, and informal impressions can all matter.
- **Tactic 133 — Develop a Healthy Belief in the Impossible.** Do not reject difficult goals merely because precedent says they cannot be achieved.
- **Tactic 134 — Update Your Spouse or Companion on Pertinent Business Information When Attending a Business Event.** Give a companion enough nonconfidential context to participate comfortably at business functions.
- **Tactic 135 — Know Your Worth.** Before negotiating compensation, research market rates and prepare evidence of accomplishments plus future goals.
- **Tactic 136 — Remember, Your Boss Is Lonely at the Top.** Senior leaders may be informationally isolated because subordinates filter bad news or say what they think the boss wants to hear.
- **Tactic 137 — If the Company Has Not Yet Developed a Mission Statement, Volunteer to Help Write One.** Study other mission statements, interview people at multiple levels about real values, synthesize recurring themes, and keep the result concise.
- **Tactic 138 — Don't Become Married to a Particular Project or Idea.** Separate identity from the method you proposed. Listen to alternatives, combine useful elements, and adapt when circumstances change.
- **Tactic 139 — See the Forest Through the Trees.** Details matter, but not all details deserve equal attention.
- **Tactic 140 — Don't Steal the Limelight from Your Boss.** When recognition legitimately belongs to the boss, do not turn the moment into your own performance.
- **Tactic 141 — Maintain a Sense of Humor.** Humor can create perspective, reduce tension, and help people connect.
- **Tactic 142 — Never Undersell Yourself.** Make sure meaningful accomplishments are known. Accept deserved praise and act as a measured advocate for your own value.
- **Tactic 143 — Never Oversell Yourself.** The counterweight to Tip 142: do not exaggerate capability or boast so much that expectations outrun performance.
- **Tactic 144 — Go the Extra Mile.** Build a reputation for doing more than the bare job description when customers or the team need it.
- **Tactic 145 — Don't Take Yourself Too Seriously.** Take the work seriously without becoming self-important. Promotions should not destroy approachability.
- **Tactic 146 — Ask for Help.** Seek expertise wherever it resides—boss, peer, subordinate, or frontline worker.
- **Tactic 147 — Don't Rely on Intuition Unless You're Absolutely Positive You're Right.** The book treats intuition as accumulated experience expressed through a “gut” signal, but advises using it proportionally to its historical accuracy and the stakes involved.
- **Tactic 148 — Delegate.** Assign lower-value or specialized work to capable people so managerial attention stays on higher-priority tasks.
- **Tactic 149 — Don't Shoot the Messenger—Reward Him for Bad News.** Explicitly welcome unfavorable information, ask questions, and verify facts.
- **Tactic 150 — Don't Resist Change—Embrace It.** Recognize that refusing change is itself a choice.
- **Tactic 151 — Develop Your Networking Skills.** Build relationships inside and outside the company, including deliberate contacts who can broaden your career.
- **Tactic 152 — Never Tolerate a Lack of Integrity.** Management behavior sets moral tone. False claims, unfair treatment, shoddy value, and self-serving executive behavior corrode pride and culture.
- **Tactic 153 — Never Tolerate Disloyalty.** The authors recommend visibly defending the employer against hostile remarks and interpret silence as possible disloyalty.
- **Tactic 154 — Unclutter Your Cluttered Desk.** Organize the workspace to reduce search time and avoid projecting disorder.
- **Tactic 155 — Be Likeable.** Warmth, friendliness, and respect make others more willing to cooperate.
- **Tactic 156 — Don't Be a Creature of Habit.** Experiment with ways to improve routine tasks rather than performing them mindlessly.
- **Tactic 157 — Surround Yourself with People Who Reflect the Values of Your Company.** When hiring or staffing teams, assess value fit through substantive interviews rather than resumes alone.
- **Tactic 158 — Know When to Say No.** Avoid the “slow no” that keeps others waiting and wastes time.
- **Tactic 159 — Treat Your Boss Like You Treat Your Best Customer.** Continuously understand needs, anticipate problems, make the boss's work easier, and help the boss succeed.
- **Tactic 160 — Schedule Appointments with Your Boss.** For important matters, request dedicated time rather than dropping in while the boss is distracted.
- **Tactic 161 — Take Ownership.** Act as if you have a stake in the organization's success even without equity.
- **Tactic 162 — Inform Your Boss About Your Career Plan.** Write an intermediate (roughly 3–5 year) and long-range (10–20 year) career plan, discuss it with the boss, seek advice, and periodically review progress.
- **Tactic 163 — Don't Be a Prima Donna.** High performance does not entitle someone to contemptuous behavior, endless exceptions, or policy bending.
- **Tactic 164 — Be Generous with the Gifts You Give Your Boss.** The book recommends reciprocating gifts and choosing thoughtful, hobby-related, useful, not-too-personal items.
- **Tactic 165 — Never Underestimate the Power of the Grapevine.** Rumors can be distorted, but informal observations sometimes detect operational trouble before leaders do.
- **Tactic 166 — Block Out Irrelevant Remarks and Concentrate on What's Important.** Extract the essential issue from rambling conversation and guide meetings back to objectives.
- **Tactic 167 — Avoid Awkward Food When Dining Out.** At high-stakes business meals, choose food that is easy to eat neatly and does not make you conspicuous.
- **Tactic 168 — Don't Allow Failure to Defeat You.** Risk and failure are linked. Learn, regroup, and separate failed outcomes from personal identity.
- **Tactic 169 — Go for the Bottom Line.** Understand how your work contributes to financial sustainability.
